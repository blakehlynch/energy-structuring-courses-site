Natural gas and LNG: hubs, contracts and the LNG chain

Gas links oil and power. A structurer meets it as the fuel in a toll, the inventory in a storage deal, the basis in a hedge and, increasingly, as LNG cargoes that can sail to whichever market pays most. This course covers the gas market from the molecule to the cargo. It starts with units and the LNG chain, moves through traded hubs and pipeline capacity, then works through the economics of liquefaction, the long-term contracts that finance it, the ships that move it and the terminals that receive it. It ends by valuing the flexibility that has turned LNG from a floating pipeline into a traded commodity. The main text is Tusiani and Shearer's guide to the LNG business. Mokhatab and co-authors supply the engineering facts, Edwards the North American gas market, and Yergin and Stopford the history and geopolitics.

By the end you will be able to

  • Convert between tonnes, bcm, MMBtu and therms, and build a delivered cost stack and netback.
  • Read hub and basis prices, and value firm pipeline capacity as a spread option.
  • Compute a cost-of-service liquefaction fee and the US Henry Hub-linked FOB price.
  • Price LNG with oil slopes, S-curves and base-period formulas, and work a take-or-pay shortfall.
  • Cost a round voyage per delivered MMBtu and size a fleet.
  • Choose a destination by netback and value a flexible US cargo as an option.

How this course draws on the books

  • The LNG business, contracts and costs: Tusiani & Shearer ch.2–4, 6–7, 10–15, 18. Tusiani & Shearer
  • LNG properties, units and the supply chain: Mokhatab et al. ch.1. Mokhatab et al.
  • North American gas, hubs, transport and storage: Edwards ch.2.1, 5.1–5.3. Edwards
  • History and geopolitics: Yergin ch.4, 14 and Stopford §12.4. Yergin Stopford

A note on dates

Tusiani and Shearer wrote in early 2015, just as oil prices fell and before the first US export cargo sailed in February 2016. Their prices, costs and project lists are from that moment. Since then US exports have grown into the largest supply source, Europe has lost most Russian pipeline gas, and the TTF and JKM prices spiked to record levels in 2022. Outside the texts The commercial logic in the books still holds, but every price and project figure should be read as of 2015. Each module's Dated box lists what to check. Mokhatab et al. (2014) and Edwards (2017) are current on engineering and market mechanics.

More depth in this course

Going-deeper sections cover why the MMBtu content of a tonne varies, firm capacity as an insurance product, why second trains are cheap, the S-curve as a collar, the hidden cost of boil-off, and why destination flexibility is a best-of option. Tools include an LNG chain calculator, a pipeline capacity valuer, a liquefaction tariff model, an SPA price formula explorer, a voyage cost calculator and a flexible cargo valuer.

Modules

  1. Gas, units and the LNG chain ≈ 55 min ·
    What gas is, how it is measured, the four links of the LNG chain, who owns each link, and where the cost and the netback sit.
  2. Gas markets, hubs and pipeline capacity ≈ 60 min ·
    Liberalisation and third-party access, Henry Hub, NBP and TTF, oil-indexed Asia, basis, pipeline tariffs, firm capacity as a spread option, and storage.
  3. LNG project economics and business models ≈ 65 min ·
    Capital and operating costs, availability and liquids, fiscal terms, integrated, merchant and tolling structures, cost-of-service fees and the US Henry Hub-linked model.
  4. LNG sale and purchase agreements ≈ 65 min ·
    Quantities, take-or-pay and make-up, oil-indexed slopes and S-curves, European base-period formulas, Henry Hub pricing, reopeners, delivery terms, force majeure and destination clauses.
  5. LNG shipping and logistics ≈ 55 min ·
    Ships, boil-off and heel, charter types and hire, voyage economics, fleet sizing, scheduling and who controls shipping.
  6. Terminals, destination flexibility and LNG arbitrage ≈ 65 min ·
    Regasification terminals and FSRUs, terminal use agreements and third-party access, netbacks, the US cargo as a spread option, and the value of choosing a destination.
