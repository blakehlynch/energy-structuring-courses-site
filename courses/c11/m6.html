Terminals, destination flexibility and LNG arbitrage

Core≈ 65 min8 quiz questions6 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Describe onshore terminals and FSRUs and what drives their cost.
  • Compare regulated third-party access with terminal use agreements.
  • Compute destination netbacks and choose between Europe and Asia.
  • Value a US capacity holder's cargo as an option and estimate the value of destination choice.

Prerequisites

  • Modules 1–5; Course 7 Module 2 (spread options); Course 8 Module 1 (real options).

Import terminals

A receiving terminal has marine berths and unloading arms, insulated storage tanks, pumps and vaporisers that warm the LNG back to gas, and metering into the pipeline grid. Some can blend LPG to raise the heating value or inject nitrogen to lower it. Tusiani & Shearer ch.11 (pp.369, 376) Sizes vary with the market served. Sabine Pass, built as an import terminal in a supply region with big pipelines, had 0.8 million m³ of storage and 4.3 Bcf/d of sendout. Japanese and Korean terminals have similar throughput but up to 2.9 million m³ of storage, because LNG is their whole supply and the terminal is their storage. Tusiani & Shearer ch.11 (p.370)

Storage tanks, marine works and safety systems make up more than 60% of a terminal's cost, and tanks set the construction schedule: two to three years above ground, up to five in the ground. Terminals can be expanded cheaply by adding vaporisers until a new tank is needed. Tusiani & Shearer ch.11 (pp.371–373, 375) A conventional onshore terminal with 200,000–300,000 m³ of storage costs at least $750 million, excluding land and pipelines. Tusiani & Shearer ch.2 (p.62) Many terminals anchor a gas-fired power plant, which often runs mid-merit at 50–70% load. Tusiani & Shearer ch.11 (p.374)

Floating storage and regasification units (FSRUs) take two to three years to build, about two-thirds the time of an onshore terminal, and can be moved or traded as carriers in off-peak seasons. A newbuilt FSRU costs upward of $250 million and can import 3–4 MMt/y. Tusiani & Shearer ch.11 (pp.394–396) FSRUs suit small and new markets and fast-track needs. The fleet was expected to grow from 16 units at the end of 2014 to about 25 by 2020. Tusiani & Shearer ch.11 (p.393) As a rule of thumb, regasification adds about $0.60/MMBtu to delivered cost, more in Asia with its large storage and less in Europe. An FSRU charter can cost about $175,000 a day. Tusiani & Shearer ch.2 (p.62) Cheniere's third-party rate in the Gulf of Mexico was about $0.35/MMBtu plus vaporiser fuel of about 1.75% of throughput. Tusiani & Shearer ch.7 (pp.284–285)

Yergin shows why terminals matter beyond their cost. Lithuania, fully dependent on Russian gas, opened an FSRU named Independence in 2014. Its energy minister said the terminal won a price cut from Gazprom simply by existing. Europe went on to build more than 30 receiving terminals. Yergin ch.14

Terminal access: regulated TPA or a TUA

Early terminals were built by utilities for their own supply and recovered their cost through the rate base. Liberalisation brought two access models. Tusiani & Shearer ch.18 (pp.531–532)

  • Regulated third-party access. The operator publishes a tariff approved by the regulator, much like a pipeline tariff, offering throughput rights, scheduling and vaporiser fuel retention on non-discriminatory terms. Regulated access proved poor at getting new capacity built. Tusiani & Shearer ch.18 (pp.532, 537) In 2014, 13 of Europe's 18 terminals were under regulated access. Tusiani & Shearer ch.18 (p.533)
  • Proprietary access under a terminal use agreement (TUA). The capacity holder negotiates terms directly. The fee is usually fixed for a 20-year primary term, paid in advance, and payable even during force majeure, much like charter hire, which is what lets the owner raise highly leveraged project finance. Tusiani & Shearer ch.18 (pp.540–541) The US allowed this from 2002, starting with the Hackberry decision for what became Cameron LNG. Tusiani & Shearer ch.18 (p.539)

Exempt European terminals must still run "use it or lose it" (UIOLI) rules so that unused slots reach secondary shippers. Tusiani & Shearer ch.18 (p.533) Shared terminals are complicated: cargoes arrive in lumps, sendout is steady, and LNG from different customers is commingled, so inventories are effectively lent and borrowed between them. Customers often sign an operational coordination agreement (OCA) among themselves to manage this. Tusiani & Shearer ch.18 (pp.538, 541) Terminals now sell reloading, truck loading, storage, bunkering and transshipment. Zeebrugge reloaded 25 cargoes in 2012, a sign of arbitrage at work. Tusiani & Shearer ch.18 (p.534)

Netbacks and the destination decision

A cargo owner with destination freedom compares the netback each market pays at the loading port: the delivered price minus shipping and, if selling into a gas hub, regasification. A higher price in one market does not mean it pays more after transport; Tusiani and Shearer warn that voyage length and short-term shipping costs can reverse the ranking. Tusiani & Shearer ch.4 (p.167) They also note that, when NBP stood $4.50 above Henry Hub in 2014, the margin was still short of the $5.25–5.50 needed to cover liquefaction, shipping, regasification and a trading margin. Tusiani & Shearer ch.2 (pp.63–64)

Worked example 1 · Europe or Asia for a US cargoChecked by code

A US FOB cargo can sell into TTF at $9.00, paying a regas fee of $0.35 plus 1.75% fuel, or deliver into Asia at $10.20 with no regas cost to the seller. Shipping costs come from Module 5: $0.744 to Europe and $1.506 to Japan. Henry Hub is $3.00, so the feed gas costs 1.15 × 3.00. Tusiani & Shearer ch.7 (pp.284–285) Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (p.462)

regas into Europe = 0.35 + 1.75% × 9.00 = $0.5075 Europe netback = 9.00 − 0.5075 − 0.744 = $7.748 Asia netback = 10.20 − 1.506 = $8.694 → Asia by $0.946 variable cost of the cargo = 1.15 × 3.00 = $3.45 margin over variable cost (Asia) = $5.244 margin after the $3.10 liquefaction fee (Module 3) = $2.142

The $1.20 headline premium in Asia shrinks to under a dollar after shipping and regas, but it still wins. For a US capacity holder the decision to lift depends only on the margin over variable cost, because the fee is paid whether or not the cargo sails. Whether the contract was a good deal depends on the margin after the fee. Synthesis

The US cargo as a spread option

A US capacity holder pays the fixed fee every month. For each cargo it can then buy feed gas at 115% of Henry Hub, liquefy it, ship it and sell it, or decline the cargo and leave the capacity idle. Disintegrated US LNG is destination-free, and the holder buys its own feed gas and arranges its own ships. Tusiani & Shearer ch.4 (pp.161, 167) Standard US tolling terms also let the holder decline a cargo and simply pay the fee. Outside the texts Each cargo is therefore a call on the destination price minus 1.15 × Henry Hub, with a strike equal to shipping and regas costs, and the tolling fee is the premium. Synthesis

A Europe-bound cargo loads in six months. TTF volatility is 50%, Henry Hub 40%, and their correlation 0.30. The discount rate is 4%. The strike combines the $0.35 regas fee and $0.744 shipping, and fuel scales TTF by 0.9825. The fee is $3.10.

TTF $8.00: intrinsic $3.250, option value $3.304, net of fee $0.203 TTF $6.00: intrinsic $1.324, option value $1.564 (time value $0.240), net of fee $−1.538 TTF $4.50: intrinsic $0.000, option value $0.559, net of fee $−2.542

At $8 TTF the cargo is deep in the money and worth almost exactly its intrinsic margin. At $6 it will very likely be lifted, but its value is $1.54 short of the fee: the holder still sails, losing less than it would by cancelling. At $4.50 the cargo is out of the money, though still worth $0.56 of option value, and the fee is a sunk cost. This is the logic behind mass cargo cancellations when the spread collapses. The value of choosing where to sell

A flexible cargo is worth more than a single-destination option because it can go to whichever market pays more at loading. That is a best-of option: the payoff is the larger of two margins, or zero. Its value over the better single destination depends on how independently the two markets move. Synthesis Tusiani and Shearer describe exactly this arbitrage, with Atlantic cargoes chasing Asian winter premiums until the oil price fall of 2014 closed the gap. Tusiani & Shearer ch.4 (p.165)

Add a JKM forward of $9.00 with 55% volatility, a TTF–JKM correlation of 0.80 and a 0.30 correlation of both with Henry Hub. Values are per MMBtu, from 40,000 correlated simulations of all three prices at loading.

Europe-only option = $3.309 Asia-only option = $4.038 best of Europe, Asia or cancel = $4.481 (uplift over Asia-only $0.444) probability the cargo is lifted = 96.1%, going to Asia in 61.8% of those cases

Destination choice adds about $0.44 per MMBtu, about $1.7 million a cargo on 3.9 million MMBtu delivered. Even with a 0.80 correlation, the best market changes often enough to be worth paying for ships and terminal access. The uplift grows as the two markets decouple. Synthesis

ALM analogy

A US tolling contract is a long-dated fixed liability (the fee) backing a strip of monthly spread options (the cargoes), and destination choice is an exchange option between two markets. The holder's surplus is option value minus the present value of fees. Like a book of guaranteed annuities backed by equity options, it looks fine while spreads are wide and loses heavily when they compress. Synthesis

Dated: terminals, FSRUs and spreads

Since 2022 Europe has added many FSRUs, notably in Germany, to replace Russian pipeline gas, and TTF–JKM spreads have mostly been narrow because the two markets now compete for the same cargoes. US capacity has grown several-fold. Check the GIIGNL annual report for terminals and FSRUs, and broker data for TTF–JKM–Henry Hub spreads. Outside the texts

Going deeper: why destination flexibility is a best-of option

The best-of payoff max(A, B, 0) can be split into the better single option plus an exchange option on the difference between the two markets. The exchange option's value depends on the volatility of the TTF–JKM spread, which falls as their correlation rises. So the uplift from flexibility is small when markets move together and large when they decouple. That is when regional supply shocks, weather or nuclear outages hit one market but not the other. Synthesis Contract terms can capture or give away that value. Destination restrictions remove it. Diversion clauses that split incremental profit share it between seller and buyer. Agreeing that split is hard when pricing is opaque or the parties hold different hedges. Tusiani & Shearer ch.4 (pp.167–168) Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (pp.474–475) A structurer valuing a flexible SPA should model the best-of payoff explicitly rather than assume the best destination of today's forwards, which captures only intrinsic value. Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • Terminal cost is driven by storage tanks and marine works; FSRUs are faster, smaller and mobile.
  • Regulated access suits existing capacity; TUAs with fixed, hell-or-high-water fees get new capacity financed.
  • Compare destinations by netback, after shipping and regas, not by headline price.
  • A US capacity holder's cargo is a spread option and the fee is its premium. Destination choice adds a best-of uplift that grows as markets decouple.
