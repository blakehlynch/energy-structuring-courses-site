LNG project economics and business models

Learning objectives

  • Explain the drivers of liquefaction capital cost and why unit costs rose.
  • Compute a cost-of-service liquefaction fee from capex, return, life, opex and availability.
  • Compare integrated, merchant, tolling and US capacity-tolling structures by who carries price and volume risk.
  • Build a US FOB price and delivered prices into Asia and Europe.

Prerequisites

  • Modules 1–2; Course 9 Modules 1–2 (project finance and offtake contracts).

Why LNG projects are built on contracts

A crude oil field can be developed on the assumption that a buyer will be found once oil flows, because oil is fungible and cheap to move. LNG needs a liquefaction plant, specialised ships and a receiving terminal before a single molecule can be sold. Building the plant without buyers, ships and import capacity lined up would be an enormous gamble, so the whole chain has traditionally been contracted before the final investment decision (FID). Tusiani & Shearer ch.7 (pp.263–265) For forty years almost every project followed this integrated model, selling to creditworthy utilities whose costs were passed through regulated tariffs. Lenders insisted on signed buyer commitments, certified reserves and dedicated shipping before they would lend. Tusiani & Shearer ch.4 (pp.145–146)

Capital costs

Liquefaction is normally the largest investment in the chain. Unit costs fell for three decades, to below $200 per tonne a year of capacity by about 2003. They then rose sharply to $1,000–2,000 per t/y as steel, nickel and cement prices climbed, experienced EPC contractors became scarce, and projects moved to remote and hostile sites. Tusiani & Shearer ch.7 (pp.267–268) Australian projects, with high labour costs and strong unions, reached $3,000–4,000 per t/y including upstream; the three-train, 15.6 MMt/y Gorgon project was reported at $54 billion. Early US projects, built at existing import terminals, came in around $650–750 per t/y. Tusiani & Shearer ch.2 (pp.59–60)

Several features of the plant drive the number:

  • Front-end treatment to remove CO2, mercury, H2S and water, and to strip heavier liquids, can be up to 20% of liquefaction cost. Tusiani & Shearer ch.7 (p.269)
  • Storage and marine works. Too little storage relative to output risks hitting "tank tops" if a ship is late, which forces a shutdown. Breakwaters and dredging can add large sums. Tusiani & Shearer ch.7 (pp.268–269)
  • Construction approach. Modular construction suits high-labour-cost locations but adds steel and coordination risk. Tusiani & Shearer ch.7 (p.270)
  • Scale and expansions. If the infrastructure for the first train can handle a second, output can double for about 50% more capital. Trinidad's trains 2 and 3 together cost about $1.1 billion against $950 million for train 1. Tusiani & Shearer ch.7 (p.270)

Operating costs, availability and liquids

Annual opex for the upstream and liquefaction is typically 2–5% of total capital cost, excluding fuel. Economics are driven by availability, the share of days the plant actually produces, which targets about 93% once operations are stable. Upstream shortfalls, turbine maintenance, unplanned trips and tank tops all cut it. Tusiani & Shearer ch.7 (pp.271–272) Condensate and LPG sold alongside LNG can be worth $0.50/MMBtu or more of credit to the project, and in some fields they cover most of the upstream investment. Tusiani & Shearer ch.7 (p.272) Tusiani & Shearer ch.2 (pp.44–45) The variable cost of the chain is close to zero, because the main variable cost, fuel gas, has little value left in the ground. Owners therefore push volume even when prices are low. Tusiani & Shearer ch.2 (p.53)

Government terms

Upstream gas is taxed like oil, through either production sharing contracts or licences with royalties and taxes. Royalties on LNG projects run about 5–20% of wellhead revenue and are paid before any costs are deducted, which makes them heavy for a capital-intensive project. Depreciation for cost recovery is usually straight-line at 10–25% a year, with some regimes front-loaded and some adding uplifts of 105–120%. Corporate tax on LNG projects ranges from about 30% to 50% and averages about 37%. Some countries use resource-rent taxes that bite only above a threshold return. Tusiani & Shearer ch.7 (pp.274–277) Liquefaction itself is usually taxed like manufacturing, which is lower than upstream. Where the plant sits in the value chain therefore changes the total tax bill. Tusiani & Shearer ch.7 (pp.275, 278)

Business structures and the transfer price

The main difference between structures is where the margin is booked, and so who carries price and volume risk. Tusiani & Shearer ch.7 (p.279)

  • Integrated joint venture. The same partners own upstream and liquefaction, there is no transfer price, and all risks are shared. Norway's Snøhvit is an example. Tusiani & Shearer ch.7 (p.279)
  • Tolling plant. Liquefaction earns a fixed return, usually 8–12% on its capital, taken "off the top". Feed gas is priced as a netback: the FOB price minus the tolling fee. The rent goes upstream, which suits plants fed by several fields with different owners. Tusiani & Shearer ch.7 (pp.279–280)
  • Formula-priced feed gas. The upstream is paid a set share of the LNG price, usually under 25%, and shares little of the upside. Tusiani & Shearer ch.7 (pp.281–282)
  • US capacity tolling. The US model reverses the netback: the FOB price is built up from the Henry Hub feed-gas price plus a fixed liquefaction fee. The plant owner takes no gas or LNG price risk, and the capacity holders take both. Tusiani & Shearer ch.7 (p.280) Tusiani & Shearer ch.4 (p.159) Tusiani and Shearer call it more an infrastructure play than a commodity one.

Yergin tells how the first US export project came about. Cheniere had borrowed heavily to build Sabine Pass as an import terminal. In 2009, as shale gas flooded the US market, Chesapeake and Shell each asked whether it could liquefy instead. Cheniere's board approved a plan of at least $8 billion for the first phase in 2010, and the first cargo sailed to Brazil in February 2016. Yergin ch.4

The cost-of-service fee

A tolling plant has to charge enough to recover its capital at the agreed return over its life, plus opex, on the energy it actually produces. A level annual capital charge uses the capital recovery factor:

\[ \text{fee} = \frac{\text{capex}\times \text{CRF}(r,n) + \text{opex}}{\text{capacity}\times\text{availability}\times \text{MMBtu per tonne}}, \qquad \text{CRF}(r,n) = \frac{r}{1-(1+r)^{-n}} \]
Worked example 1 · a liquefaction feeChecked by code

A 4.5 MMt/y train costs $1,000 per t/y. The toll is set to earn 10% on capital over 20 years, the middle of Tusiani and Shearer's 8–12% range. Opex is 3% of capex a year and availability is 93%. Energy is 51.12 MMBtu per tonne, as in Module 1. Tusiani & Shearer ch.7 (pp.271, 279) Synthesis

capex = 1,000 × 4.5m = $4.50bn CRF(10%, 20 years) = 0.11746 annual capital charge = 4.5bn × 0.11746 = $528.6m annual opex = 3% × 4.5bn = $135.0m energy produced = 4.5m × 0.93 × 51.12 = 213.9m MMBtu fee = (528.6 + 135.0) / 213.9 = $3.10/MMBtu (capital $2.47)

About four-fifths of the fee is the return of and on capital, so the fee is almost fixed regardless of throughput. That is why tolling payments are often hell-or-high-water: payable whatever happens upstream or downstream. Tusiani & Shearer ch.2 (p.56) The result sits inside Tusiani and Shearer's $2.50–3.50 range for US liquefaction fees. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (p.463)

Worked example 2 · why unit cost and return dominateChecked by code
fee at $2,000/t (Australian-style cost) = $6.20/MMBtu fee at $700/t (US brownfield) = $2.17/MMBtu fee at $1,000/t with an 8% return = $2.77/MMBtu fee at $1,000/t with a 12% return = $3.45/MMBtu

Doubling unit capex doubles the capital part of the fee, which is how Australian projects came to need $15–16 delivered prices while US projects could compete at $10.50–11.50. Tusiani & Shearer ch.2 (p.63) Synthesis Four points of return move the fee by about $0.67, which is why the credit quality of offtakers, and so the cost of debt, matters so much. Lenders can support higher leverage against predictable tolling fees than against an integrated project's margin. Tusiani & Shearer ch.7 (p.280)

The US Henry Hub-linked price

Tusiani and Shearer give the typical US FOB formula as 115% of Henry Hub plus a liquefaction fee of $2.50–3.50. The 15% covers pipeline transport, basis and fuel to the plant. They then build a delivered price for Asia and test the arbitrage against 2013 prices. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (pp.462–463)

\[ P_{\text{FOB}} = 1.15 \times P_{\text{HH}} + \text{fee} \]
Worked example 3 · US LNG into Japan and the UK, 2013Checked by code

Henry Hub averaged $3.71 in 2013. Use a $3.50 fee, a trading margin of $0.75 and shipping of $2.50 to Japan. Tusiani and Shearer put shipping to Europe at roughly half the Asian cost. Japan's average import price was $16.17 and NBP averaged $10.13. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (p.463)

FOB = 1.15 × 3.71 + 3.50 = $7.77 delivered Japan = 7.77 + 0.75 + 2.50 = $11.02 → headroom against $16.17: $5.15 delivered UK = 7.77 + 0.75 + 1.25 = $9.77 → headroom against NBP $10.13: $0.36

This was the trade that drew Asian utilities and portfolio players into US capacity contracts. Selling to Japan cleared about $5 of headroom, while selling to the UK cleared under a dollar. By early 2015, with oil near $50 and oil-linked Asian prices falling with it, both margins had largely disappeared. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (p.463) Tusiani & Shearer ch.4 (p.154)

Worked example 4 · the tolling netbackChecked by code

In a tolling structure the upstream receives the FOB price minus the fee. With the $3.10 fee from example 1 and an $8.00 FOB price:

feed-gas netback = 8.00 − 3.10 = $4.90/MMBtu

The plant's revenue is fixed and the upstream bears all the price risk. In the US model the arrow runs the other way: the capacity holder buys at Henry Hub, pays the fee whatever happens, and keeps whatever the cargo earns above that. Tusiani & Shearer ch.7 (p.280) Synthesis

ALM analogy

A tolling fee is a fixed-rate annuity priced off a cost of capital, and the capacity holder is long a floating-rate spread asset (the cargo margin) funded by that fixed liability. When the spread compresses, as it did in 2015, the capacity holder has the classic negative carry of a mismatched book. Synthesis

Going deeper: why second trains are cheap and first trains are risky

Most of a greenfield project's non-process capital, such as jetties, storage, breakwaters, utilities and housing, is sunk with the first train. A second train that reuses it adds output for roughly half the cost, and later expansions can be cheaper still. Oman's third train was estimated at under $750 million against $2 billion for the first two. Tusiani & Shearer ch.7 (p.270) That is a real option: the first train buys the right to expand cheaply if demand appears. Course 8's expansion option applies directly, and it explains why developers often plan two trains from the start and why sponsors want rights over expansion volumes. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (p.455) Synthesis The flip side is construction risk. With projects too large for contractors to take lump-sum risk, cost overruns shifted from contractors to owners. Tusiani & Shearer ch.2 (p.59)

Key takeaways

  • LNG projects are contracted before FID because the chain only creates value once it is complete.
  • Unit liquefaction costs rose from under $200 to $1,000–2,000 per t/y. Location, treatment, storage and scale drive them.
  • Structures differ by where the margin sits: integrated, tolling, formula-priced feed gas, or US capacity tolling.
  • A cost-of-service fee is mostly return of capital, so unit capex and the cost of capital set it. The US FOB price is 115% of Henry Hub plus that fee.
