Gas markets, hubs and pipeline capacity

Core≈ 60 min8 quiz questions7 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Explain how unbundling and third-party access created traded gas hubs.
  • Quote and convert hub prices, and read a basis price.
  • Value firm pipeline capacity as a spread option and compare it with the reservation charge.
  • Explain why spot gas is more volatile than forwards and why location spreads blow out.

Prerequisites

  • Module 1; Course 7 Module 2 (spread options and Kirk's approximation).

From utilities to traded hubs

For most of the twentieth century, gas was sold by vertically integrated utilities and pipelines that earned a regulated return on their assets and passed the cost of gas through to customers. They had little reason to buy cheaply, because any saving went back to consumers. Tusiani & Shearer ch.4 (p.152) Liberalisation broke this up. Pipelines had to unbundle their merchant role from their transport role and offer capacity to anyone on non-discriminatory terms, known as open access or third-party access (TPA). Many shippers could then use the same line, and gas could be bought and sold along the grid. It started in the US in the mid-1980s and spread to the UK, Canada and then continental Europe. Tusiani & Shearer ch.4 (p.152) Where it went furthest, gas-on-gas competition replaced oil-linked contract prices, regional hubs emerged with transparent benchmarks, and futures, swaps and basis markets followed. Tusiani & Shearer ch.4 (p.153)

Gas also became the marginal fuel in most competitive power markets, so liberalised gas and power markets now move together. Tusiani & Shearer ch.4 (pp.152–153) Course 5 covers why the marginal plant sets the power price; this course covers the fuel side.

The benchmark hubs

  • Henry Hub. A physical interconnection of 13 pipelines in Louisiana and the delivery point of the NYMEX futures contract. It is the North American benchmark, and unlike an equity index it is the price at one place, not an average. Edwards ch.2.1
  • NBP. The UK National Balancing Point is a virtual trading point inside the grid, not a physical location. It is Europe's oldest liquid hub, trades on ICE and is quoted in pence per therm. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (pp.460–461)
  • TTF and the continental hubs. The Dutch TTF, Zeebrugge and the German hubs grew rapidly as buyers pushed sellers such as Norway and Russia to add hub price terms to their contracts. Oil indexation held on longest in southern and eastern Europe. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (pp.461–462)
  • Asia. Without a grid, third-party access or competing pipeline supply, Asian LNG has mostly been priced against crude, through the Japan Customs-cleared crude (JCC) index. Tusiani and Shearer doubted that a true traded hub could develop there. Tusiani & Shearer ch.4 (p.168) Module 4 covers oil-indexed formulas. The JKM spot assessment that is now widely used for Asian cargoes is outside the texts. Outside the texts

Hub prices are quoted in different units. NBP trades in pence per therm; since 10 therms make one MMBtu, converting to dollars per MMBtu needs only the exchange rate. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (p.461)

\[ P_{\$/\text{MMBtu}} = \frac{P_{\text{p/therm}}}{100} \times 10 \times \text{FX}_{\$/\pounds} \]
Worked example 1 · pence per therm and basisChecked by code

NBP trades at 70 p/therm with sterling at $1.25. A Texas trader hears that Henry Hub is $3.52 and Waha gas is $3.70. Edwards ch.2.1

NBP = 70 / 100 × 10 × 1.25 = $8.75/MMBtu Waha basis = 3.70 − 3.52 = +$0.18/MMBtu ("Waha plus 18")

North American traders quote locations as a basis to Henry Hub. A position is usually built in two pieces: a liquid Henry Hub futures trade, then a basis swap to move the exposure to the location. The all-in price is the index plus the basis. Edwards ch.2.1 This is the stack-and-roll and basis hedging logic of Course 7 Module 6 applied to gas.

What basis measures

Tusiani and Shearer describe basis as a proxy for the market value of moving gas between two places, even when the pipeline charges a regulated tariff that rarely equals the basis. Tusiani & Shearer ch.4 (p.170) Swindle makes the same point for TETM3 against Henry Hub: locational price relationships come from real infrastructure costs. Swindle ch.6 (p.123) Edwards explains the regional patterns. The Gulf Coast produces gas, has plenty of storage and trades close to Henry Hub. The East Coast and New England have high demand, little storage and the highest winter basis. The West is separated by the Rockies and correlates weakly with Henry Hub. Edwards ch.2.1 Basis is small and stable when pipes have spare capacity, and spikes when they are full.

Pipeline capacity and its tariff

Shippers buy transport from the pipeline at two levels of service. Firm service is guaranteed except for force majeure. Interruptible service is offered on a best-efforts basis whenever firm shippers are not using their capacity, and is much cheaper. Edwards ch.5.1 A firm contract has several charges, all published in the pipeline's tariff: Edwards ch.2.1

  • a reservation charge, a fixed monthly payment for the right to the capacity, whether used or not;
  • a commodity charge per MMBtu actually shipped;
  • fuel retention, a percentage of the gas kept by the pipeline to power its compressors;
  • small surcharges, and either zone-to-zone rates or a single postage-stamp rate.

Edwards frames firm transport as insurance: the shipper pays a premium (the reservation charge) for guaranteed delivery, and the pipeline sells the unused capacity on an interruptible basis. Edwards ch.5.1 A shipper with firm capacity from a supply hub to a market area holds a series of options. Each day it can buy at the hub, pay fuel and the commodity charge, and sell at the market, or leave the capacity idle. That is a spread option with a strike equal to the variable cost, and the reservation charge is its premium. Synthesis

\[ V_{\text{capacity}} = E\left[\max\left(P_{\text{market}} - (1+f)\,P_{\text{hub}} - c,\ 0\right)\right] \quad\text{vs. the reservation charge} \]

Course 7 Module 2 valued spark spreads the same way with Kirk's approximation. Here the heat rate is replaced by one plus the fuel percentage, and the variable cost by the commodity charge. Synthesis

Worked example 2 · firm capacity into a winter marketChecked by code

A shipper can buy firm capacity for the winter delivery month three months ahead. Henry Hub forwards are $3.40 and the market-area forwards are $4.20. Volatilities are 45% and 60% and the correlation is 0.80. Using Edwards's illustrative tariff, fuel is 0.24%, the commodity charge is $0.035 and the reservation charge is $0.27/MMBtu. Edwards ch.2.1 The discount rate is 4%.

intrinsic (discounted) = e^(−0.04×0.25) × (4.20 − 1.0024 × 3.40 − 0.035) = $0.7493 spread-option value (Kirk) = $0.7960 time value = $0.0467 value net of reservation charge = 0.7960 − 0.27 = $0.5260/MMBtu

When the basis is wide, capacity is worth mostly its intrinsic spread and the reservation charge is easily covered. The case that matters for pricing is a narrow basis.

Worked example 3 · capacity that looks out of the moneyChecked by code

Now the market-area forward is only $3.55, so the forward basis is $0.15 and the intrinsic value net of fuel and commodity charge is about a dime.

intrinsic (discounted) = $0.1058 option value at ρ = 0.80 = $0.3058 → net of $0.27 reservation $0.0358 option value at ρ = 0.95 = $0.2139 (below the reservation charge)

Capacity that loses money on the forward basis can still be worth the reservation charge, because winter basis blows out when the pipe is full. The value comes from the volatility of the spread, so correlation drives it: as the two hubs move more closely together, the spread volatility and the capacity value collapse. Edwards notes that spot gas is far more volatile than forwards and that location spreads widen exactly when transport cannot be arranged at short notice, which is when firm capacity pays out. Storage, seasonality and spot volatility

Pipelines must run continuously, but demand swings by hour, day and season, so gas has to be stored at both ends of the system. Edwards ch.2.1 Most storage is underground: depleted fields, salt caverns and aquifers. They differ in how much cushion gas they must hold to keep pressure up (about 50%, 33% and 80% of capacity respectively) and in how fast they cycle. Salt caverns can be filled and emptied several times a year and earn money on short-term spikes. Edwards ch.5.2 Course 7 Module 4 values a storage facility as a strip of calendar spread options.

Gas forward curves follow the seasons rather than a smooth carry slope, because they reflect expected winter heating and summer power demand. Spot prices are far more volatile than forwards, since gas cannot be moved or stored at short notice. Volatility therefore rises as a contract nears delivery. Forward prices at different locations are highly correlated, because there is time to move gas between them. Spot correlations are lower, because there is not. Edwards ch.2.1 Weather is the largest single driver of demand. Edwards ch.2.1

ALM analogy

Firm capacity is a liquidity facility. An insurer pays a commitment fee for a credit line it may never draw, because the draw happens exactly when markets are stressed. A shipper pays a reservation charge for the same reason. Valuing it on the forward basis is like valuing a liquidity line at today's spread: it ignores that the line is worth most in the tail. Synthesis

Dated: hubs after 2021

Tusiani and Shearer describe NBP as Europe's deepest hub. TTF has since become the European benchmark, and JKM is the reference for Asian spot LNG. In 2022 the loss of most Russian pipeline flows sent TTF and JKM to record highs, and European prices became set by the LNG needed to replace that gas. Check ICE and CME volume statistics and ACER's market monitoring reports for current liquidity. Outside the texts

Going deeper: firm capacity as an insurance product

Edwards works through a pipeline that pays $12.50 per MMBtu of firm service it fails to deliver, earns $1 per MMBtu on delivered firm service and $0.15 on interruptible service. Adding spare compressors raises reliability, so more capacity can be sold as firm, which multiplies profit even though the backups sit idle. Edwards ch.5.1 The economics are those of an insurer: reliability is the product, and the reserve (spare compression) is what makes the guarantee credible. From the shipper's side, firm capacity is a strip of daily spread options plus protection against being cut off. Its value depends on joint price behaviour at both ends in the few days a year the system is tight, which a monthly forward-based model will understate. Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • Unbundling and third-party access turned gas into a traded commodity with hubs, futures and basis markets.
  • Henry Hub is a physical point, NBP and TTF are virtual hubs, and Asian LNG has mostly been oil-indexed.
  • Basis prices the value of moving gas between two places; it widens when pipes are full.
  • Firm capacity is a spread option whose premium is the reservation charge. Its value comes from spread volatility, so correlation is the key input.
