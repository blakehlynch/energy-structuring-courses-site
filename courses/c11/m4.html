Two inheritances: take-or-pay and oil indexation

Long-term LNG contracts borrowed two features almost from the start. The first was take-or-pay, inherited from North American pipeline gas contracts of the 1960s and 1970s: the buyer commits to lift a set volume each year or pay for the shortfall, which gives lenders a predictable revenue stream. The second was oil indexation. The very first contracts used fixed prices with cost escalators, much like a regulated pipeline tariff. Inflation and the oil shocks of the 1970s broke that model, and exporters, many of them OPEC members, pushed for prices linked to oil. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (p.448) By 1980 almost all LNG contracts combined take-or-pay with crude-linked prices, often with an S-curve. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (p.449)

The US experience shows what happens when the market under these contracts changes. Wellhead decontrol and recession created a gas surplus in the early 1980s. In 1984, FERC Order 380 let pipeline customers walk away from their purchase obligations while the pipelines stayed bound to their producers, and the resulting take-or-pay claims put several pipelines into bankruptcy. Algeria's LNG contracts with the US collapsed at the same time. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (p.450) The El Paso–Sonatrach dispute is the classic case: Algeria sought oil parity, the US government refused to accept much more than the $1.30 contract price, and deliveries stopped in 1980. Tusiani & Shearer ch.4 (pp.149–150) Nearly three-quarters of LNG traded in 2014 was still sold under long-term contracts. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (p.451)

Quantities

The value of an SPA is set by its quantities, prices and term, plus whether it is sold FOB, CIF or DAP. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (p.451) The quantity clauses are where most of the optionality sits:

  • Annual contract quantity (ACQ), usually in MMBtu, which the buyer must take or pay for. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (p.451)
  • Build-up. Early deliveries while the plant is commissioned are on a reasonable-efforts basis, not take-or-pay. Buyers also get a ramp-up to grow their market. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (pp.451–452)
  • Downward quantity tolerance (DQT), the buyer's right to cut its ACQ by a set amount, normally around 5%. Total downward flexibility in traditional contracts was under 10%. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (p.452) Tusiani & Shearer ch.4 (p.146)
  • Round-up and round-down, because an ACQ in MMBtu cannot be delivered exactly in whole cargoes. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (pp.452–453)
  • Excess quantities produced by conservatively designed plants. These are very profitable, and who gets first call on them is a hard-fought point; an FOB buyer with ships is better placed to claim them. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (p.453)
  • Make-up. A buyer that pays for a shortfall can usually take the equivalent volume later, with scheduling priority, often within about five years, sometimes with a price adjustment. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (p.454)

These are the swing and take-or-pay features valued in Course 7 Module 5. A DQT is a strip of options to decline gas, and make-up turns a penalty into a prepayment for future gas. Synthesis

Worked example 1 · a take-or-pay shortfallChecked by code

An SPA has an ACQ of 50 TBtu (50 million MMBtu), a 5% DQT and a $9.00 contract price. In a weak year the buyer lifts only 44 TBtu. Spot LNG is $6.00 and it would cost $0.80 to divert and resell a cargo.

minimum take = 50 × (1 − 0.05) = 47.5 TBtu take-or-pay shortfall = 47.5 − 44.0 = 3.5 TBtu payment for shortfall = 3.5m × $9.00 = $31.5m (make-up gas owed later) alternative: lift and resell = 3.5m × (9.00 − 6.00 + 0.80) = $13.3m loss

Lifting and reselling loses $13.3 million, against paying $31.5 million now for gas that can be taken later. If the make-up gas can be taken within the window and used profitably, the $31.5 million is mostly a prepayment and costs only its financing and the risk that make-up gas is worth less when it arrives. If it is likely to be forfeited, lifting and reselling is cheaper. That is why buyers with ships and trading desks lift and resell rather than pay. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (p.454) Tusiani & Shearer ch.4 (p.164) Synthesis

Pricing in Asia: slopes and S-curves

Asian LNG prices have been set against crude because buyers had no competing gas supply. Japan uses the JCC, an average of up to 200 crudes imported each month. The original idea was that LNG landed in Japan should cost about the same as crude, which power stations then burned. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (p.456) The formula is linear in the crude price:

\[ P_{\text{LNG}} = \beta \times P_{\text{JCC}} + \alpha \]

The slope β was 0.1485 in early Indonesian contracts. It is renegotiated higher in tight markets and lower in loose ones. The constant α can include shipping and inflation terms, and prices are usually averaged and lagged by a few months. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (p.457) A barrel of crude holds about 5.8 MMBtu, so a slope of 1/5.8 ≈ 17.2% would be energy parity. Swindle ch.3 (p.33) A 14.85% slope therefore prices LNG at a discount to crude per unit of energy. Synthesis

As oil became more volatile, both sides wanted to damp the link. The S-curve keeps the base slope inside a band of oil prices and applies a flatter slope outside it, sometimes with a floor and ceiling. The seller gets downside protection, which also helps the project's financing, and the buyer gets upside protection. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (pp.457–458) Tusiani and Shearer do not give standard kink points or outer slopes; those are negotiated, so the numbers below are illustrative. Synthesis

Worked example 2 · a 14.85% slope with an S-curveChecked by code

Price = 14.85% × JCC + $0.50. Between JCC $50 and $90 the base slope applies; outside that band the slope is 7%.

JCC $70: 0.1485 × 70 + 0.50 = $10.895 JCC $110: straight line 0.1485 × 110 + 0.50 = $16.835 S-curve 0.1485 × 90 + 0.50 + 0.07 × 20 = $15.265 JCC $30: straight line = $4.955 S-curve 0.1485 × 50 + 0.50 − 0.07 × 20 = $6.525

At $110 oil the buyer saves $1.57/MMBtu; at $30 the seller earns an extra $1.57. The S-curve is a collar on the oil link: the buyer is long a call spread above the upper kink and the seller is long a put spread below the lower one. Synthesis

Pricing in Europe and the Atlantic

Continental European LNG and pipeline gas was indexed to oil products as well as crude, reflecting the fuels gas displaced in end-use markets: gasoil, low- and high-sulphur fuel oil and sometimes coal. Most European contracts had no S-curve, though many had price floors, which raises the question of a matching ceiling or a recoupment mechanism. The typical formula indexes changes from a base period. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (p.459)

\[ P_n = P_0 \times \left( W_1 \frac{F_{1,n}}{F_{1,0}} + W_2 \frac{F_{2,n}}{F_{2,0}} \right) \]
Worked example 3 · a base-period formulaChecked by code

The base price is $8.00, weighted 60% to gasoil and 40% to fuel oil.

gasoil +10%, fuel oil −10%: 8.00 × (0.6 × 1.10 + 0.4 × 0.90) = $8.16 gasoil +30%, fuel oil +20%: 8.00 × (0.6 × 1.30 + 0.4 × 1.20) = $10.08

Because the formula tracks percentage changes in substitute fuels, a buyer's price follows its competitors' fuel costs, which was the point when gas competed with oil in boilers and power plants. Once hub gas was available, oil-indexed buyers lost customers to rivals buying spot, and contracts were reset towards hub prices, often in arbitration. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (pp.461, 464) Tusiani & Shearer ch.2 (p.64)

Contracts for power generators sometimes index to fuel oil, coal, inflation or even power prices, so that the gas plant stays in merit and runs at a high load factor. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (p.460) US import contracts were priced against Henry Hub, at 85–93% of it in 2005–06. US exports reverse the logic with the 115%-of-Henry-Hub formula of Module 3. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (pp.460, 462)

Reviews, term and other clauses

  • Price reviews. Many contracts allow periodic reopeners, open-ended or within bands, with reference to arbitration if the parties cannot agree. They are rarer where liquid hubs exist. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (pp.463–464)
  • Term. Traditional SPAs run 20 years or more, set to balance the reserves that must be proven against the time needed to recover the investment. Make-up and force majeure can extend them. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (p.464)
  • Gas quality. Rich LNG suits Asian buyers; lean LNG suits North America. Off-specification cargoes are handled by compensating the buyer's treatment cost, for example nitrogen injection or LPG spiking, rather than rejection. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (pp.465–466)
  • Transport. Under FOB the seller provides a safe berth and the buyer's ship must be compatible. The buyer pays if its ship overstays at the berth, and the seller pays demurrage if loading is delayed by the plant. A ship that arrives warm must be cooled down at the buyer's cost. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (pp.466–467) Course 3 covers laytime and demurrage for oil.
  • Scheduling is now reset on a rolling basis, for example every 30 days for the next 90, and interacts heavily with take-or-pay and make-up. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (p.468)
  • Payment and credit. Payment within 10–30 days used to rest on utility credit. Newer, weaker or special-purpose buyers post letters of credit or guarantees, as in Course 4. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (pp.468–469)
  • Title and risk pass at the loading flange under FOB, on unloading under DAP, and wherever agreed under CIF, often on the high seas for tax reasons. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (pp.469–470)
  • Force majeure covers physical prevention, not a contract becoming uneconomic ("price majeure"). Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (pp.470–471)
  • Law and disputes. English or New York law, with confidential international arbitration. "Best efforts" means more under English law than under US law. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (pp.471–473)
  • Destination and diversion. Traditional contracts named the receiving terminal. FOB buyers learned to divert cargoes to premium markets, and sellers responded with destination restrictions or profit-sharing clauses, including for reloads. EU policy forced suppliers to drop destination clauses. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (pp.474–475) Tusiani & Shearer ch.4 (p.164)
  • Spot trades run under master SPAs that fix the standard terms, with a short confirmation for each cargo's price, timing and ports. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (p.474)
ALM analogy

Take-or-pay with make-up is a policy with a surrender penalty and a reinstatement right. The buyer can lapse (pay the shortfall) and later reinstate (take make-up gas), so the penalty's real cost is the time value and the chance of not reinstating. DQT and round-up are the partial-withdrawal options. Price reviews are the guaranteed-rate resets that let both sides reprice a long contract when the market has moved. Synthesis

Dated: pricing after 2015

Tusiani and Shearer describe oil indexation retreating in northwest Europe but holding in Asia. Since then, Asian buyers have signed more Henry Hub- and JKM-linked volumes, slopes on new oil-linked deals have moved with the market cycle, and Europe signed new long-term contracts after 2022 to replace Russian gas. Check current slope levels in industry reporting and the GIIGNL annual report. Outside the texts

Going deeper: the price formula decides who bears which risk

Every SPA price formula is an allocation of risk. A pure oil slope makes the buyer long oil-price risk relative to its gas-priced customers, and the seller short the basis between oil and gas. Tusiani and Shearer summarise the traditional bargain simply: the buyer took the volume risk through take-or-pay, and the upstream took the price risk. Tusiani & Shearer ch.2 (p.43) Henry Hub-linked US LNG gives the buyer gas-on-gas exposure and a fixed fee, removing the oil link but creating a Henry Hub-to-destination spread risk. A hedger of LNG supply has to decompose the formula into the same price level, basis and conversion risks as in Course 7 Module 6: crude or Henry Hub level, the lag and averaging, and the spread to the market where the gas is sold. Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • LNG SPAs inherited take-or-pay from pipeline gas and oil indexation from the 1970s oil shocks.
  • Quantity clauses (DQT, round-up, excess, make-up) are embedded options; a shortfall payment is partly a prepayment.
  • Asia uses a slope on JCC, often with an S-curve; Europe used base-period product formulas; US exports use 115% of Henry Hub plus a fee.
  • Delivery terms, title transfer, force majeure and destination clauses decide who can capture arbitrage.
