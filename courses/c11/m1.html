Gas, units and the LNG chain

Learning objectives

  • Convert between tonnes of LNG, bcm, MMBtu and therms, and explain why the conversion depends on gas quality.
  • Describe the physical, ownership and commercial chains of an LNG project.
  • Build a delivered cost stack and compute a wellhead netback.
  • Size a train's annual output in cargoes and energy.

Prerequisites

  • Course 1 Module 1 (the oil value chain) for the idea of a netback.
  • No gas background assumed.

What natural gas is

Natural gas is mostly methane, with varying amounts of ethane, propane, butane and heavier hydrocarbons, plus nitrogen, carbon dioxide and traces of sulphur compounds. Gas that is nearly pure methane is called dry; gas carrying a lot of the heavier hydrocarbons is called wet. Edwards ch.2.1 Gas is found on its own (non-associated) or produced alongside crude oil (associated gas). Before it can be sold it must be treated to strip out water, CO2, sulphur, mercury and the heavier liquids. Those liquids, condensate and LPG, are sold separately and can pay for a large share of an upstream development. Tusiani & Shearer ch.2 (pp.44–45) LNG itself is typically 87–99% methane by mole, depending on the field and the processing. Mokhatab et al. §1.3.1

Because the mix varies, gas is traded by energy content, not by volume. A cubic foot of dry gas holds roughly 1,000 Btu, and North American pipeline gas must meet a heating-value band of about 1,035 Btu per cubic foot, plus or minus 5%. Edwards ch.2.1 Two gases with the same volume can therefore be worth different amounts. This matters most for LNG, where cargoes from different plants carry noticeably different energy per tonne.

Units: energy, volume and mass

The market uses three families of unit, and a structurer has to move between them without thinking:

  • Energy. The MMBtu (one million British thermal units) is the North American and LNG pricing unit. One billion cubic feet (Bcf) of gas is about one million MMBtu. A therm is one-tenth of an MMBtu, so a dekatherm (10 therms) equals one MMBtu. Edwards ch.2.1 Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (p.461) The UK NBP hub trades in pence per therm, which Module 2 converts.
  • Gas volume. Standard cubic feet in North America and cubic metres (bcm for billions) elsewhere. National balances and pipeline flows are quoted in bcm a year or Bcf a day.
  • LNG mass and volume. Plants are rated in million tonnes a year (MMt/y, also written Mtpa). Ships and tanks are sized in cubic metres of liquid. Mokhatab et al. §1.3.4

Liquefaction cools gas to about −162°C at atmospheric pressure, shrinking it to roughly 1/600th of its gas volume. The liquid's density is about 430–470 kg per cubic metre, less than half that of water. Mokhatab et al. §1.3.1 Mokhatab et al. give rules of thumb for converting plant output: one million tonnes a year of LNG corresponds to about 1.316 bcm a year of gas, or about 127 million cubic feet a day. They stress that the true factor depends on composition. Mokhatab et al. §1.3.4 Stopford uses 1.38 bcm per million tonnes, which shows how much the factor can move. Stopford §12.4

To get from tonnes to MMBtu you need a heating value for the regasified gas. The books give the volume conversion but not a single energy factor, so the examples on this site assume 1,100 Btu per standard cubic foot. That sits above the 1,035 pipeline specification because LNG for Asia is typically richer in ethane and propane. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (p.465) Synthesis Gas quality moves the energy per cubic foot far more than the energy per tonne. By mass, methane carries slightly more energy than ethane or propane, so rich and lean LNG hold almost the same MMBtu per tonne, and the rich cargo just regasifies to fewer cubic feet. The site therefore holds energy per tonne fixed at the value below, and the widely quoted market rule of thumb of about 52 MMBtu per tonne sits in the same range. Outside the texts

A single 4.5 MMt/y train, the size of Tusiani and Shearer's generic project, runs at 93% availability. Tusiani & Shearer ch.7 (pp.264, 271) It loads 174,000 m³ ships with LNG at 0.45 t/m³. The regasified gas is worth 1,100 Btu per cubic foot.

energy per tonne = 1,316 × 35.3147 × 1,100 / 10^6 = 51.12 MMBtu/t nameplate gas equivalent = 4.5 × 1.316 = 5.92 bcm/y tonnes produced = 4.5m × 0.93 = 4.185 million t energy produced = 4.185m t × 51.12 MMBtu/t = 213.9 million MMBtu cargo = 174,000 m³ × 0.45 t/m³ = 78,300 t ≈ 4.00 million MMBtu cargoes a year = 4.185m t / 78,300 t = 53.4 cargo value at $8/MMBtu = $32.0m

One train fills a large carrier roughly once a week, and each cargo is worth about $32 million at $8. Quality changes the volume, not the energy: at the 1,035 Btu pipeline specification the same tonne would regasify to 1.399 bcm per million tonnes instead of 1.316, and Stopford's 1.38 factor matches gas of about 1,049 Btu per cubic foot. The two books' factors describe gases of different quality rather than disagreeing. A volume quoted without a heating value can be 5–10% off in energy, which is why SPAs measure and invoice in MMBtu, not cubic metres or cubic feet. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (p.469) Synthesis

Why LNG exists: stranded gas

Pipelines are the cheap way to move gas over land and over short sea crossings. About 70% of internationally traded gas still moves by pipeline and about 30% as LNG. Mokhatab et al. §1.2 LNG wins on long routes, above all across oceans where an undersea pipeline would be prohibitively expensive. Mokhatab et al. put the break-even against an offshore pipeline at about 700 miles, and against an onshore line at about 2,200 miles. Mokhatab et al. §1.2 The cost of carrying the energy is far higher than for oil: Stopford notes that moving a tonne of LNG has cost $25–100, against $7–10 for a tonne of crude from the Middle East to Europe. Stopford §12.4 That gap explains most of what follows. Oil can be produced first and sold later. An LNG project needs its buyers, ships and terminals lined up before anyone builds the plant. Tusiani & Shearer ch.7 (p.263)

The physical chain

Tusiani and Shearer describe four linked stages, each tied to the next by long contracts. Tusiani & Shearer ch.2 (pp.41–43)

  • Upstream. Field development, treatment and a feed-gas pipeline to the coast. Unlike the other stages, upstream needs continuing investment to offset field decline. Tusiani & Shearer ch.2 (pp.44–45)
  • Liquefaction. The plant treats the gas again and cools it to about −163°C in parallel processing units called trains. Train sizes grew from about 1 MMt/y in the earliest plants to 7.8 MMt/y in Qatar, though recent projects prefer 4–5 MMt/y. The plant includes double-walled storage tanks of up to about 200,000 m³ and a jetty. Tusiani & Shearer ch.2 (pp.45–47)
  • Shipping. Insulated carriers of 155,000–170,000 m³ are the standard, with Qatar's largest ships at about 266,000 m³. Some cargo boils off every day of the voyage, up to 0.15% a day on older ships, and is usually burned as fuel. A small heel is kept on board to keep the tanks cold. Tusiani & Shearer ch.2 (pp.48–49)
  • Regasification. Import terminals unload, store and warm the LNG, then send it out as gas. Many are now floating storage and regasification units (FSRUs). Tusiani & Shearer ch.2 (pp.49–50)

The delivery term decides who arranges the ship. Under FOB the buyer charters the vessel and takes title at loading. Under a delivered sale (DAP, which replaced the older DES term in Incoterms 2010, or CIF) the seller arranges shipping. Tusiani & Shearer ch.2 (pp.48, 64)

The ownership chain

Each project divides the chain differently, but Tusiani and Shearer distinguish four main models. Tusiani & Shearer ch.2 (pp.55–56)

ModelLiquefaction is…Who carries price and volume risk
A. IntegratedOwned by the same partners as the upstream; no transfer price for feed gasAll partners, through one combined margin
B. Profit centreA separate company that buys feed gas and sells LNGThe liquefaction company, whose margin moves with LNG prices and volumes
C. Cost centre or tollPaid a fixed or semi-fixed fee to process gas, often on hell-or-high-water termsMostly the gas owners; the plant earns a utility-like margin
D. Capacity tolling (US)Owned by an infrastructure company that leases capacityThe capacity holders, who buy feed gas at hub prices and sell the LNG anywhere

The trend runs in both directions. Producers integrate downstream by taking terminal capacity, and buyers integrate upstream by taking equity in plants and buying FOB. Tusiani & Shearer ch.2 (pp.52–54) Liquefaction is now normally project-financed with 60–70% debt, and a project can carry a better credit rating than its host country because lenders look through to the buyers' contracts. Tusiani & Shearer ch.2 (p.52) Course 9 covers that financing structure.

The commercial chain and the netback

Value is only realised when the gas is sold in the end market. The buyer's resale revenue pays the terminal and, under FOB, the ship. The LNG price then pays the plant, the feed gas, the debt and the host government. Integrated sponsors care most about their netback: the value left at the wellhead after every downstream cost has been paid. Tusiani & Shearer ch.2 (p.58) Tusiani and Shearer's generic 4.5 MMt/y project from East Africa to Asia costs $9–14.5 billion in total. Of that, $3–5 billion goes upstream, $4–7 billion on liquefaction, $1.5–1.7 billion on ships and $0.4–0.7 billion on a terminal. That works out to roughly $2–3, $3–5, $1.5 and $0.5 per MMBtu, and a delivered cost of $7–10. Tusiani & Shearer ch.7 (p.264)

Worked example 2 · cost stack and wellhead netbackChecked by code

Take the midpoints of Tusiani and Shearer's ranges: production $2.50, liquefaction $4.00, shipping $1.50 and regasification $0.50 per MMBtu. The cargo sells into Asia at $10.00 delivered.

delivered cost = 2.50 + 4.00 + 1.50 + 0.50 = $8.50/MMBtu liquefaction share = 4.00 / 8.50 = 47.1% shipping share = 17.6% wellhead netback at $10 = 10.00 − 0.50 − 1.50 − 4.00 = $4.00/MMBtu (margin over full cost $1.50) wellhead netback at $7 = 7.00 − 0.50 − 1.50 − 4.00 = $1.00/MMBtu

Liquefaction is the largest single slice, as both Tusiani and Shearer and Stopford stress. Stopford's older split put about 40% of chain cost in liquefaction and 25% in shipping. Tusiani & Shearer ch.2 (p.45) Stopford §12.4 The netback is a residual: when the delivered price falls from $10 to $7, every dollar comes out of the upstream. At $1, the netback is below the $3–4 feed-gas cost Tusiani and Shearer quote for typical LNG developments. Tusiani & Shearer ch.2 (p.59) Synthesis

ALM analogy

The LNG chain is a matched-funding structure. Each stage is a long-dated, illiquid asset financed against a contract with the next stage, the way a project-financed annuity book is matched to its liabilities. The netback is the residual equity: senior claims (regas, shipping, debt service on the plant) are paid first, and the upstream absorbs the price risk, just as surplus absorbs asset-liability mismatches. Going deeper: why a tonne is not a fixed number of MMBtu

The more ethane and propane LNG carries, the more Btu each cubic foot of regasified gas holds. Pacific buyers historically wanted rich LNG, North American pipeline systems wanted lean gas, and Europe sat in between. Rich LNG carries more MMBtu per cubic metre of ship, which lowers transport cost per unit of energy. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (p.465) Per tonne the gain is small, because the richer liquid is also denser. Outside the texts The composition also changes on the voyage. Nitrogen and methane boil off first, so the cargo gets heavier and its heating value rises the longer it sails, a process called weathering or ageing. Mokhatab et al. §1.4.5.1 Contracts handle this by measuring the energy delivered: tank gauges give the volume, samples give the composition, and the product is invoiced in MMBtu. Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (p.469) For a structurer, quoting a price per cubic metre is a basis risk in disguise. Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • Gas trades by energy. LNG output is quoted in tonnes, but the conversion to MMBtu depends on composition.
  • LNG exists because ocean pipelines are uneconomic beyond a few hundred miles, but it costs far more per unit of energy to move than oil.
  • The chain has four contract-linked stages. Ownership models differ in who earns the margin and who carries price and volume risk.
  • Liquefaction is the largest cost. The wellhead netback is a residual that absorbs every change in the delivered price.
