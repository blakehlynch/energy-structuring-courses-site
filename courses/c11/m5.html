LNG shipping and logistics

Core≈ 55 min8 quiz questions6 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Describe LNG carrier sizes, containment systems, boil-off and heel.
  • Explain time, bareboat, trip-time and voyage charters and who pays which costs.
  • Compute the cost per delivered MMBtu of a round voyage.
  • Size a fleet for a train and explain why shipping control matters for flexibility.

Prerequisites

  • Modules 1 and 4; Course 3 Module 5 (tanker freight).

The ships

LNG carriers are double-hulled ships with insulated cargo tanks that hold the liquid just above atmospheric pressure at about −160°C. Mokhatab et al. §1.4.5.1 There are two families of containment. Self-supporting tanks, mostly Moss spheres, are robust and resist sloshing. Membrane tanks use the ship's hull as the outer tank with a thin metal barrier and insulation inside; they use the hull more efficiently, and more than three-quarters of ships built in 2001–2011 were membrane designs. Mokhatab et al. §1.4.5.1 Sizes have grown from 125,000 m³ in the 1980s to an average of 164,000 m³ for ships delivered since 2010. Tusiani & Shearer ch.7 (p.283) The trade has settled on about 170,000 m³ as the flexible standard, rather than Qatar's 216,000 m³ Q-Flex and 266,000 m³ Q-Max ships, which some terminals cannot take. Tusiani & Shearer ch.10 (p.362)

Propulsion has moved from steam turbines, which burn boil-off easily but use a lot of fuel, to dual- and tri-fuel diesel-electric engines and then slow-speed gas-injection engines. Tusiani and Shearer's table shows fuel use falling from about 180 tonnes a day on a 145,000 m³ steam ship to about 110 on a 174,000 m³ gas-injection ship, at a 19.5-knot service speed. Tusiani & Shearer ch.10 (p.363) A modern 174,000 m³ ship cost about $205–210 million at the yard, or $225–230 million delivered with finance and owner's costs. Tusiani & Shearer ch.2 (p.61) Tusiani & Shearer ch.7 (p.283)

Boil-off, heel and cooldown

No insulation is perfect, so some cargo evaporates every day. The boil-off must be removed to hold tank pressure: it is burned as fuel, burned in a gas combustion unit, or reliquefied on ships fitted to do so. Tusiani & Shearer ch.10 (pp.364–365) Typical rates are 0.10–0.15% of the cargo a day, with older ships up to 0.25% and the newest claiming about 0.09%. Mokhatab et al. §1.4.5.1 Tusiani & Shearer ch.10 (p.364) Because nitrogen and methane boil first, the cargo slowly becomes heavier and richer, raising its heating value. Mokhatab et al. §1.4.5.1

After discharge a ship keeps a small heel of LNG to keep its tanks cold for the ballast voyage, and that heel's boil-off fuels the return trip. A ship that has used up its heel, or has come from a shipyard, must be cooled down before it can load at full rate, which takes hours, uses LNG and is usually charged to whoever caused it. Tusiani & Shearer ch.10 (pp.363–364) Tusiani & Shearer ch.14 (p.467) Loading or discharging a large ship takes about 14 hours at a shore terminal, at about 12,000 m³ an hour; ship-to-ship transfer to an FSRU runs at about half that rate. Tusiani & Shearer ch.10 (p.366)

Charter contracts

A charterparty is the contract for the ship's service, separate from the bill of lading that covers the cargo. Tusiani & Shearer ch.15 (pp.477–478) The main types differ in who runs the ship and who pays which costs: Tusiani & Shearer ch.15 (pp.478–484)

CharterWhat the charterer paysTypical use
Time charterDaily hire (a capital element plus an operating element), plus voyage costs: fuel, port charges, canal dues and boil-offLong-term project shipping, often for the life of the SPA
Bareboat (demise)Hire equal to the capital element; the charterer crews, runs, maintains and insures the shipCharterers wanting full control, often with a purchase option
Trip-timeDaily hire for one voyage, with fuel and port costs; no demurrage, since hire runs until dischargeSpot and short-term cargoes
Voyage / COAA rate per tonne of cargo covering voyage costs; a COA is ship-or-pay over many cargoesRare in LNG because of disputes over the value of boil-off

The shipowner guarantees boil-off, speed, fuel use and pumping rates, and pays penalties if it misses them. The charterer stops paying hire when the ship is off-hire, for example during a breakdown. Dry-docking typically takes about 25 days every five years. Tusiani & Shearer ch.15 (pp.481–482, 492) Shell's LNG Time 1 form, issued in 2005, has become the reference charterparty. Tusiani & Shearer ch.15 (p.482) Long-term hire was about $70,000 a day in 2014. Spot rates have ranged from about $20,000 when ships were idle to $150,000 when the market was tight. Crewing and maintenance run $4–6 million a year, with about $3 million more for insurance and other owner's costs. Tusiani & Shearer ch.7 (pp.283–284)

What a voyage costs

The cost of moving an MMBtu depends on distance, speed, hire, boil-off and port and canal charges, spread over the energy actually delivered. Representative costs run from about $0.25–0.30/MMBtu within the Mediterranean to $1.00 from the US to Europe and $2.40 or more from the US to the Far East. Tusiani & Shearer ch.2 (pp.61–62) Tusiani & Shearer ch.7 (p.284) A simple round-voyage model:

\[ \text{cost per delivered MMBtu} = \frac{\text{hire}\times(\text{sea days}+\text{port days}) + \text{boil-off}\times P_{\text{gas}} + \text{port and canal}}{E_0\,(1 - b\times \text{sea days})} \]

Here E0 is the energy loaded and b the daily boil-off rate, applied to both legs because the heel kept for the ballast leg also boils off. Synthesis

Worked example 1 · US Gulf to northwest EuropeChecked by code

A 174,000 m³ ship loads LNG at 0.45 t/m³ and 51.12 MMBtu/t. It sails 4,900 nautical miles each way at 19.5 knots, spends four days in port, pays $70,000 a day of hire and $0.5 million in port costs, and boils off 0.10% a day, valued at $8. The route distance is an approximation from outside the texts. Outside the texts

laden days = 4,900 / (19.5 × 24) = 10.47 round voyage = 2 × 10.47 + 4 = 24.94 days energy loaded = 174,000 × 0.45 × 51.12 = 4.003m MMBtu boil-off = 0.10% × 20.94 days × 4.003m = 83.8k MMBtu → $0.671m delivered = 4.003m − 0.084m = 3.919m MMBtu hire = 70,000 × 24.94 = $1.746m total = 1.746 + 0.671 + 0.5 = $2.916m → $0.744/MMBtu

This is a little below Tusiani and Shearer's $1.00 for the US to Europe, which includes costs this sketch leaves out, such as positioning and idle time. Boil-off is almost a quarter of the cost. Tusiani & Shearer ch.2 (p.62) Synthesis

Same ship, 9,200 nautical miles each way, with $1.5 million for port and Panama Canal costs. Distance and canal cost are approximations from outside the texts. Outside the texts

round voyage = 2 × 19.66 + 4 = 43.32 days hire = 70,000 × 43.32 = $3.032m boil-off value = $1.259m total = 3.032 + 1.259 + 1.5 = $5.791m → $1.506/MMBtu at a $150,000/day spot hire = $2.407/MMBtu

At long-term hire the Asian voyage costs about twice the European one. At a tight-market spot rate it reaches Tusiani and Shearer's $2.40, so the charter rate can swing the Asian netback by almost a dollar. Tusiani & Shearer ch.2 (p.62) Tusiani & Shearer ch.7 (p.284) Synthesis

Fleet sizing and scheduling

Distance also decides how many ships a plant needs. Tusiani and Shearer give the example of a Middle East supplier: in the time one ship delivers two cargoes to the United States, it can deliver three to Spain, so the cost of extra short-term shipping can decide which market is preferred. Tusiani & Shearer ch.4 (p.167)

Worked example 3 · ships for one trainChecked by code

The 4.5 MMt/y train of Module 1 produces 53.4 cargoes a year. Allow each ship 350 trading days.

cargoes per ship, Europe = 350 / 24.94 = 14.0 → ships needed 3.8 cargoes per ship, Japan = 350 / 43.32 = 8.1 → ships needed 6.6

Serving Japan needs nearly three more ships than serving Europe, roughly $650 million of extra fleet at $225–230 million a ship. That is why destination flexibility needs access to spare ships, not just a contract clause. Tusiani & Shearer ch.4 (p.159) Synthesis

Scheduling ties the chain together. If ships are late and storage is small, the plant hits tank tops and has to cut production. Tusiani & Shearer ch.7 (pp.268–269) With several buyers each running their own ships of different sizes, scheduling gets harder and can cost production. Tusiani & Shearer ch.4 (p.160)

Who controls the ship

Historically, sellers controlled shipping to protect their much larger capital in the plant and to keep deliveries reliable. That has shifted. Buyers want ships for flexibility, destination choice, extra cargoes and cheaper charter rates on their stronger credit, and sellers worry about liabilities in foreign waters. Tusiani & Shearer ch.15 (pp.484–485) Control matters for arbitrage: when a plant tenders a spare cargo FOB, only firms with a ship available can bid, which often freezes out traders in winter when shipping is tight. Tusiani & Shearer ch.4 (pp.166–167) Owning a ship brings balance-sheet exposure, liability, residual value (ships now trade for about 40 years) and technical risk. Chartering moves those to the owner, but the charterer's only remedy for a breakdown is off-hire, which may not cover a missed take-or-pay delivery. Tusiani & Shearer ch.15 (pp.486–491)

ALM analogy

A long-term time charter is a fixed-rate lease liability that funds a floating-rate service, the cargo's delivered value. Spot chartering is the same service funded at a floating rate. The choice between them is a duration decision: lock in hire and carry the risk of paying above market when ships are idle, or stay floating and risk being priced out of a tight winter market. Synthesis

Dated: fleet and rates

The fleet has roughly doubled since Tusiani and Shearer wrote, slow-speed gas-injection ships now dominate orders, and IMO carbon-intensity rules (CII and EEXI, from 2023) penalise older steam ships. Spot charter rates spiked above $400,000 a day in late 2021 and 2022 before collapsing in 2024. Check Clarksons, Poten and broker reports for current rates and the GIIGNL annual report for fleet statistics. Outside the texts

Going deeper: the hidden cost of boil-off

Boil-off looks like a technical loss, but it is a cash cost paid by the charterer, who owns the cargo. Tusiani & Shearer ch.7 (p.284) Tusiani & Shearer ch.15 (p.481) Its value scales with the gas price and the voyage length, so long voyages become disproportionately expensive when gas is dear. In the Japan example, at $8 gas, boil-off adds about $0.33 per delivered MMBtu; at $20 gas it would add about $0.82. That links shipping cost to the price level and makes long-haul arbitrage less attractive exactly when prices spike. This is one reason charterers negotiate maximum guaranteed boil-off rates, and why Qatar's largest ships were built with reliquefaction plants to deliver their whole cargo. Tusiani & Shearer ch.15 (p.482) Tusiani & Shearer ch.2 (pp.48–49) Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • The standard carrier is about 170,000 m³; boil-off of 0.10–0.15% a day is burned as fuel or reliquefied, and a heel keeps tanks cold.
  • Time charters split hire into capital and operating parts, and the charterer pays fuel, port costs and boil-off.
  • Voyage cost per delivered MMBtu rises with distance, hire and the gas price; Asia from the US Gulf costs roughly twice Europe.
  • Fleet size scales with voyage time, and control of shipping decides who can capture arbitrage.
