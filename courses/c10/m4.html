Solar PPA hedge: shape and basis

Capstone≈ 70 min8 quiz questions4 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Decompose a pay-as-produced PPA into baseload price, shape, basis and volume.
  • Estimate the buyer's expected settlement and tail risk.
  • Design shape and basis hedges (firming, FTRs, fixed-shape swaps).
  • Explain the risks the buyer keeps.

Prerequisites

  • Courses 5 and 7.
The brief

A technology company wants to buy the output of a new 120 MW solar farm (about 250,000 MWh a year) through a 10-year virtual PPA at $38/MWh. It doesn't take physical power: each hour it receives the market price at the farm's node minus $38, times the farm's actual output (negative when prices are below $38). Its treasury asks: what does this cost us on average, how bad can a year get, and what should we hedge? Hub baseload forwards are $50. Synthesis

1. Decompose the exposure

A virtual PPA is a contract for differences on the farm's output. It hedges only if the buyer's own power purchases settle at the same price, which they rarely do. Kirschen & Strbac §5.3.5 Yescombe ch.6 (pp.107–108) Break the settlement into four layers. Synthesis

  • Price level: the hub baseload price (hedgeable with liquid swaps).
  • Shape: solar produces at midday, when prices are lowest in solar-heavy markets, so its capture price sits below baseload (Course 5's capture rate).
  • Basis: the farm's node trades at a discount to the hub when the local grid is congested. It is hedgeable with FTRs or CRRs. Kirschen & Strbac §5.3.5
  • Volume: weather drives output, and output correlates with price. High solar output pushes prices down, the same wrong-way covariance as Course 7's load swaps. Swindle ch.15 (p.377)

Hub baseload is $50 and solar captures 75% of it, so the capture price is $37.50. Node basis averages −$2 ± $3, and annual output varies ±6%, mildly negatively correlated with price. Price-level uncertainty is 25%, and we run 5,000 simulated years.

expected annual settlement at expected volume = 250,000 × (37.50 − 2 − 38) = $−0.625m (simulated mean $−0.646m once the −0.2 volume–price correlation is included) shape costs $3.125m a year relative to baseload, and basis $0.5m 5th percentile year $−4.28m; 95th percentile $+3.74m

A $38 PPA looks cheap against $50 baseload, but after shape and basis it is slightly out of the money. The buyer breaks even only if capture stays above 0.80 of baseload. If solar build-out pushes capture to 60%, expected cost rises to $−2.50m a year. Synthesis

2. Hedge design

  • Price level: if the buyer wants a fixed cost, it can sell hub baseload forward against expected output. This leaves shape exposure, because the swap is flat while output is peaky. Synthesis
  • Shape: a fixed-shape swap matching the expected solar profile hedges expected shape, but not weather-driven deviations. Swindle warns that fixed-shape swaps carry more weather risk than they appear to, and that shaping coefficients estimated from a few years of prices carry large errors. Swindle ch.15 (pp.380–385)
  • Basis: FTRs from the node to the hub, or settlement at the hub with the developer keeping basis (and pricing it in). Kirschen & Strbac §5.3.5
  • Firming: pairing the PPA with storage or a firming product converts as-produced output into a block. Kirschen and Strbac list better forecasts, intraday trading and pairing with flexible plant or storage as the remedies for intermittency. Kirschen & Strbac §4.5.3

3. The developer's side

For the solar developer, the PPA is the bankable revenue its lenders need. A CfD-like contract with a creditworthy corporate gives debt capacity close to a utility PPA. The merchant tail after year 10, and any volume shortfall, stay with the project. Yescombe ch.9 (pp.224–225) Lenders size debt on conservative output cases (often P90 or P99) and on the buyer's credit (Course 9). Outside the texts Hub settlement suits the buyer and node settlement the developer. Whoever takes basis risk should hold the FTRs. Kirschen & Strbac §5.3.5

4. Term sheet sketch

  • Volume: as-produced (100% of output), a fixed share, or a firmed shape. Settlement hourly or sub-hourly at a node or hub. Synthesis
  • Price: fixed, possibly escalating, with a floor or collar on negative prices. Treatment of negative-price hours (curtailment rights, zero-settlement). Outside the texts
  • Availability guarantees or minimum annual output with compensation. Allocation of curtailment risk. Swindle ch.15 (pp.399–402)
  • Credit support from the buyer (rating triggers, LCs) for the developer's lenders. Termination payments based on mark-to-market. Synthesis
ALM analogy

A virtual PPA is a long-dated amortising swap whose notional depends on weather and whose floating leg is an imperfect proxy for the buyer's actual cost. Shape is the mismatch between a level swap and a seasonal cash flow, basis is the gap between the hedge index and the liability index, and volume risk is like a lapse-dependent notional with wrong-way correlation. Synthesis

Dated: the corporate PPA market

The course texts predate the corporate PPA boom. Solar capture rates have fallen sharply in high-penetration markets (California, Spain, parts of Texas and Germany). Negative-price hours are frequent, and hybrid solar-plus-storage PPAs and 24/7 matching products have emerged. Outside the texts Check LevelTen or Pexapark price indices, ISO capture-rate data and recent PPA accounting (hedge or derivative treatment) for the buyer.

Going deeper: is the buyer hedged at all?

A corporate buyer's real exposure is its retail electricity bill across many sites and utilities. That bill often doesn't move one-for-one with the farm's node price, and it may even be regulated. The virtual PPA can therefore add risk rather than remove it: it is a financial position in a distant market with weather-driven notional. The case for signing is often about carbon attributes and additionality, with price exposure as a cost to manage, not a hedge. A structurer should quantify the correlation between the PPA settlement and the client's actual bill before calling it a hedge. Swindle ch.15 (p.377) Kirschen & Strbac §5.3.5 Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • Split a solar PPA into price level, shape, basis and volume. Each needs its own view and hedge.
  • Low headline PPA prices can be out of the money after shape and basis discounts.
  • Shape swaps, FTRs and firming hedge parts of the risk. Weather-driven volume risk stays.
  • For the developer, a creditworthy CfD-style PPA makes the project bankable. For the buyer, check whether it hedges anything real.
