Project-financed terminal with throughput contract

Capstone≈ 75 min8 quiz questions3 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Build CFADS from contracted and merchant capacity.
  • Size debt with differentiated cover ratios.
  • Solve the tariff for a target equity return.
  • Design the throughput contract and key lender protections.

Prerequisites

  • Courses 3, 4 and 9.
The brief

Your trading company and an infrastructure fund plan a $500m clean-products import terminal with 36.5m bbl a year of throughput capacity (100 kb/d). Fixed opex is $14m a year, the life is 20 years, and banks offer 15-year debt at 6.5%. Your trading arm will sign a throughput (ship-or-pay) contract for part of the capacity, and the rest will be sold short term to third parties. The fund wants a 12% equity IRR. What tariff and contracted share make this bankable? Synthesis

1. Why project finance, and why a throughput contract

A terminal is a classic midstream project: long-lived, capital-heavy, and earning revenue by selling capacity forward. With creditworthy users on long contracts, lenders accept gearing of 80% or more and DSCRs as low as about 1.2. Clews ch.18 (pp.350–352) A throughput contract commits a user to minimum volumes at a minimum price. It is the pipeline and terminal version of take-or-pay. Yescombe ch.6 (p.108) Ring-fencing in a project company keeps the terminal's debt off both sponsors' balance sheets and lets partners of different financial strength invest together. Yescombe ch.2 (pp.23–24) Gatti ch.1 (pp.25–28)

The conflict to manage: your trading arm is both a sponsor and the main customer. The tariff it pays is the fund's return. Synthesis

2. Size the debt

Lenders size debt from CFADS and cover ratios (Course 9). Here they apply a tight 1.30 cover to contracted cash flow and a cautious 2.00 to the merchant share, reflecting Yescombe's ranking of offtake versus merchant risk. Yescombe ch.12 (pp.322–327) Synthesis

Worked example 1 · base caseChecked by code

80% of capacity contracted at $2.00/bbl. The remaining 20% is expected to run 60% utilised at $1.60, with opex allocated pro rata.

contracted revenue $58.40m, merchant $7.01m → CFADS $51.41m debt (annuity, 15 years, 6.5%) = $361.2m (72.2% gearing), overall DSCR 1.34 equity IRR = 10.5%, below the fund's 12%

The tariff that gets the fund to 12% is $2.060/bbl, and to 15% it is $2.159. Each cent of tariff on 29.2m contracted barrels is worth about $0.3m a year to the fund and costs your trading arm the same. Synthesis

If your trading arm contracts only 50% of capacity at $2.00:

gearing falls to 52.6%, and equity IRR to 3.8%

Contracted share is the main driver of bankability. A sponsor-offtaker that contracts less to keep flexibility pushes the project toward merchant financing terms, as Yescombe's leverage spectrum predicts. Yescombe ch.12 (p.327) Clews ch.18 (p.352)

3. Risks and protections

  • Offtaker credit: lenders look through the throughput contract to your trading company's credit and may want a parent guarantee or LC. Clews ch.18 (p.354)
  • Regulation and access: third-party access rules or competition law can constrain long exclusive contracts. Check them before relying on revenue. Clews ch.18 (p.351)
  • Construction and completion: EPC with LDs plus sponsor completion support. Watch project-on-project dependence, such as port dredging or a connecting pipeline. Clews ch.18 (p.354)
  • Residual value: the asset outlives the contract, so lenders value uncontracted years conservatively. Clews ch.18 (p.351)
  • Cash control: the cascade, a six-month DSRA and a 1.20 lock-up (Course 9, Module 6). Yescombe ch.14 (pp.372–376)

4. Term sheet sketch

  • Throughput contract: 15+ years (covering the debt tenor plus a tail), minimum annual volume, take-or-pay deficiency payments, tariff with inflation indexation, make-up rights. Yescombe ch.6 (p.108) Yescombe ch.12 (p.324)
  • Related-party protections: arm's-length tariff benchmarking, an independent board vote on contract amendments, and step-in or replacement rights if the offtaker defaults. Synthesis
  • Financing: 15-year amortising debt sized at 1.30 contracted and 2.00 merchant, DSRA, lock-up, sweep of merchant upside above a threshold. Synthesis
  • Shareholders' agreement: tariff renegotiation triggers, offtaker's right of first refusal on capacity, exit and transfer restrictions. Outside the texts
ALM analogy

The terminal is a closed book of contracted annuity income (the throughput contract) plus a smaller variable-income block (merchant capacity), funded with long fixed-rate debt and a thin equity layer. Lenders credit the contracted block at a tight solvency margin and the variable block at a wide one, exactly as a regulator applies different capital factors to guaranteed and non-guaranteed business. Synthesis

Dated: market context

Product-terminal economics have changed with the energy transition: longer-term demand uncertainty for road fuels, conversion of terminals to biofuels and ammonia or hydrogen carriers, and private infrastructure funds as the dominant equity buyers. Debt pricing reflects current base rates (SOFR, SONIA, €STR) rather than LIBOR. Outside the texts Check recent terminal financings and storage tariff benchmarks.

Going deeper: the related-party tension

As sponsor and offtaker, your trading company gains from a low tariff on one side and a high equity return on the other. The fund, holding only equity, wants the tariff high. Lenders want a tariff high enough for cover but not so high that the offtaker has an incentive to walk away, which is Yescombe's warning that contracts unfair to one side are vulnerable. Yescombe ch.9 (pp.201–202) Solutions include benchmarking the tariff to third-party prices, splitting returns through shareholder loans rather than the tariff, and giving the fund consent rights over contract changes. Gatti's discussion of sponsor–lender conflicts frames why lenders insist on these. Gatti ch.1 (p.29) Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • Throughput contracts make midstream assets bankable at high gearing and low cover.
  • Size debt with differentiated cover on contracted and merchant cash flow, then solve the tariff for the target IRR.
  • Contracted share drives gearing and returns more than anything else.
  • Manage the sponsor–offtaker conflict with benchmarking, governance and arm's-length terms.
