Refinery supply and offtake intermediation

Capstone≈ 75 min8 quiz questions3 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Map the refiner's working-capital and price exposures.
  • Choose between inventory repo, supply-and-offtake and prepayment structures.
  • Price the intermediation fee from funding, capital, credit and hedging costs.
  • Draft the key commercial and credit terms.

Prerequisites

  • Courses 2, 3 and 4.
The brief

A 150 kb/d independent refinery has seen its bank lines cut after a poor year. It holds about 25 days of crude (from loading through tank) and 12 days of products, and its suppliers want letters of credit it can no longer open. Management asks your trading company to "take over the barrels": supply all crude to the refinery gate and buy all products at the gate, for a fee. Crude is $80/bbl and the product slate averages $95/bbl. Synthesis

1. Understand the refiner's problem

The refiner has two separate problems that look like one. The first is working capital: crude in transit and in tank, and products waiting to be sold, tie up roughly $470m at these prices. This is the same arithmetic as Course 4's funding gap. Trafigura CD ch.11 The second is price exposure on that inventory and on the margin: a refiner is naturally long the crack spread. Edwards ch.2.3 Banks fund traders more readily than weak producers because the loans are secured on the commodity and repaid deal by deal. That is why intermediation exists. Trafigura CD ch.11 Moors & Rutten ch.6

2. Structure options

StructureWhat the trader doesMain risk to the trader
Inventory repoBuys tank stock and resells it to the refiner later at a fixed price plus a funding costRefiner fails to repurchase; liquidation of stock
Supply and offtake (intermediation)Owns crude until it enters the refinery and buys products at the gate; refiner pays on termsRefiner credit on the intermediated value; operating outages
Prepayment for productsPays in advance for future product deliveriesRefiner performance; long tenor

Course 4's repo is the lightest option. The bank or trader owns the stock, needs less capital and has a cleaner claim, but takes on the risks of ownership: environmental liability, licences and possible recharacterisation as a loan. Moors & Rutten ch.5 Supply-and-offtake goes further, because the trader takes over supply logistics and the refiner's price exposure, earning a fee plus trading optionality on crude selection and product placement. Prepayment adds long-dated performance risk (Course 4, Module 4). Moors & Rutten ch.6 Synthesis The rest of this case prices supply-and-offtake.

3. Price the fee

Build the fee from the bottom up: funding cost on the bank-financed share of inventory, a return on the trader's own capital (the unfinanced haircut), expected credit loss on the refiner, hedging costs and operating cost. Synthesis Banks advance against stock at a discount to value, the haircut, which acts as the borrower's equity. Hedged stock gets a higher advance rate. Moors & Rutten ch.5

Worked example 1 · the intermediation feeChecked by code

150 kb/d (54.75m bbl a year), 25 crude days at $80 and 12 product days at $95. Banks advance 85% at 6.5%, the trader's capital earns 15%, refiner credit is PD 2% × LGD 40% on the inventory exposure, hedging costs $0.05/bbl and operations $0.10/bbl.

average inventory = $471m → bank funding $400.4m, trader capital $70.65m per barrel: funding $0.475 + capital $0.194 + expected loss $0.069 + hedging 0.05 + operations 0.10 = $0.888/bbl minimum fee

About half the fee is funding. The fee must therefore float with interest rates and with the price level: the same structure at $110 crude needs $1.163/bbl, and holding 35 crude days instead of 25 needs $1.076. Write the fee as a fixed operating component plus funding indexed to price × days × the benchmark rate. Synthesis

4. Hedge and manage risk

  • Flat price. The trader owns the barrels, so it hedges flat price systematically and manages the differentials (crude grade, product spreads) as its basis book, which is Trafigura's standard practice. Trafigura CD ch.10 The refiner can then choose separately whether to lock its crack. Edwards ch.2.3
  • Liquidity. A price rally creates margin calls on the short hedges before the physical gain is realised, so committed revolvers sized for stress are essential. Trafigura CD ch.11
  • Credit and performance. Exposure peaks when products are bought but not yet paid for. Cash collection through accounts the trader controls, short payment terms and title retention over unpaid barrels limit it. Moors & Rutten ch.6 Synthesis
  • Operations. Refinery outages and turnarounds change crude needs and product availability. Contract tolerances and nomination schedules must flex with them. Downey ch.7 Downey ch.11
  • Collateral reality. If the refiner fails, stock in its tanks must be secured. Lenders estimate forced-sale losses of 30–40%, so title, tank access and independent inspection matter more than paper. Moors & Rutten ch.5
Worked example 2 · the price-level effectChecked by code
at $110 crude / $130 products: average inventory $646.5m (from $471m), fee $1.163/bbl

Higher prices mean more capital at work and more margin-call liquidity. Intermediation deals that look profitable at $60 can strain credit lines at $110, which is exactly when refiners most need them. Trafigura CD ch.11 Synthesis

5. Term sheet sketch

  • Tenor 2–3 years, with renewal. Volumes are the refinery's crude needs within a band, with monthly nominations and an operational tolerance. Downey ch.11
  • Crude priced at the trader's delivered cost, or a formula on the benchmark plus a differential. Products bought on published quotations plus or minus agreed differentials. Long §3.2.1
  • Fee: fixed $/bbl plus funding indexed to price, days and the benchmark rate. Minimum volume or a take-or-pay for the fixed element. Synthesis
  • Title to crude passes at the refinery inlet. Title retention on products until paid. Inventory inspected by an independent party. Moors & Rutten ch.5
  • Termination on refiner default, with the trader's right to sell inventory, net settlement, and step-in on tank access. Synthesis
ALM analogy

Intermediation is a secured financing book priced like a repo desk prices balance sheet: funding cost on the haircut-adjusted asset, a capital charge on the haircut, expected loss and operations. The refiner's crack-spread risk is passed through as a separate hedge, much as a bank might fund a client's bond portfolio while the client decides whether to hedge duration. Synthesis

Dated: market practice

Supply-and-offtake intermediation for US East Coast and European refiners grew in the 2010s, provided by banks and trading houses. Several banks have since exited physical commodities, and the counterparties are now mainly trading houses and some private credit funds. Regulatory treatment of physical holdings and repo capital has changed. Outside the texts Check recent refiner filings (10-Ks often disclose intermediation agreements) for current terms.

Going deeper: the trader's real margin

The fee covers costs. The trader's profit often comes from optionality the fee doesn't price: choosing crude grades and sources (Course 3's differentials and arbitrage), timing purchases inside pricing windows, and placing products in the best market. That is also where conflicts arise, because the refiner's crude slate decisions now affect the trader's book. A good agreement separates the cost-plus intermediation from any value-sharing on crude selection, with transparent benchmarks. Long §3.2.1 Trafigura CD ch.10 Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • Separate the working-capital problem from the price problem. Intermediation solves the first and makes the second explicit.
  • Build the fee from funding, capital, expected loss, hedging and operations. Index the funding part to price, days and rates.
  • Hedge flat price and run basis. Size liquidity for margin calls in rallies.
  • Protect against refiner credit with title, cash control and inspection, not paper alone.
