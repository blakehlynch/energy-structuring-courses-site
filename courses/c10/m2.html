CCGT tolling agreement

Capstone≈ 75 min8 quiz questions3 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Value a monthly spark strip and separate intrinsic from extrinsic value.
  • Apply an operating-constraint haircut and find the break-even capacity payment.
  • Plan the delta and vega hedge.
  • Identify credit, outage and correlation risks.

Prerequisites

  • Courses 5, 6 and 7.
The brief

An infrastructure fund owns a 500 MW combined-cycle plant (7.2 heat rate, $3/MWh VOM) in an energy-only market. Its lenders want revenue certainty for next year, so it offers a one-year financial toll on the on-peak blocks. The buyer pays a fixed monthly capacity payment and receives the spark spread each day it calls the plant. The fund asks $6.00/kW-month. Your desk must decide whether to bid, and at what price. Synthesis

1. What you would own

A toll is a strip of daily spark-spread options on the plant's capacity. You pay a fixed premium (the capacity payment) and receive power minus heat rate × gas minus VOM whenever you exercise. Swindle ch.14 (p.336) Capacity payments for asset hedges limit the buyer's credit exposure to an owner who has pledged the plant to its lenders. Here you are the buyer, so your credit risk on the owner is mainly delivery performance. Swindle ch.9 (pp.189–190) A real plant isn't a frictionless option. Start costs, minimum load and outages cut the value, especially near the money (Course 7, Module 3). Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.9 (pp.412–415)

2. Value the toll

Price each month's on-peak option off the power and gas forwards with Kirk's spread formula, split value into intrinsic and extrinsic, and haircut the extrinsic part for constraints and imperfect exercise. Eydeland and Wolyniec warn that spread-option values overstate what a constrained unit can capture, and that simple, consistent dispatch rules are the realistic benchmark. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.9 (p.406) Outside the texts

Twelve monthly on-peak forwards (power $38–$66, gas $3.90–$4.80), 45% power and 35% gas volatility, correlation 0.8, r = 5%. Extrinsic value is haircut 30% for constraints.

intrinsic $29.4m; total after haircut $29.8m cost at $6.00/kW-month: 500,000 kW × 6 × 12 = $36.0m nominal, $35.0m discounted at r on the same monthly timing as the option values → NPV $−5.2m break-even capacity payment = $5.11/kW-month

An efficient CCGT is mostly intrinsic. Most of its value can be locked with forward spark-spread sales, so you shouldn't pay much for volatility. Counter around $4.65–4.90, keeping a margin for hedging costs and model risk. Swindle ch.14 (p.346) Synthesis

For less efficient units, the haircut and the correlation assumption drive the bid. Swindle found implied toll correlations far above historical ones, so the market is already paying less for that optionality than history suggests. Swindle ch.14 (p.344)

3. Hedge plan

  • Day one: sell the forward spark spread on the deltas. Sell gas-equivalent first if power is thin, then convert into power as liquidity allows, tracking monthly mismatches from strip hedges. Swindle ch.14 (pp.346–348)
  • Don't over-hedge vega with single-commodity options. A toll is not a power call struck at a fixed gas price. Swindle ch.14 (p.347)
  • Exercise day-ahead into the day-ahead market and settle deviations in real time. Your dispatch rule should match what your valuation assumed. Kirschen & Strbac §3.3.2 Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.9 (p.406)
  • Location: if the toll settles at the plant's node but your hedges are at the hub, you carry locational spread. Hedge it with FTRs or settle the toll at the hub. Kirschen & Strbac §5.3.5

4. Risks you keep

  • Outages: a forced outage during a spike leaves you short power you sold forward. Negotiate availability guarantees, with capacity-payment reductions or liquidated damages. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.9 (p.388) Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.9 (p.425)
  • Correlation and skew: marked rather than traded, so value them conservatively. Swindle ch.14 (pp.354–355)
  • Owner credit: if the owner defaults, its lenders may step in. Make sure the toll survives enforcement through a direct agreement with the lenders. Outside the texts

5. Term sheet sketch

  • 500 MW, on-peak blocks, 12 months. Day-ahead exercise by a set deadline. Heat rate 7.2 with a VOM adder of $3/MWh. Swindle ch.14 (p.336)
  • Capacity payment per kW-month, reduced for availability below a guaranteed level, with outage LDs. Synthesis
  • Settlement location (hub or node), fuel and power indices, and start-cost and minimum-run provisions or limits on starts. Synthesis
  • Credit support: buyer pays monthly (so little exposure to the buyer), owner's performance backed by a parent guarantee or LC, and lender direct agreement. Swindle ch.9 (p.190)
ALM analogy

Buying a toll is buying a portfolio of exchange options for a level premium, much like buying a strip of caps paid annually. Most of an in-the-money cap's value is intrinsic and hedgeable with swaps, and only the time value depends on volatility models. Bid for the hedgeable part, and be stingy with what you pay for the model-dependent part. Synthesis

Dated: market structure

Toll markets have changed with renewables. Shoulder-hour spreads are now more volatile, battery storage tolls compete for capacity, and many markets have scarcity adders and capacity markets that change the plant's revenue stack. ISO rules on day-ahead offers and start costs differ. Outside the texts Check current forward spark spreads, ISO manuals and recent broker toll prints.

Going deeper: who should own the toll?

The fund sells the toll because its lenders want contracted revenue (Course 9). You buy it because your desk can hedge, dispatch and absorb the residual risks more cheaply. The deal makes sense if your cost of holding constraint, outage and correlation risk is below the fund's, which is Yescombe's allocation principle applied to a trading desk. If the fund's lenders count your capacity payments as contracted revenue for its debt sizing, your credit rating becomes part of the fund's financing, and that is worth something in negotiation. Yescombe ch.9 (p.199) Yescombe ch.9 (pp.224–225) Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • Value the toll as a spread strip, then haircut the extrinsic value for constraints and exercise.
  • Efficient units are mostly intrinsic: bid off the hedgeable value and pay little for volatility.
  • Hedge spark deltas, with gas first if power is illiquid, and avoid single-commodity vega hedges.
  • Protect against outages, location basis and owner default in the contract.
