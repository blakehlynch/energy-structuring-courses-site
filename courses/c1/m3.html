Demand, inventories and the forward curve

Foundation≈ 70 min9 quiz questions11 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Explain the income, price, tax and seasonal drivers of demand.
  • Distinguish headline from usable inventory cover.
  • Compute forward yields and test a curve against full carry.
  • Value a cash-and-carry trade onshore and on a tanker.
  • Explain how local storage constraints move benchmark spreads.

Prerequisites

  • Modules 1–2.
  • Continuous compounding.

What drives demand

  • Income. Oil demand tracks economic activity. McNally cites income elasticities around 1.0 overall, about 0.55 for advanced economies and about 1.1 for fast-growing ones. Kaiser notes that since 1980 about half the variation in oil consumption is "explained" by world GDP. McNally ch.3 Kaiser §4.6.1 Long §1.2.2
  • Price, in the short run, barely matters, because there are few substitutes in transport and vehicles take years to replace. Price matters much more over the long run. Module 4 quantifies this. McNally ch.3 Long §1.2.2
  • Taxes and subsidies separate consumer prices from crude. Long compares the US, which uses roughly twice as much oil per head, with high-tax European countries. Long §1.2.2 McNally ch.3
  • Season. Long cites a 3–4 mb/d swing between winter peak and summer trough in world demand. Northern winters lift heating oil, kerosene and propane. Summers lift gasoline and jet. Long §1.2.2 Downey ch.13 Swindle ch.3

Downey's seasonality chapter is a checklist of what traders watch: heating and cooling degree days, the US driving season from Memorial Day to Labor Day, hurricane season (June–November, peaking in September), Rhine water levels for inland Europe, Baltic ice, Chinese New Year and flu seasons. Downey ch.13 The jet differential to heating oil (the "regrade") widens in winter because jet and heating oil compete for the same middle distillate molecules. Downey ch.13

Inventories: the market's shock absorber

Inventories are held at every link of the chain: crude at fields, terminals and refineries, products in tanks and pipelines, plus strategic government stocks. McNally argues that storage is the one part of the system that can dampen volatility, by buying and storing in gluts and releasing in shortages. Trafigura describes traders doing exactly this. McNally ch.3 Trafigura CD ch.3 Long §1.2.1

  • Minimum operating levels. Pipelines, tank bottoms and refinery systems need oil just to work. Long cites an Exxon study that counted most US company stocks as minimum operating inventory. Downey shows that headline "days of cover" overstates usable supply: only the stock above the operating minimum is really available. Long §1.2.2 Downey ch.12
  • Strategic stocks. IEA members commit to hold at least 90 days of net imports. The US Strategic Petroleum Reserve stores crude in salt caverns, with Downey citing capacity of about 727 MMbbl and a maximum drawdown of about 4.4 mb/d. China runs a dual government-and-industry system. Downey ch.5, ch.12
  • Floating storage. When land tanks fill, tankers become storage, at a price. Swindle cites roughly fifty VLCCs holding over 100 MMbbl in the 2008–2009 contango. Yergin describes the world nearly running out of storage in April 2020. Swindle ch.2 Yergin ch.36 Downey ch.12
Worked example 1 · usable days of coverChecked by code

A region holds 440 MMbbl of commercial crude, of which about 300 MMbbl is minimum operating inventory. Refineries run 16 mb/d.

headline cover = 440 / 16 = 27.5 days usable cover = (440 − 300) / 16 = 8.75 days

The market is a continuous flow with a thin buffer, which is why weekly stock changes move prices. Downey makes the same point with US numbers. Downey ch.12

The forward curve and the theory of storage

Most commodity hedging happens in forward markets, because most participants cannot store the physical. For energy, forward prices cannot be derived from spot plus carrying costs the way bond or equity forwards can; the forward curve itself is the primary data. Swindle ch.2 A curve is in contango when forwards rise with tenor and in backwardation when they fall. Swindle ch.2 Schofield ch.5 Downey ch.18

BackwardationContango
Physical stateScarcity, low inventoriesGlut, high inventories
Price behaviourVolatile, rising, "fear premium"Calmer, weak, "complacency discount"
IncentiveDraw stocks, deliver nowBuy now, store, sell forward

Characterisation from Schofield's ch.5 and Swindle's ch.2. Synthesis

Swindle treats the curve like a yield curve. The annualised forward yield between tenors \(T\) and \(T+S\) is

\[ y(t,T,T+S) = \frac{1}{S}\ln\frac{F(t,T+S)}{F(t,T)}, \]

positive in contango and negative in backwardation. Swindle ch.2 For an asset you can always buy and store, arbitrage caps the forward at full carry, but a consumption commodity can sit far below it:

\[ F(t,T) \le F(t,t)\,e^{(r+q)(T-t)}, \qquad F(t,T) = F(t,t)\,e^{(r+q-\eta)(T-t)}. \]

Here \(r\) is the funding rate and \(q\) the storage cost. The convenience yield \(\eta\) is the plug that makes the equality hold: the benefit of holding the physical now. Swindle ch.2 Hull ch.5 Swindle points out that markets only reveal \(q-\eta\) together, that storage costs themselves respond to the curve (storage leases get dearer when spreads widen), and that OECD data show low inventories alongside negative forward yields (backwardation) and high inventories alongside contango. He also shows the relationship is noisy. Swindle ch.2

ALM analogy

Swindle draws the analogy himself. Convenience yield is like a bond trading "special" in repo: when everyone needs the physical, lending it out earns a negative effective rate. The forward yield curve is the commodity term structure, and the inventory–yield relationship is a liquidity premium that rises as buffers fall. Swindle ch.2

Worked example 2 · is this contango beyond full carry?Checked by code

The front month is $78.00 and the second month $78.90. Funding costs 5%. Storage costs $0.50/bbl/month.

forward yield = 12 × ln(78.90/78.00) = 13.77% a year storage yield = 0.50 × 12 / 78 = 7.69% full-carry yield = 5% + 7.69% = 12.69%

The spread pays more than it costs to carry. Anyone with tank space can buy prompt, store and sell the second month at a profit. That buying lifts the prompt and the selling caps the deferred until the spread falls back to full carry. In practice storage costs rise first, because owners of tank space charge what the spread allows. Swindle ch.2

Worked example 3 · cash-and-carry, onshore versus floatingChecked by code

Spot is $70.00 and the 6-month forward $75.40. Onshore tank storage costs $0.45/bbl/month, insurance $0.03/bbl/month, financing 5.5% a year, and losses 0.2%. A VLCC on time charter costs $60,000 a day for 2 MMbbl.

carry earned (F − S) = 5.40 financing 70 × 5.5% × 6/12 = 1.925 storage + insurance (0.48 × 6) = 2.88 losses 0.2% × 70 = 0.14 onshore profit = 5.40 − 4.945 = 0.455 $/bbl floating storage cost = 60,000 × 30 / 2,000,000 = 0.90 $/bbl/month → profit −2.245 $/bbl

The same spread is profitable in a cheap onshore tank and loss-making on a ship. That is why the contango has to widen a lot before tankers fill up, as in early 2009 and 2020. Trafigura describes exactly this cash-and-carry trade. Trafigura CD ch.4, ch.7 Swindle ch.2 Freight rates (Course 3) set the marginal storage cost in a deep glut.

Location matters: the Cushing lesson

Storage is local. Swindle shows that rising inventories in the US Midwest (PADD 2) after 2010, driven by growing Canadian imports, limited local demand and too little pipeline capacity out, pushed WTI (delivered at Cushing, Oklahoma) far below Brent and other crudes. The Brent–WTI spread tracked PADD 2 stocks closely. Brent became the dominant benchmark and the EIA switched its forecasts to Brent. Swindle ch.3 Kaiser Box 4.2 Yergin's account of April 2020, when the expiring WTI contract settled below zero as Cushing storage filled, is the extreme case of the same mechanism. Yergin ch.36 Synthesis

Dated: stocks and storage facts

SPR capacity, inventory levels and storage practices quoted by Downey (2009) and Long (around 2000) have changed. The US SPR was partly sold down after 2015 and drawn heavily in 2022, and Cushing and Gulf Coast storage and export capacity have expanded. Downey ch.12 Long §1.2.2 Outside the texts For current figures use the EIA Weekly Petroleum Status Report, the IEA Oil Market Report stock tables, and the US DOE's SPR inventory page.

Going deeper: why backwardation is "normal" for oil

Schofield argues that oil and base metals backwardate more often than gold does, because oil is costly to store relative to its value, so little sits above ground. A demand surge cannot be met from stocks and spot prices jump relative to forwards. Schofield ch.5 Producers want to sell forward to lock in revenue and consumers buy less far out. Hedging-pressure explanations of risk premia in the curve, which Swindle surveys among inventory models, are part of Course 6. Swindle ch.2 Practically, a backwardated curve rewards being long prompt physical. For a producer it means forward sales lock in prices below spot. For a consumer it means hedging forward looks "cheap". Neither is a forecast; it is the price of carry. Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • Demand follows income closely and price only weakly in the short run, with strong seasonal patterns by product.
  • Inventories absorb shocks, but usable cover is much smaller than headline cover.
  • Curve shape signals inventory: contango means plenty and pays storage; backwardation means scarcity and draws stocks. Convenience yield is the plug that reconciles the two.
  • Storage is local. Benchmark spreads (WTI–Brent) and even negative prices follow local tank space.
