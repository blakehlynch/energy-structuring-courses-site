Three regimes in 160 years

McNally's history has one thread. Because oil prices are naturally prone to booms and busts (Module 4), industry and governments have repeatedly tried to manage supply, and prices have been calm only while someone held both the power and the will to swing output. McNally Introduction, Epilogue Kaiser's data make the point numerically. The average year-to-year change in crude price was about 6.9% from 1862 to 1934, about 3.5% from 1934 to 1970 under Texas Railroad Commission prorationing, and about 13.7% from 1970 to 2017. Kaiser §4.4.1

How the managed era worked, and why it ended

The Oklahoma and Texas episodes of 1927–1933 (giant field discoveries, prices collapsing to cents, martial law to shut wells) turned producers from opponents of regulation into its advocates. They also set the template OPEC later copied: allocate production by quota and hold spare capacity off the market. McNally ch.4 Kaiser §4.1 McNally notes that the Texas-plus-Seven-Sisters system controlled about 89% of world reserves, 95% of production and 57% of refining. Saudi Arabia today has a far smaller share: about 16% of reserves, 13% of production and 3% of refining. McNally Epilogue Market power, more than intent, is what made price management stick.

The OPEC era began with the 1973 embargo and a fourfold price rise. Kaiser cites a move from about $2.9 to $11.5/bbl within months. A second shock followed in 1979–80. Kaiser §4.1 Yergin ch.7 McNally ch.6 McNally argues OPEC truly acted as swing producer only from 1982 to 1985, and even then Saudi Arabia did all the swinging. When Riyadh gave up the role, prices collapsed in 1986. McNally ch.7, Epilogue Long §1.2.2 Downey adds that quotas linked to reserves led members to inflate reported reserves in the 1980s, and that OPEC never had a robust way to verify compliance; tanker tracking became the market's check. Downey ch.5

Why cartels leak

Each member gains by producing a little over quota while others hold back. If everyone does it, the price collapses. McNally cites research finding OPEC produced above its official quotas 96% of the time between 1982 and 2009. McNally Epilogue Downey ch.5

Worked example 1 · the cheater's arithmeticChecked by code

Ten identical members each have a quota of 3 mb/d, with the price at $70. Each extra 1 mb/d of supply knocks $6 off the price (illustrative). One member quietly adds 0.5 mb/d.

honest revenue = 3.0 × $70 = $210m/day cheater's revenue = 3.5 × ($70 − 6 × 0.5) = 3.5 × 67 = $234.5m/day (gain $24.5m/day) if all ten cheat : price = 70 − 6 × 5 = $40 → each earns 3.5 × 40 = $140m/day

This is a textbook prisoner's dilemma: individually rational cheating destroys the group's revenue. Cartels hold together only with a dominant member willing to punish. Downey notes Saudi Arabia's implied threat to flood the market. Downey ch.5 Synthesis

Worked example 2 · should a swing producer cut alone?Checked by code

The market is 100 mb/d at $60 with a combined short-run elasticity of 0.10. A producer pumping 10 mb/d considers cutting 1 mb/d alone.

price multiple = (100/99)^(1/0.10) = 1.1057 revenue before = 10 × 60 = $600m/day revenue after = 9 × 60 × 1.1057 = $597.1m/day → slightly worse off same 1 mb/d cut by a producer of 30 mb/d: $1,800m → $1,924.0m/day → better off

Whether cutting pays depends on market share. A small-share producer that cuts alone mostly hands revenue to everyone else. McNally makes this argument about why Saudi Arabia will cut "with others, not alone", and Yergin recounts the November 2014 decision not to cut without non-OPEC participation. McNally Epilogue Yergin ch.34 (Elasticities here are illustrative; the real calculation also depends on how long the cut lasts and on how others respond.)

2004 to 2020: the end of management, and OPEC+

  • 2004–2008: demand growth led by China and the other BRICs, the "commodity supercycle", absorbed spare capacity. In 2005 OPEC effectively let members produce at will, echoing the Texas Railroad Commission's 1971 move. Prices peaked near $147–148 in 2008 before collapsing with the financial crisis. Downey ch.5 Yergin ch.7 McNally ch.9
  • 2014–2016: shale supply plus slowing demand. In November 2014 OPEC chose to let the market rebalance, and prices fell from about $105 in mid-2014 to about $30 by early 2016. Roughly a hundred US producers went bankrupt; the rest cut costs and became more resilient than expected. McNally ch.3 Yergin ch.34
  • Late 2016: OPEC and eleven non-OPEC producers led by Russia agreed coordinated cuts, which became "OPEC-Plus". Yergin argues the old "OPEC versus non-OPEC" frame gave way to a "Big Three": the US, Russia and Saudi Arabia. Yergin ch.7, ch.34
  • 2020: OPEC+ split in March as the pandemic hit and Saudi Arabia raised output sharply. Storage filled, and in April the expiring WTI futures contract settled below zero. A US-brokered OPEC+ deal cut 9.7 mb/d, the largest cut ever. Yergin ch.36
Reconciling McNally and Yergin

McNally, writing in 2016, doubted OPEC would regain control and saw only ad hoc emergency cuts after busts. McNally Epilogue Yergin, writing in 2020, describes OPEC+ cuts in 2016 and 2020. Yergin ch.34, ch.36 The two accounts are consistent: both OPEC+ actions were reactive cuts after price collapses, which is exactly the pattern McNally predicted, not the sustained, proactive swing-producer management of the Texas era.

Boom-bust investment cycle

Lags plus sticky elasticities produce endogenous cycles; a swing producer's buffer damps them

This is a stylised model. Demand grows each year; price clears against capacity with a short-run elasticity; projects sanctioned on today's price arrive after a lag; capacity declines every year. A swing producer, if present, holds back up to twice its normal spare to keep price at the reference level. All parameters are illustrative.

ALM analogy

This is the insurance underwriting cycle. High prices (hard market) attract capacity that arrives with a lag (capital, new entrants); the excess capacity then drives prices down (soft market) and capacity exits. A large, disciplined player with surplus capital can damp the cycle, but only if it is willing to give up market share when the market softens. Saudi Arabia faced exactly that dilemma.

Dated: OPEC+ and after 2020

McNally's narrative ends around 2016 and Yergin's in mid-2020. OPEC+ cooperation, voluntary cut structures, membership (several members have left OPEC since 2019) and the market's response after 2020 lie outside both books. Outside the texts Check OPEC's press releases and Monthly Oil Market Report, the IEA OMR, and OIES commentary for the current state of OPEC+ policy.

Going deeper: why lags create cycles

In the simulator, investment decided at time \(t\) on price \(P_t\) arrives at \(t+L\). A low price today means too little capacity \(L\) years later, then a high price, then over-investment, and so on. The cycle's period is roughly twice the lag plus adjustment time. Its amplitude grows with the investment response and shrinks with elasticity. McNally's verbal version: busts sow the seeds of the next boom by cancelling multi-year investment, and booms sow the next bust. McNally ch.3, Epilogue Kaiser §4.4.3 Spare capacity held by a swing producer, or a large storage buffer, breaks the feedback by absorbing imbalances before they reach price. Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • Price stability has come only from supply management backed by market power: Standard Oil, then the Texas Railroad Commission with the Seven Sisters, then (briefly) Saudi Arabia.
  • Cartels leak: each member gains by cheating, and the group loses if all cheat.
  • Since 2008 the market has been largely unmanaged, with reactive OPEC+ cuts after busts and shale as a fast but decentralised margin.
  • Investment lags plus sticky elasticities generate endogenous boom-bust cycles. Buffers damp them.
