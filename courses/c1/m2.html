Upstream supply: reserves, fiscal terms, decline and cost

Foundation≈ 70 min9 quiz questions11 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Interpret P90/P50/P10 reserves and explain why lenders and sponsors use different cases.
  • Compute a production-sharing contract split and explain how it evolves over field life.
  • Contrast short-cycle and long-cycle supply.
  • Use a decline curve to estimate the drilling needed to hold production flat.
  • Find the marginal barrel on a supply cost stack and distinguish operating from full-cycle cost.

Prerequisites

  • Module 1.

The field life cycle

Every barrel passes through exploration, appraisal, development, production and abandonment. Clews §6.2 Edwards ch.1.3 Downey ch.6

  • Exploration is a lottery with expensive tickets. Clews puts the success rate at roughly one discovery in five wells, with a single exploration well able to cost well over $20 million. For that reason exploration is equity-funded, almost never project-financed. Clews §6.2.1
  • Development sets the shape of the production profile: number of wells, plateau rate, facility size. A field development plan (FDP) is usually needed for government approval. Clews §6.2.2
  • Production follows a decline curve once the plateau ends. Operators fight decline with water injection, infill drilling and enhanced recovery. Downey warns that under-investment, for example badly managed water injection, can permanently reduce what a field ever recovers. Clews §6.2.2 Downey ch.5
  • Abandonment brings decommissioning obligations that lenders and buyers must provide for. Clews §6.2.2

Reserves: a probability distribution, not a number

Reserves are the part of the resource judged technically and commercially recoverable from known reservoirs under current prices and rules. They are reported by confidence level. Clews §6.3.1 Edwards ch.1.3 Downey ch.14

  • Proved (1P, P90): at least 90% probability that recovery equals or exceeds the estimate.
  • Proved + probable (2P, P50) and proved + probable + possible (3P, P10): progressively less certain.
  • Reserves move with price: higher prices make more barrels commercial. Accounting and disclosure rules (e.g. the SEC's) shape what companies book. Downey describes OPEC's reported reserves jumping in the 1980s when quotas were linked to reserves, and treats those figures as unreliable. Edwards ch.1.3 Downey ch.5, ch.14
Worked example 1 · reading a reserves report & portfolio sub-additivityChecked by code

An independent engineer's report gives P90 = 40 MMbbl, P50 = 110 MMbbl and P10 = 230 MMbbl for a single field. Now suppose a multi-field RBL portfolio holds 4 statistically independent fields, each with P90 = 40 MMbbl and P50 = 110 MMbbl (Gaussian approximation: \(\sigma = (P_{50}-P_{90})/1.2816\) = 54.6 MMbbl):

single field: proved (1P) = 40 MMbbl, probable (2P − 1P) = 70 MMbbl, possible (3P − 2P) = 120 MMbbl 4-field sum of field 1Ps = 4 × 40 = 160 MMbbl (portfolio 2P = 440 MMbbl) portfolio P90 = 440 − 1.2816 × √4 × 70 / 1.2816 = 300 MMbbl → diversification uplift +87.5%

Because independent geological tails do not all fail simultaneously, the portfolio \(P_{90}\) exceeds the sum of field-by-field \(P_{90}\)s by 140 MMbbl. Reporting rules (SEC, and SPE-PRMS beyond the field level) require arithmetic summation, so booked 1P ignores this diversification. Lenders are also cautious about crediting it, because fields often share price, fiscal and operator risks that are not independent; some multi-field RBLs and acquirers give partial credit. The Gaussian approximation is itself rough: this field's P10 (230) sits further above P50 than its P90 sits below, so the true distribution is right-skewed and the uplift here is illustrative. Clews §6.3.1 Synthesis Outside the texts

ALM analogy

P50 against P90 is the same distinction as best-estimate reserves against a high-confidence provision or capital level, and portfolio reserve uplift is the exact counterpart of diversification credit across independent insurance lines under Solvency II SCR. Equity investors price the median; creditors want the tail.

Who gets the barrel: fiscal regimes

Outside the US and Canada, subsurface minerals almost always belong to the state. Governments grant access in three main ways. Clews §6.4.1 Edwards ch.1.3

  1. Concession or licence (tax and royalty). The company owns the production and pays royalty and taxes. In the US, private landowners often take a royalty around one-eighth. Edwards ch.1.3
  2. Production sharing contract (PSC). The state keeps ownership. The contractor funds the work and recovers its costs from "cost oil", subject to a cap. The remaining "profit oil" is split, and royalty and tax may also apply. Modern PSCs rarely use a flat profit-oil split: instead, the contractor's profit-oil share slides down as a function of daily output tiers or an R-factor (\(R\) = cumulative contractor receipts ÷ cumulative capital and operating outlays), making the contractor's entitlement concave in crude prices. Clews describes stabilisation clauses and state ownership of assets as key issues for lenders. Clews §6.4.1 Synthesis
  3. Service contract. The contractor is paid a fee and takes no ownership; the state keeps most of the risk and reward. Edwards ch.1.3
Worked example 2 · one year of a production sharing contract & R-factor step-downChecked by code

Per barrel produced: revenue $80. Royalty 10% of gross. Operating cost $15. Unrecovered development capex brought forward $120. Cost recovery capped at 60% of gross revenue. Under a sliding-scale R-factor schedule, the contractor gets 40% of profit oil while \(R < 1.0\) (pre-payout), stepping down to 25% for \(1.0 \le R < 1.5\), and 15% once \(R \ge 1.5\), paying 30% income tax on its profit oil.

royalty = 10% × 80 = 8.00 cost-oil cap = 60% × 80 = 48.00 cost oil = min(15 + 120, 48) = 48.00 → capex recovered 33.00, carried forward 87.00 profit oil = 80 − 8 − 48 = 24.00 contractor profit oil = 40% × 24 = 9.60 (state 14.40) contractor tax = 30% × 9.60 = 2.88 contractor take (R<1.0) = 48 + 9.60 − 2.88 = 54.72 → cash after opex 39.72 government take (R<1.0) = 8 + 14.40 + 2.88 = 25.28 (31.6% of gross) post-payout (R=1.60, capex=0): cost oil = 15.00, profit oil = 57.00, contractor 15% share → take $20.98 (gov 73.8%)

Early in field life (\(R < 1\)) the cost-recovery cap front-loads the contractor's take and the state's share is 31.6%. Once capex is recovered and \(R\) crosses 1.5, cost oil shrinks to $15 opex and the R-factor cuts the contractor's profit-oil share from 40% to 15%, lifting government take to 73.8%. That fiscal concavity is why a reserve-based loan or prepayment secured on a PSC must model entitlement barrels under the full R-factor waterfall rather than assuming a constant working-interest share. Clews §6.4.1 Synthesis

Short cycle and long cycle

Yergin's vocabulary is now standard. Long-cycle supply, such as deepwater, oil sands or LNG, takes five to ten years and hundreds of millions to billions of dollars per project, then produces for decades. Short-cycle supply, chiefly US shale (tight oil), can go from decision to production in months: a well drilled in days at a cost Yergin reports falling from about $15 million to about $7 million. Yergin ch.7, ch.34 Edwards ch.1.3 The catch is decline. Shale wells lose much of their output in the first year, so producers must keep drilling just to stand still. Yergin calls it a manufacturing business. Yergin ch.7

Mature conventional fields decline too, though more slowly. McNally cites an IEA estimate of about 9% a year for mature fields without investment, and Downey gives typical field depletion of 2–8% a year. McNally Epilogue Downey ch.5 Global supply is therefore a race between decline and new capacity. That is why investment cuts after a price collapse sow the next shortage (Module 5).

Decline curves

Engineers summarise production declines with Arps curves. The rate \(q\) at time \(t\) is

\[ q(t) = \frac{q_0}{(1 + bDt)^{1/b}} \quad (0 < b \le 1), \qquad q(t) = q_0 e^{-Dt} \quad (b = 0) \]

where \(D\) is the initial decline rate and \(b\) controls how fast the decline itself slows. Downey lists decline-curve analysis among the standard reserve-estimation methods. Downey ch.14 The Arps form and the parameter values used below are standard petroleum-engineering practice rather than taken from the course texts. Outside the texts

Worked example 3 · the shale treadmillChecked by code

An illustrative shale type curve: initial rate 600 b/d, hyperbolic decline with \(D = 1.4\), \(b = 1.0\). What is the first-year decline, and how many wells a year hold a basin flat at 1 mb/d (evaluating each annual cohort at its mid-year age \(k+0.5\) as a midpoint quadrature approximation to continuous spudding)?

rate after 1 year = 600 / (1 + 1.4) = 250 b/d → first-year decline 58.3% wells/year to hold 1 mb/d flat (program mature for 20 yrs) = 707 at ≈ $7m per well (Yergin's figure) = $4.95 billion a year just to stand still

Stop drilling and production falls fast, which is what makes shale "short cycle" in both directions. Restart drilling and production recovers within months. McNally cautions that drilled-but-uncompleted wells (DUCs) behave more like a one-off inventory than lasting spare capacity. McNally Epilogue Yergin ch.7, ch.34

ALM analogy

A shale basin is a run-off block. Each year's wells are a new cohort that "lapses" fast, so production stays flat only if new business replaces the run-off. Holding volume flat needs a steady flow of new wells, and the capital needed to stand still is the analogue of the new business strain needed to keep a closed book's reserves level.

Costs and the supply curve

Production costs vary enormously. Kaiser cites Middle East fields at roughly $5–10/bbl, with deepwater, harsh-environment and oil-sands projects two to five times as expensive. Kaiser §4.6.3 Downey cautions that the "under $5–10" figure for OPEC finding and development cost is outdated, and that the price a producing state "needs" includes fiscal and social spending. Yergin reports that in early 2020 Russia's budget was planned on $42 oil, while the IMF put the Saudi budget-balancing price at $80 or higher. Downey ch.5 Yergin ch.36 Two different costs matter:

  • Operating (cash) cost: the short-run shut-in floor. McNally gives roughly $5 to perhaps $30/bbl, and notes oil often keeps flowing even below this. McNally ch.3
  • Full-cycle (breakeven) cost, including capital: what sets new investment, and therefore long-run supply. Kaiser §4.6.3 Clews §6.3

Shale moved the middle of the curve down after 2014: Yergin recounts Gulf officials expecting shale to fold at $70, which it did not. Kaiser §4.6.3 Yergin ch.34 Synthesis

Going deeper: why price falls don't cut supply quickly

Put the pieces together. Sunk costs mean producers keep running as long as price covers operating cost. Debt service and royalty obligations push smaller and state producers to produce even more when prices fall. Projects sanctioned at high prices keep arriving for years. McNally gives the examples of Gulf of Mexico platforms sanctioned at $100 coming on stream into the 2015–2016 glut, Russia's output hitting a post-Soviet record in 2016, and UK North Sea output rising in 2015. McNally ch.3 Long §1.2.2 Synthesis In option terms, an operating field is a strip of options to produce, struck at operating cost and deep in the money at almost any price. Supply only bends when price crosses that strike or when new investment stops, which shows up as missing supply years later.

Key takeaways

  • Reserves are a distribution (P90/P50/P10). Lenders lend on the tail and equity prices the median.
  • Fiscal regimes (royalty and tax, PSC, service) decide who owns the barrel and how the take shifts over field life.
  • Short-cycle shale responds within months but must keep drilling to stand still. Long-cycle supply responds only after years.
  • The supply curve is a cost stack: operating cost sets the short-run floor and full-cycle cost of the marginal barrel sets the long-run price.
