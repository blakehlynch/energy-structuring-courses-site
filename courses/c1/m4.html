Price formation: elasticities, shocks and spare capacity

Learning objectives

  • State McNally's short- and long-run elasticities and explain why they are small.
  • Estimate the price impact of a net shortfall with linear and constant-elasticity formulas.
  • Net a disruption against spare capacity and stock releases.
  • Explain McNally's price corridor and Kaiser's caution on forecasting.
  • Use the call on OPEC to link balances, stocks and prices.

Sticky supply, sticky demand

McNally sums up the core of oil price behaviour in one idea: both supply and demand barely respond to price in the short run, so small imbalances need large price moves to clear. McNally ch.3 Long and Kaiser make the same point from the trading and refining sides. Long §1.2.3 Kaiser §4.5

Elasticity (from McNally's survey)Short runLong run
Demand with respect to priceabout −0.06about −0.2 to −0.3
Supply with respect to priceabout 0.04about 0.35
Demand with respect to income (GDP)about 1.0 (0.55 advanced, 1.1 fast-growing economies)

McNally stresses these are hard to pin down and that US demand appears to have become less price-elastic between 1980 and 2007. McNally ch.3

Why demand is sticky: oil is a necessity, has few substitutes in transport, and fuel is a small cost next to the vehicle that burns it. Large price changes that consumers see as permanent do change behaviour over years, as with the 1970s shift of power and heating away from oil and the 1990s SUV boom on cheap gasoline. McNally ch.3 Why supply is sticky: sunk, single-purpose capital, low operating costs and long lead times (Module 2). McNally ch.3 Long §1.2.3

How big a price move does a shortfall need?

If a net shortfall of fraction \(s\) of supply must be cleared by price alone, a first-order approximation with supply elasticity \(\varepsilon_S\) and demand elasticity \(\varepsilon_D < 0\) is

\[ \frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx \frac{s}{\varepsilon_S - \varepsilon_D}. \]

McNally's own example uses demand elasticity alone: a 5% loss with \(\varepsilon_D = -0.06\) needs a price rise of roughly 85%. McNally ch.3 For large shocks the linear approximation understates the move. With constant-elasticity curves,

\[ \frac{P_1}{P_0} = \left(\frac{1}{1-s}\right)^{1/(\varepsilon_S - \varepsilon_D)}, \]

which grows faster than linearly. Both formulas follow from standard elasticity algebra applied to McNally's numbers; the derivation is ours rather than the book's. Synthesis

Worked example 1 · a 4% net shortfallChecked by code
short run, εS = 0.04, εD = −0.06 : linear 4%/0.10 = 40.0% constant-elasticity 50.4% demand response only (εS = 0) : 4%/0.06 = 66.7% long run, εS = 0.35, εD = −0.25 : 4%/0.60 = 6.7%

The same physical shortfall moves price six to eight times more in the short run than in the long run. Spikes are therefore sharp and short, and high prices sustained for years destroy demand and call forth supply. McNally adds that a recession is the "brutal" fast route to lower demand. McNally ch.3

ALM analogy

Low elasticity is low market depth. Forcing a 4% quantity adjustment through a market with an elasticity of 0.1 is like liquidating a large position in an illiquid market: price impact is roughly quantity divided by depth. The non-linear version behaves like convexity, with the last barrels costing the most. Buffers such as spare capacity and stocks act like a liquidity reserve that lets you meet outflows without forced selling.

The three buffers: spare capacity, strategic stocks, commercial stocks

  • Spare capacity is installed production capacity held idle that can come online quickly, mostly in Saudi Arabia, with some in the UAE and Kuwait. Yergin notes it is why Saudi Arabia is called the market's "central bank". McNally cites about 5% of world production as a widely used comfort threshold and argues that 1–2 mb/d is too thin to cap spikes. Yergin ch.8 McNally Epilogue Kaiser §4.6.5 Kaiser notes theory predicts that low spare capacity means high and volatile prices, but the observed relationship "is sometimes true and sometimes not". Kaiser §4.6.5
  • Strategic stocks (SPR and other IEA holdings) are released by policy decision. Downey records IEA-coordinated actions such as 60 MMbbl after Hurricane Katrina in 2005, and notes that a release only offers oil to the market, which does not have to buy it. Downey ch.5, ch.12
  • Commercial stocks above minimum operating levels, drawn when backwardation makes holding uneconomic (Module 3).
Worked example 2 · a 6 mb/d disruption with buffersChecked by code

The world consumes 102 mb/d. A conflict removes 6 mb/d. Spare capacity is 3.2 mb/d, of which 80% can arrive in time. Strategic stocks can deliver 1.5 mb/d and commercial stocks 0.5 mb/d. Use short-run elasticities.

spare capacity used = 0.8 × 3.2 = 2.56 mb/d net shortfall = 6 − 2.56 − 1.5 − 0.5 = 1.44 mb/d (1.41% of supply) price impact ≈ 1.41% / 0.10 = 14.1% (constant-elasticity 15.3%) without any buffers ≈ 5.88% / 0.10 = 58.8%

Buffers turn a crisis-scale move into a manageable one. This is the quantitative case for holding spare capacity and strategic stocks, and the reason markets track spare capacity so closely. The usable fraction of spare capacity and the timing of stock draws are judgement calls; a desk should run several cases.

A price corridor between pain points

McNally closes with a range rather than a forecast. In an unmanaged market, prices oscillate between a floor near shut-in cost (well below $30 in his 2016 framing) and a ceiling where expenditure causes demand destruction or recession (well above $100). The width of the swing depends on how large the imbalance is and whether anyone manages supply. McNally Epilogue Kaiser makes a related point about price formation: market factors (macro demand, OPEC, spare capacity, disruptions, technology) dominate crude-specific factors and cannot be reliably forecast. Kaiser §4.4

Dated: elasticities, buffers and thresholds

Elasticity estimates come from literature McNally surveyed up to about 2016. The rise of shale (a faster supply response), electric vehicles (a long-run demand substitute) and the large 2022 strategic stock releases may have changed the short-run numbers. Spare-capacity levels and the price "pain thresholds" are period-specific. McNally ch.3, Epilogue Outside the texts Check the IEA Oil Market Report and EIA STEO for current spare-capacity estimates, and recent academic estimates of oil supply and demand elasticities.

Going deeper: the call on OPEC and stock changes

Analysts reconcile balances with an identity. Production = consumption + change in stocks, with "missing barrels" absorbing data errors. The IEA's call on OPEC is the OPEC crude needed to keep global stocks unchanged, given forecasts of demand and non-OPEC supply. Downey ch.12 If OPEC produces above the call, stocks build, the curve drifts toward contango and prices fall, and the reverse when below. Combining this with Module 3's inventory–curve link gives the fundamental analyst's chain: balance → stock change → curve shape → price. Elasticities tell you how much price must move to close any gap that buffers cannot. Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • Short-run price elasticities are tiny (roughly −0.06 demand, 0.04 supply), so a small net shortfall needs a large price move. Long-run responses are many times larger.
  • For large shocks the response is non-linear, with the last barrels costing the most.
  • Spare capacity, strategic stocks and commercial stocks cut the net shortfall and so the price impact. Track them alongside the disruption itself.
  • Without a supply manager, prices oscillate in a wide corridor between shut-in costs and demand destruction.
