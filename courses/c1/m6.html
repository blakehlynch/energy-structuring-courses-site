Geopolitics and the new map

Learning objectives

  • Explain how shale changed US trade and the geopolitics of sanctions.
  • Describe the structure of 2014 sanctions on Russia's energy sector.
  • Identify chokepoint and import-dependence risks for major consumers.
  • Reconstruct the 2020 oil shock using earlier modules' tools.
  • Sketch long-run demand and decline-replacement needs and their sensitivities.

Prerequisites

  • Modules 1–5.

The new map, as of 2020

Yergin's The New Map (2020) organises the geopolitics of energy around five maps: America's, Russia's, China's, the Middle East's, and the climate and technology map. Long's early observation still holds: oil is "a highly political commodity", with most reserves in unstable regions and consumers dependent on imports. Yergin Introduction Long §1.2.3 This module summarises the themes most relevant to a physical originator. Read it with the dated box at the end in mind; it is the part of the course most exposed to events since 2020.

America: from importer to exporter

  • Shale took US net oil imports from about 60% of consumption in the mid-2000s to under 3% by 2019. The United States became the world's largest producer, reaching about 13 mb/d in February 2020. Yergin ch.7
  • The 40-year ban on crude exports ended in December 2015, in a budget deal that extended renewable tax credits. US light crude now flows to Europe and Asia, while complex Gulf Coast refineries keep importing heavier grades. That pattern only makes sense through Course 2's lens of value-in-use. Yergin ch.7 Trafigura CD ch.3 Synthesis
  • Shale changed the geopolitics of sanctions. When Iranian exports were sanctioned in 2012, rising US output offset the loss and the sanctions held. Yergin presents this as case study one of shale's geopolitical impact. Yergin ch.8

Russia: energy as statecraft

Yergin describes Russia's energy exports, especially gas to Europe, as a pillar of its great-power strategy. After the 2014 annexation of Crimea, Western sanctions hit three areas: named individuals, access to Western finance, and future oil growth. The oil sanctions targeted Arctic offshore and shale technology, deliberately not current output, to avoid raising world prices. Yergin ch.9–12 Yergin also records US Treasury warnings that heavy use of dollar-based financial sanctions could push trade toward other currencies and payment systems over time. Yergin ch.12 In Course 4 sanctions return as a compliance and structuring constraint. Wynne ch.12

China: the marginal buyer

China became the world's second-largest oil consumer and, before 2020, accounted for about half the growth in world demand. By early 2020 imports met about 75% of its consumption. Most of those imports pass the Strait of Malacca, and much of it originates in the Gulf and passes Hormuz. Yergin notes the South China Sea carries about 15 mb/d of oil, nearly as much as Hormuz. China built strategic reserves and diversified suppliers in response. Yergin ch.17–25 (esp. ch.22), ch.36 Downey ch.12

The Middle East: still the centre of spare capacity

Most of the world's spare capacity sits in Saudi Arabia, with some in the UAE and Kuwait. Yergin ch.8 Yergin calls the September 2019 drone and missile attacks on Saudi processing at Abqaiq and Khurais the largest disruption ever to world oil supplies. Yet the price spike was short-lived because Aramco repaired the facilities quickly and US shale cushioned sentiment. Yergin ch.34, photo captions Regional rivalry shapes oil policy too. In 2014 the Saudi–Iranian rivalry made Gulf producers unwilling to cut output, since cutting would also have benefited Iran. Yergin observes that geopolitics, for once, drove prices down. Yergin ch.34

Worked example 1 · a geopolitical risk premium, back of the envelopeChecked by code

A desk judges there is a 10% chance over the next quarter of a 4 mb/d disruption that buffers cannot offset, in a 102 mb/d market. Use short-run elasticities and an $80 base.

impact if it happens ≈ 3.92% / 0.10 = +39.2% naive expected premium ≈ 10% × 39.2% × $80 = $3.14/bbl

This is a deliberately simple expected-value sketch, not a pricing model. Real premia depend on risk aversion, inventories, the option market's implied volatility and skew (Course 6), and on how believable the threat is. Its value is discipline: it forces a desk to state a probability, a size and a buffer assumption instead of reacting to headlines. Synthesis

2020: when demand collapsed

Yergin's account of 2020 is a stress test of everything in Modules 3–5. Demand fell by about 8 mb/d in the first quarter and much more in April as lockdowns spread; US gasoline use fell by about half and Europe's by about two-thirds. OPEC+ broke up on 6 March and Saudi Arabia pledged to raise output toward 12.3 mb/d. Storage ran short, and on 20 April the expiring WTI contract settled below zero. In April OPEC+ agreed, with US involvement, the largest cut in history, 9.7 mb/d, with Russia and Saudi Arabia each cutting 2.5 mb/d from an 11 mb/d baseline. Yergin ch.36

Worked example 2 · how fast storage fillsChecked by code

Suppose demand loss leaves an illustrative 15 mb/d surplus and a 9.7 mb/d cut offsets part of it. Over 60 days:

residual surplus = 15 − 9.7 = 5.3 mb/d → stock build over 60 days = 318 MMbbl

Even after the largest cut in history, several hundred million barrels would need a home within two months. That explains floating storage, super-contango and the April price collapse at the landlocked WTI delivery point (Module 3). Yergin ch.36 Swindle ch.3 Synthesis

The transition map

  • As of 2020 the world energy system was still more than 80% oil, gas and coal. Yergin quotes the description of the transition so far as "energy addition": renewables growing on top of conventional supply. Yergin ch.46
  • Demand outlook (IHS Markit scenarios cited by Yergin): in the planning case, oil demand peaks in the mid-2030s and is about 113 mb/d in 2050, against roughly 100 mb/d before COVID. In aggressive climate-policy cases it falls to 60–80 mb/d by 2050. Cars are about 20% of oil demand (35% with light trucks); fleets turn over slowly at about 6–7% of the fleet sold each year, which caps how fast EVs can bite. Yergin ch.46
  • Supply never stops needing investment: Yergin cites a need for 3–5 billion barrels a year of new supply just to offset decline, and an IEA estimate of over $20 trillion of oil and gas investment over two decades. Yergin ch.46
  • New dependencies come with the transition: minerals for batteries and renewables, with their own supply chains and security concerns. Yergin ch.46
Worked example 3 · a transition demand sketchChecked by code

Start at 100 mb/d. Cars and light trucks are 35% of demand. By 2050, 30% of that fleet is electric and the remaining combustion vehicles are 20% more efficient. Other uses (trucks, aviation, shipping, petrochemicals) grow 1.2% a year for 30 years. All inputs are illustrative.

road demand = 35 × (1 − 0.30) × (1 − 0.20) = 19.6 mb/d other demand = 65 × 1.012^30 = 93.0 mb/d total 2050 = 112.6 mb/d supply to replace decline at 9%/yr on 100 mb/d = 3.29 billion bbl a year

These deliberately simple inputs land near the planning-case figure Yergin cites. The point is how sensitive the answer is: double the EV share or flatten non-road growth and 2050 demand falls well below today. Use the tool below to see which assumptions matter. The replacement figure sits inside the 3–5 billion range Yergin quotes. Yergin ch.46 McNally Epilogue Synthesis

Dated: this whole module

The texts stop in 2020. Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022 and the sanctions that followed (including the G7 price cap on Russian crude and products and the EU embargo), the diversion of Russian exports to Asia, and the growth of a "shadow fleet" of tankers all lie outside them. So do OPEC+ policy since 2020, conflicts affecting Red Sea shipping, and the actual paths of EV adoption and US shale. Outside the texts Before using any of this module's figures, check the IEA Oil Market Report and World Energy Outlook, the EIA STEO and International Energy Outlook, OPEC's MOMR, OFAC and EU sanctions guidance, and OIES research.

Going deeper: geopolitics as a variable in your models

For an originator, geopolitics enters deals in four ways, each covered elsewhere in the curriculum. Synthesis

  1. Price and volatility through disruption risk (Module 4 shock arithmetic; Course 6 for volatility and skew).
  2. Trade flows and differentials, as sanctions and conflict reroute barrels: Yergin's Iran and Russia cases; Course 3 for freight and netbacks.
  3. Counterparty and country risk: political risk insurance, export credit agencies and stabilisation clauses (Courses 4 and 9; Clews on political risk). Clews ch.13
  4. Legal permissibility: sanctions compliance, which can make an otherwise profitable trade unbookable (Course 4). Wynne ch.12

Key takeaways

  • Shale made the US an exporter and a strategic swing factor; the old OPEC-versus-non-OPEC frame gave way to the "Big Three".
  • Sanctions, chokepoints and regional rivalry move flows and differentials as much as flat prices.
  • 2020 showed every mechanism of this course at once: demand collapse, a cartel breakup, storage limits, negative prices and a record coordinated cut.
  • The transition changes long-run demand slowly, because fleets turn over slowly, while decline keeps the need for new supply large. All of this is dated; verify against current sources.
