The oil value chain and who trades it

Foundation≈ 55 min9 quiz questions8 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Describe the segments of the oil chain and why its single-purpose assets make supply sticky.
  • Identify the main market players and what each needs from an originator.
  • Explain why open trading grew after 1973 and why traders focus on differentials.
  • Convert fluently between barrels, gallons, tonnes, MMBtu and BOE.
  • Decompose a retail fuel price into crude, refining, distribution and taxes.

Prerequisites

  • None: entry point to the curriculum.

From well to wheel

The oil industry has three segments. Upstream finds and produces crude. Midstream moves and stores it by pipeline, tanker, rail and truck. Downstream refines and markets products. Synthesis Downey ch.5 McNally ch.3 Clews ch.5 Two physical facts shape the whole chain.

  • Oil is a liquid. It needs dedicated, single-purpose equipment at every step: wells, pipelines, tanks, tankers, refineries. McNally stresses that the gear is expensive and good for nothing else. Once built, every segment wants to run flat out, which makes supply slow to respond to price. McNally ch.3 Long §1.2.1
  • Oil is traded twice. Crude is sold to refiners, and products are sold to wholesalers and consumers. The two markets are linked by refinery economics (Course 2) but have different geography: crude is a global seaborne market, while products are more local and spec-bound. Long §1.2.1 Swindle ch.3

Trading units follow the logistics. North Sea crude moves in cargoes of around 600,000 bbl, the standard Brent forward cargo (500,000 bbl when Long wrote). Long-haul crude from the Gulf or West Africa is typically shipped in VLCCs (very large crude carriers) of up to about 2 million bbl, so a single cargo is a nine-figure dollar exposure. Products move in smaller ships and barges. Long §1.2.1 Edwards ch.2.3 Crude can take months to travel from wellhead to pump, so prices often move when the right grade is in the wrong place or arrives at the wrong moment. Long contrasts this with financial assets, which move instantly. Long §1.2

Why trading grew

Before 1973, integrated majors moved most oil internally and the open market only cleared imbalances. Nationalisations in the 1970s cut the majors off from their equity crude. They had to buy at arm's length, and external prices began to set internal transfer prices. Price volatility then created demand for hedging instruments. Long §1.1 McNally ch.6 Long describes the result as a set of "interlocking markets": physical spot and term trades, forward paper (e.g. Brent), futures, swaps and options. Hedging and arbitrage tie them together, so no single place "sets" the price. Long §1.3

Traders mostly trade differentials (grade, location, time) rather than the flat price. Trafigura frames the business as buying where untransformed value is low and selling transformed value where it is high, when the price gap exceeds the cost of transformation. Arbitrage then narrows the gap that created it. Trafigura CD ch.4 Long §1.3.3

ALM analogy

A trading house is closer to a bank's balance-sheet desk than to a fund. It earns a spread for carrying inventory and basis risk across time and location, funds that carry with short-term secured credit, and hedges the flat price. Its edge lies in logistics (the cost of transformation) and funding cost, much as an insurer's edge is in underwriting and the liability funding rate rather than in predicting market direction. Trafigura CD ch.4 Synthesis

Units every originator must convert fluently

  • 1 barrel = 42 US gallons. A barrel of crude holds roughly 5.4–5.8 MMBtu; Swindle cites about 5.4 from BP and notes that other sources ascribe 5.8. Swindle ch.3 Edwards ch.1.3
  • Barrel of oil equivalent (BOE) for gas: about 5,800 cubic feet per BOE. Edwards ch.1.3
  • Crude barrels per tonne: about 7.3–7.6 depending on density. Products range from about 6.35 for fuel oil to 8.3 for gasoline. Downey ch.17 Schofield ch.5
  • An efficient gas-fired combined-cycle plant burns about 7 MMBtu of gas per MWh, which links oil, gas and power prices (Courses 5 and 7). Swindle ch.3
Worked example 1 · where the pump price goesChecked by code

Kaiser describes the EIA's method for splitting the retail gasoline price. Crude cost is the refiner acquisition cost converted to $/gal. "Refining" is the wholesale spot price minus that crude cost. Taxes are added. "Distribution and marketing" is whatever is left. Kaiser Box 4.4 Take crude at $80/bbl, spot gasoline $2.40/gal, total taxes $0.55/gal (illustrative) and retail $3.40/gal.

crude = 80 / 42 = $1.905/gal (56.0%) refining = 2.40 − 1.905 = $0.495/gal (14.6%) taxes = $0.550/gal (16.2%) distribution = 3.40 − 2.40 − 0.55 = $0.450/gal (13.2%)

Crude dominates. That is why pump prices track crude and why, in high-tax countries, the same crude move changes the retail price by a much smaller percentage. Downey contrasts US and UK taxes for this reason, and Clews notes that tax was over 70% of the UK retail petrol price when he wrote, with crude cheap. Downey ch.17 Clews §7.2.2 At today's higher pump prices the share is nearer 55%, because UK fuel duty is a fixed amount per litre: the tax share falls when crude rises. Outside the texts

Reading the market's data flow

Fundamental analysts ("barrel counters") build supply-demand balances from published data. Downey describes the main sources:

  • the weekly EIA petroleum status report (mandatory survey);
  • the API weekly figures (voluntary, so less trusted);
  • the IEA's monthly Oil Market Report with its "call on OPEC";
  • Euroilstock (voluntary, heavily revised);
  • Japan's METI;
  • Singapore's weekly stocks.

Downey ch.5, ch.12 McNally argues that poor data, especially on non-OECD inventories, itself adds to volatility. McNally Epilogue

Dated: names, members and data

Downey's lists of OPEC members, the largest companies and refineries, and agency practices date from around 2008–2009. Several companies have since merged or been renamed, OPEC's membership has changed, and the IEA's membership and data products have evolved. Downey ch.5 Outside the texts For current membership and data, check OPEC's website, the IEA, and the EIA's Weekly Petroleum Status Report documentation.

Going deeper: why integration and trading coexist

McNally explains vertical integration as a defence against volatility. A company that owns both wells and refineries is naturally hedged on the crude price, and horizontal combination historically restrained over-investment. McNally ch.3 Yet independent traders thrive. The reconciliation is that integration hedges the flat price but leaves location, quality and timing mismatches inside the system. Long and Trafigura both describe the physical market as a web of these mismatches. Long §1.3.3 Trafigura CD ch.4 Synthesis An originator's product is often the solution to one specific mismatch, such as a storage lease, a blending service, a prepayment or a supply-and-offtake deal, rather than a view on the price level.

Key takeaways

  • Oil is a liquid moved and processed in single-purpose, capital-heavy assets, which makes the system slow to adjust.
  • Crude and products are traded twice and in interlocking markets. Most professional trading is in differentials, not the flat price.
  • Know the units (bbl, gal, tonnes, MMBtu, BOE) and the orders of magnitude.
  • Crude is most of the pump price where taxes are low. Data quality and transparency are themselves market drivers.
