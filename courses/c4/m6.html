Receivables, forfaiting and securitisation

Core≈ 55 min9 quiz questions7 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Compare recourse and non-recourse receivables finance.
  • Explain forfaiting mechanics and markets.
  • Describe receivables and future-flow securitisations.
  • Allocate pool losses across reserve, junior and senior layers.

Prerequisites

  • Modules 2 and 5.

Why sell receivables?

Every sale on credit turns a cargo into a receivable that ties up working capital until the buyer pays (Module 1). Selling receivables releases that cash. If the sale is without recourse, it can also move the buyer's credit risk off the seller's books. Wynne distinguishes buyer-led programmes ("supply chain finance", where a strong buyer's suppliers sell their invoices to a financer) from supplier-led ones (a seller sells receivables on many buyers). Wynne §2.11 Rutten calls factoring and forfaiting "fraternal twins": both rely on receivables but serve different markets. Moors & Rutten ch.3

Factoring

In cross-border factoring, the exporter assigns its receivables to an export factor, which works with an import factor in the buyer's country. The import factor assesses buyers and sets credit limits. The exporter receives an advance (typically 70–80% of the invoice), and the balance less fees when the buyer pays. Moors & Rutten ch.3 Without recourse, the factor bears buyer default (collection plus insurance plus finance, sometimes called "old-line" factoring). With recourse, the exporter keeps the credit risk. "Reverse factoring", initiated by a strong buyer, lets suppliers fund at the buyer's credit cost. Some buyers forbid assignment of their payables. Moors & Rutten ch.3

The fee buys credit assessment, collection and (without recourse) default cover. Compare it with credit insurance plus a borrowing-base advance (Modules 3 and 5). Moors & Rutten ch.3 Synthesis

Forfaiting

A forfaiter buys trade receivables, usually bills of exchange, promissory notes or deferred LC payments guaranteed ("avalised") by a bank, without recourse to the exporter. The name comes from forfeiting that right of recourse. Moors & Rutten ch.3 Typical tenors are 3–5 years (from 90 days up to 10), with tickets of $1–50 m. Pricing reflects the guaranteeing bank, the country and the currency. Paper trades in a secondary market among banks and funds. Since 2013 ICC's Uniform Rules for Forfaiting (URF) standardise the terms. Moors & Rutten ch.3 Advantages: 100% financing, simple documents, fast execution and access to high-risk countries. Disadvantage: some countries can vanish from the market overnight in a crisis. Moors & Rutten ch.3 The discount arithmetic is Module 2's.

Securitisation

Securitisation packages receivables (or future export flows) in a bankruptcy-remote SPV that issues notes to capital-market investors. Moors & Rutten ch.9 Wynne ch.9

  • Trader receivables programmes. Trafigura describes the mechanics. When a cargo is sold on 30–60 day terms, the trader sells the invoice to its SPV, which has funded itself with longer-term notes. Customers pay into an SPV account, and the revolving pool shortens the trader's liquidity cycle while giving it longer-dated funding. Trafigura CD ch.11
  • Future-flow and prepayment securitisations for producers. The SPV prepays the producer, re-sells the commodities to creditworthy buyers and services notes from an escrow account. Rutten's example is EGPC's 2005 $1.55 bn Petroleum Export Limited deal, partly wrapped by monoline insurers and hedged with floors. Such deals can pierce the sovereign rating ceiling. Moors & Rutten ch.9, ch.14
  • True sale. The structure works only if a court would treat the transfer as a sale, not a disguised secured loan, in the originator's insolvency. Argentine courts upheld true sale in the provincial oil-royalty bonds discussed by Rutten. Moors & Rutten ch.5, ch.20
  • Reputation. Enron's prepay-based structures disguised loans and damaged the name of commodity prepayments and SPVs. Rutten's point is that with proper disclosure the techniques are sound. Moors & Rutten Introduction, ch.20

Credit protection in a receivables securitisation comes from layers that absorb losses before senior noteholders: a cash reserve, then junior (subordinated) notes or seller-retained interest, sized against expected defaults and "dilution" (credit notes, disputes, quality claims). Synthesis Outside the texts

Worked example 2 · loss layers in a $500 m receivables poolChecked by code

Pool $500 m; senior notes 88% ($440 m), junior 12% ($60 m), plus a separately funded cash reserve of $10 m (total first-loss cushion $70 m). Base case: 6% of receivables default with 60% loss given default, plus 1% dilution.

pool loss = 500 × (6% × 60% + 1%) = $23.0 m → reserve absorbs $10.0 m, junior $13.0 m, senior $0.0 m stress: 30% defaults → loss $95.0 m → senior loses $25.0 m

Senior notes survive anything up to about $70 m of losses (reserve plus junior). Rating agencies size that cushion as a multiple of expected losses, with concentration limits on large obligors. A pool dominated by a few refiners needs more subordination than a granular one. Synthesis Outside the texts

Dated: markets and rules

The ICC Uniform Rules for Forfaiting (2013) are current. Since the texts, supply-chain-finance programmes grew sharply, and the 2021 Greensill collapse prompted new accounting disclosure rules for supplier finance (IASB and FASB, effective 2023–24). Securitisation rules also changed: the EU and UK securitisation regulations, with risk retention and STS criteria. Outside the texts Check ICC, ITFA, the IASB and FASB, and EU and UK regulators.

Going deeper: borrow against receivables or sell them?

A trader can fund receivables three ways. Synthesis

  1. Keep them in a borrowing base, which is cheap but carries full recourse. The receivables stay on balance sheet and use credit lines.
  2. Sell them without recourse (factoring, forfaiting, receivables purchase). This costs more but removes the asset and the buyer risk. Accounting derecognition depends on transferring substantially all risks and rewards.
  3. Securitise, which gives term funding at scale with retained first loss. Disclosure and structuring costs are high, and the originator keeps much of the risk economically.

Trafigura's note that a trader can deconsolidate a prepayment when it has sold on substantially all risks and rewards shows the same accounting logic. Trafigura PD p.12 For an ALM mind: compare funding cost, capital or rating impact, and liquidity resilience, especially whether investors or banks will keep buying in a crisis.

Key takeaways

  • Factoring advances against invoices with collection and optional credit cover. Reverse factoring borrows the buyer's credit.
  • Forfaiting buys bank-guaranteed receivables without recourse, trades in a secondary market, and is governed by the ICC URF.
  • Securitisation moves receivables or future flows into an SPV. True sale, reserves and subordination protect investors.
  • The choice between borrowing against receivables and selling them turns on cost, recourse, balance-sheet treatment and liquidity in stress.
