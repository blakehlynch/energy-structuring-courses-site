Security and credit enhancement

Core≈ 60 min9 quiz questions7 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Assemble a security package across goods, contracts and accounts.
  • Use political and credit insurance appropriately.
  • Embed floors and collars while avoiding uncovered option sales.
  • Explain ECA support and OECD Consensus terms.

Prerequisites

  • Module 4; Course 3, Module 3.

The security package

Wynne's chapter on taking security in emerging markets organises the usual package around three asset types: the goods and their title, transport and storage documents; contractual rights and receivables (export contracts, insurance proceeds, hedging contracts); and cash in collection and reserve accounts. Wynne ch.6 A security provider can give no better rights than it has, so security must come from whoever owns the goods or is party to the contract at each stage. Each change of title, control or responsibility along the chain shifts the risk. Wynne §2.2.3 Where local law doesn't recognise a security trustee holding for a syndicate, lawyers use a "parallel debt" so the agent has its own claim to secure. Wynne §6.7 Outside the texts

Rutten's due-diligence chapter lists what must be checked before relying on any of this: availability of the commodity, market and price risk, quality of buyers, hedging, the contracts, every party in the chain (owner, warehouse operator, inspector, collateral manager), and country risks to performance, payments and enforcement. Moors & Rutten ch.10

Insurance

  • Political risk insurance covers events such as expropriation, contract frustration by a public buyer, war and political violence, currency inconvertibility and transfer restrictions, licence cancellation and embargoes. Private covers are typically capped at 90–95% of the risk to limit moral hazard. Exclusions, terrorism among them, need careful reading. Moors & Rutten ch.13
  • Credit insurance covers buyer non-payment, typically on open-account receivables, and can be combined with political risk as "comprehensive" cover. Rutten notes that many Argentine claims in 2001–02 were not accepted as political, which is why banks buy comprehensive cover. Moors & Rutten ch.13
  • Lenders should be named loss payees, and policies drafted so they count as credit risk mitigation for capital purposes. Moors & Rutten ch.5, ch.13 Wynne §11.6, §12.6.5.3
Worked example 1 · insuring a receivables poolChecked by code

A trader insures $100 m of 90-day open-account receivables at 90% cover for a premium of 0.40% a year (illustrative).

premium = 100 m × 0.40% × 90/360 = $100,000; uninsured retention = $10.0 m

The insured receivables now earn an 85% advance rate in the borrowing base (Module 3) instead of 60%. Here the insurance pays for itself mainly through extra funding capacity, not through expected claims. Synthesis

Price hedging inside a financing

Rutten describes three ways to embed commodity derivatives: fixing prices or setting floors on the export flow, linking interest or principal to prices, and selling options to subsidise the interest rate. Moors & Rutten ch.14 Floors raise the amount that can be lent. Collars (buy a put, sell a call) cut the premium. Selling options is the dangerous one.

  • Ashanti Goldfields, 1999. The miner had hedged about half its reserves with forwards and "zero-cost" option structures. When gold jumped, counterparties demanded margin, and the company had gold in the ground but no cash. It had to accept onerous rescue terms. Moors & Rutten ch.14, Box 14.1
  • Ghana National Petroleum Corporation, 1991. GNPC sold crude call options to cut the interest on a loan, although it imported crude and exported products. The calls were therefore uncovered, and GNPC was doubly exposed to rising crude prices. Moors & Rutten ch.14

Rutten's rule: sell options only when covered by physical exposure in the same commodity, volume and period, with capped maximum payouts. Moors & Rutten ch.14

Worked example 2 · four hedges at $50 oilChecked by code

A producer whose prepayment breaks even at about $59.70 (Module 4) compares outcomes if oil averages $50. Strikes and premiums are illustrative.

unhedged: $50.00 bought $65 puts for $4: $61.00 zero-cost collar 62/88: $62.00 sold $85 calls for $5: $55.00, but at $110 with calls on 1.5× production: $80.00

Only the put and the collar keep debt service covered at $50. Selling calls earns a little income in a falling market and, if it is over-sold, gives away the very upside that should repay the loan. That is the GNPC and Ashanti lesson in numbers. Moors & Rutten ch.14 Synthesis

Export credit agencies and official support

ECAs (state or state-backed insurers and lenders) cover political and commercial risk to promote their country's exports. Since 2008 they have been central to large, longer-tenor financings. Moors & Rutten ch.16 OECD members follow the "Consensus" arrangement: minimum premium rates by risk category, minimum fixed rates (CIRRs), and limits on tenor, down payment and local content. Moors & Rutten ch.16 Wynne notes that ECAs rarely take 100% of the risk; a typical split is 85% covered with the financer holding 15%, often under a common terms agreement and an intercreditor agreement. Wynne §2.13 Regional bodies such as Afreximbank, and development finance institutions (IFC, MIGA, regional development banks), add trade finance, guarantees and political risk cover. Moors & Rutten ch.16–17

Worked example 3 · blended cost of an ECA-covered loanChecked by code

85% of an 8-year equipment loan is ECA-covered at 4.5% plus a 6% flat premium. The uncovered 15% costs 7.5%. Figures are illustrative.

ECA tranche all-in ≈ 4.5% + 6%/8 = 5.25% → blended ≈ 0.85 × 5.25% + 0.15 × 7.5% = 5.588%

Spreading the premium evenly over the tenor is a rough approximation; amortisation profiles change the true figure. Even so, the ECA's sovereign backing usually beats commercial pricing for frontier borrowers by a wide margin. Moors & Rutten ch.16 Synthesis

ALM analogy

Insurance, guarantees and ECA cover are credit risk mitigation in the Basel sense: they substitute a stronger obligor's risk weight for part of the exposure, subject to eligibility tests on the wording. Embedded hedges are like interest-rate caps and floors inside a loan. They change the borrower's cash-flow profile and the lender's loss distribution together. Wynne §12.6.5.3 Synthesis

Dated: official support and insurance markets

The OECD Arrangement was substantially modernised in 2023 (longer tenors and climate-related flexibilities), CIRR levels have moved with interest rates, and some ECAs now restrict fossil-fuel support. Since 2022, political risk and credit insurance markets have repriced for sanctions and war exclusions. Outside the texts Check the OECD Export Credits site, Berne Union statistics and current ECA policies.

Going deeper: the cheapest enhancement for the risk

Put each mitigant against the risk it removes. Synthesis

  • Price risk: hedges and over-collateralisation.
  • Buyer payment risk: LCs, credit insurance or factoring.
  • Country transfer and political risk: offshore accounts plus political risk insurance, or ECA or MDB cover.
  • Performance risk: track record, collateral management, trader skin in the game.
  • Legal and enforcement risk: structure (ownership or SPV), choice of law, opinions.

Rutten's caution is that layering every enhancement can make a deal too expensive and slow, so pay only for the risks you cannot otherwise bear. Moors & Rutten ch.6 Each mitigant also has its own counterparty (insurer, hedge provider, ECA), whose risk is concentrated in the stress scenarios where you need it most. Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • Security covers goods and documents, contracts and receivables, and accounts. It must be taken from the right party at each stage.
  • Political and credit insurance transfer specific risks. Read the exclusions and name the lenders as loss payees.
  • Floors and collars protect debt service. Sold options must be covered and capped (Ashanti, GNPC).
  • ECAs and development banks provide long-tenor, lower-cost cover within OECD Consensus rules, rarely for 100% of the risk.
