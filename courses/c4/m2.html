Payment methods, LCs and guarantees

Core≈ 55 min9 quiz questions10 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Rank payment methods by risk to buyer and seller.
  • Explain LC confirmation, settlement and discrepancies.
  • Choose among back-to-back, transferable, revolving and red- or green-clause LCs.
  • Cost an LC and discount a deferred payment under two conventions.

Prerequisites

  • Module 1; Course 3, Module 7.

Four ways to get paid

Rutten ranks the payment methods by who carries the risk. Moors & Rutten ch.1

  • Open account: ship now, get paid later. Cheapest and simplest, but the seller carries all credit risk and the financing. Use it with trusted buyers, affiliates or in stable markets.
  • Documentary collection: banks pass documents but guarantee nothing. The buyer gets title documents only by paying (documents against payment) or by accepting a draft (documents against acceptance). Cheaper than an LC; governed by the ICC's Uniform Rules for Collections.
  • Documentary letter of credit: the buyer's bank undertakes to pay against compliant documents; the next best thing to cash in advance for the seller.
  • Advance payment: safest for the seller and riskiest for the buyer. Often partial, and sometimes restricted by currency controls.

Rutten adds the bank payment obligation (BPO), an electronic data-matching equivalent of an LC launched through SWIFT, with ICC rules approved in 2013. Moors & Rutten ch.1

How a documentary LC works

The issuing bank (buyer's bank) undertakes to pay the beneficiary (seller) when documents matching the credit are presented, typically commercial invoice, full set of B/Ls, inspection certificates and insurance. Under UCP 600 all credits are irrevocable. Moors & Rutten ch.1 Long §13.3.3 Key points:

  • Confirmation. A second, usually first-class, bank adds its own undertaking. The seller is then protected against the issuing bank and its country, not just the buyer. Moors & Rutten ch.1
  • Settlement: by sight payment, deferred payment, acceptance of a draft, or negotiation, where a bank advances funds against the documents, with or without recourse. Moors & Rutten ch.1, Box 1.1
  • Documents, not goods. Banks pay on compliant documents whether or not the oil matches the contract, and refuse discrepant ones even when the oil is fine. Rutten cites estimates that 60–70% of LCs were discrepant in some way in the early 2000s. Discrepancies matter when one side wants a way out. Moors & Rutten ch.1 Long §13.3.3
  • Costs: negotiation or payment fees around 0.10–0.15% flat. Confirmation fees are the bulk of the seller's cost, usually charged quarterly in advance on the confirmed amount and priced off the issuing bank's risk. Moors & Rutten ch.1
Worked example 1 · the seller's cost of a confirmed LCChecked by code

A $40 m crude sale is secured by an LC confirmed for 5 months at 1.2% a year, payable quarterly in advance, plus a 0.125% negotiation fee. Rates are illustrative.

quarters charged = 2 → confirmation = 40 m × 1.2% / 4 × 2 = $240,000 negotiation = 40 m × 0.125% = $50,000 → total $290,000 = 0.725% of the cargo

On a trade earning well under 1% this is a real cost, which is why sellers negotiate who pays which fees. Charging confirmation quarterly in advance also makes a 4-month credit cost exactly as much as a 6-month one: both are charged two quarters. Moors & Rutten ch.1

LC variants used by traders

  • Back-to-back LCs. A trader uses an LC from its buyer as collateral for a second LC to its supplier. The terms must mirror each other, with a lower amount and earlier dates on the second credit. It is risky because performance of the second credit depends on clean presentation under the first, and the UCP has no specific rules for it. Moors & Rutten ch.3
  • Transferable LCs. The first beneficiary (the trader) transfers all or part of the credit to its supplier. They are governed by the UCP and more secure than back-to-back, but the supplier's identity usually becomes visible to the buyer. Moors & Rutten ch.3
  • Revolving LCs reinstate after each drawing, which is cheaper for regular cargo flows. Moors & Rutten ch.3
  • Red-clause and green-clause LCs let the seller draw an advance before shipment. Red-clause advances are typically about 20% against an undertaking. Green-clause advances can reach about 80% against documents showing the goods are held for the bank, which makes them a form of structured finance. Moors & Rutten ch.3

Standbys and demand guarantees

Wynne groups standby LCs, demand guarantees and bonds as independent payment undertakings. The issuer's obligation is separate from the underlying contract and is triggered by documents alone, under UCP 600, ISP98 or URDG 758. Wynne §4.1 In oil trading, a standby is a back-up called only if the buyer fails to pay, so it is simpler and cheaper than a documentary LC (Course 3, Module 7). Long §13.3.4

Turning a deferred payment into cash

A seller holding a confirmed deferred-payment LC or an accepted draft can sell it to a bank for cash now. Under negotiation without recourse, the bank's risk is the issuing or confirming bank, not the seller. Moors & Rutten ch.1–3 Two conventions matter when comparing quotes. A straight discount deducts rate × time from face value. Discount to yield divides face value by (1 + rate × time). The straight discount costs the seller more for the same quoted rate.

Worked example 2 · discounting a 180-day LC paymentChecked by code

The $40 m is payable 180 days after B/L under the confirmed credit. A bank quotes 6.5% (actual/360).

straight discount: 40 × (1 − 0.065 × 180/360) = $38.700 m discount to yield: 40 / (1 + 0.065 × 180/360) = $38.741 m the straight discount's true yield = 6.72%

About $41,000 separates the two conventions on one cargo. Always ask which one a forfaiting or discount quote uses (Module 6). Synthesis Moors & Rutten ch.3

ALM analogy

A confirmed LC swaps the buyer's credit for a bank's, much as a guarantee or credit derivative substitutes one obligor for another in a capital calculation. Discounting it is like discounting commercial paper: price equals face less a time-based charge for the obligor's credit and funding. Synthesis

Dated: rules and practice

UCP 600 (2007), ISP98 and URDG 758 remain current. Electronic presentation has advanced since the texts: eUCP 2.0 (2019), wider adoption of electronic B/Ls, and the UK Electronic Trade Documents Act 2023 giving electronic trade documents legal status. BPO uptake has been modest. Outside the texts Check the ICC Banking Commission, the ICC Trade Register and current SWIFT trade statistics.

Going deeper: picking the instrument

A practical rule from Rutten's evaluation: use the least expensive instrument that covers the risk you actually cannot bear. Moors & Rutten ch.1, ch.3 Synthesis

  • Trusted buyer in a stable country: open account, perhaps with credit insurance (Module 5).
  • Sound buyer in an unstable country: an LC confirmed by a bank outside that country.
  • Trader in the middle: transferable LC if disclosure is acceptable, otherwise back-to-back with carefully mirrored terms, or a prepayment structure (Module 4).
  • Seller needing pre-shipment cash: a green-clause LC or structured pre-export finance.

The hidden cost in each case is operational: discrepancies, document chains and LOIs (Course 3, Module 7).

Key takeaways

  • Risk sits in a different place for each payment method: open account, collection, LC, advance.
  • LCs pay against documents, not goods. Confirmation shifts risk to a first-class bank, and discrepancies are the practical risk.
  • Traders use back-to-back, transferable, revolving and red- or green-clause LCs to finance intermediation.
  • Deferred LC payments can be discounted for cash. Check the discount convention.
