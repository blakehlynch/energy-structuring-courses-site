How trading houses are funded

Core≈ 50 min9 quiz questions5 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Explain why banks lend against commodity flows and what self-liquidating means.
  • Describe the layers of a trading house's funding.
  • Size working capital and stressed margin needs against committed lines.
  • Distinguish structured commodity finance from project finance.

Prerequisites

  • Course 3 recommended.

Why traders borrow so much, and why banks lend

Trafigura opens its funding chapter with the basic fact: trading houses buy and sell far more commodities than their own capital could pay for, so they depend on continuous short-term bank funding. Banks lend because the loans are secured on the commodities themselves. Trafigura CD ch.11 Wynne's shorthand for the lender's mindset is that "cash is king". The financer's main concern is that the sale proceeds land in an account it controls and repay the loan. Security over goods and contracts is the back-up. Wynne §2.1 Rutten defines structured commodity finance as financing in which a stock or flow of commodities is separated from its owner and serves as collateral and as the source of repayment. Moors & Rutten Introduction

Because the transaction repays itself, such lending can reach places balance-sheet lending cannot. Wynne calls this self-liquidating: the lender expects repayment from the deal performing, not from the borrower's general credit. Wynne §2.1 It does not mean the lender can always enforce and get its money back.

ALM analogy

A trading house's funding problem resembles a bank's: large, short-dated assets (cargoes and receivables) funded by short-term secured lines, plus contingent liquidity calls (futures margin) that spike in stress. The questions are the same: matched funding, committed versus uncommitted lines, and stressed outflows against liquidity buffers. Synthesis

The funding stack

Trafigura's chapter and Rutten's history together give the layers. Trafigura CD ch.11 Moors & Rutten Introduction

  1. Transactional trade finance. Bilateral LCs and B/L-backed loans for individual cargoes, repaid from each sale and marked to market at least weekly. Because repayment depends on the deal rather than the trader, a trader can carry far more of this debt than an ordinary company could. Trafigura CD ch.11
  2. Borrowing bases. Pooled facilities sized off inventory and receivables reported regularly to a group of banks (Module 3). Trafigura CD ch.11
  3. Repos. Selling inventory (often exchange-grade metal) to a bank and committing to repurchase it later. Banks prefer owning to lending here because it needs less regulatory capital. Trafigura CD ch.11 Moors & Rutten ch.5
  4. Syndicated revolving credit facilities for liquidity, above all to meet margin calls on hedges. Trafigura stresses that these can run to hundreds of millions in volatile markets, and are largest exactly when prices rise and physical stock gains value but is not yet sold. Trafigura CD ch.11
  5. Receivables securitisation through an SPV that buys invoices and funds them with longer-term notes (Module 6). Trafigura CD ch.11
  6. Capital markets and asset sales. Bonds (Trafigura issued a perpetual bond in 2013 after committing to semi-annual disclosure) and recycling of logistics assets while retaining commercial control. Trafigura CD ch.11
  7. Syndicated prepayments, which traders originate for producers and fund through bank syndicates (Module 4). Trafigura CD ch.11 Trafigura PD p.8–9

Rutten traces how the industry got here. In the 1980s banks lent to traders on a corporate basis and the traders financed producers. After several trading houses failed, the 1990s brought structured lending directly to producers against offshore escrow accounts fed by prime buyers. The 2000s added scale, Chinese banks and lighter structures. Moors & Rutten Introduction

Worked example 1 · working capital in the trade cycleChecked by code

A mid-sized oil trader turns over $25 bn a year. Cargoes sit as inventory or on the water for 20 days, buyers pay 30 days after B/L, and suppliers give 15 days. All figures are illustrative.

daily turnover = 25,000 / 365 = $68.5 m working capital = 68.5 m × (20 + 30 − 15) days = $2.40 bn

Every extra day of credit given to buyers ties up another $68 m (because physical trading gross margins are typically below 1–2%, cost of goods sold is close enough to revenue that scaling DIO, DSO and DPO off a single daily turnover figure is a standard desk approximation). This is why payment terms are a funding decision as much as a commercial one, and why receivables finance (Module 6) matters. Synthesis

Worked example 2 · margin liquidity under stressChecked by code

The same trader holds 40 m bbl of physical oil hedged with short futures. Prices rally $10/bbl in a week.

variation margin to pay = 40 m × $10 = $400 m (offset by physical gains realised only on sale) total need = 2.40 bn + 0.40 bn = $2.80 bn vs lines of $3.20 bn → headroom $403 m

A hedged book can be solvent and still illiquid. Trafigura's prescription is committed facilities with substantial headroom, sized for the most volatile conditions. Trafigura CD ch.11 Moors & Rutten ch.14

Trading-house funding needs

Working capital plus stressed margin against committed lines

Facilities are fixed at $2.2 bn of transactional lines and a $1.0 bn committed revolver. Transactional lines grow with the trade only if banks keep renewing them, which is the risk in a crisis. Trafigura CD ch.11 Moors & Rutten Introduction

Structured commodity finance versus project finance

Rutten contrasts the two. Project finance funds an asset not yet built: completion risk, equity sponsors, long tenors and forecast cash flows. Structured commodity finance lends against existing production, inventory and contracts: no completion risk, market risk mitigated by sales contracts and hedges, and short to medium tenors. Moors & Rutten Introduction, Box I.1 The two increasingly blend. Pre-export structures can fund brownfield expansions, and reserve-based loans sit between the two (Module 3; Course 9). Moors & Rutten ch.6, ch.20

Dated: the funding landscape

Rutten (2014), Wynne (2012) and Trafigura (2018–20) predate several shocks. Commodity trade-finance frauds around 2020 caused banks to cut lines to smaller traders. The 2022 energy price spike drove record margin calls and emergency facilities for some utilities and traders. LIBOR was replaced by SOFR and other risk-free rates, and private credit funds became significant lenders. Outside the texts Check recent trading-house annual reports, ICC Trade Register reports and BIS/FSB work on commodity-market liquidity.

Going deeper: why trader-originated lending works

Rutten and Trafigura both explain why credit flows through traders. Banks can lend to a trader they know, while the trader knows the producer and controls the offtake. Banks take the producer's performance risk, while the trader and creditworthy buyers carry the payment risk. Moors & Rutten ch.6 Trafigura PD p.8, p.11 Viewed through a credit lens, the structure transforms emerging-market corporate risk (high default probability, low recovery) into a combination of performance risk on a producer that needs its market access and payment risk on an OECD buyer. That is why spreads can sit well below the producer's unsecured cost: Rutten's Cocobod example fell to under 0.2% over LIBOR. Moors & Rutten ch.6, Box 6.1 Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • Traders fund large, fast-turning books with short-term secured, self-liquidating credit. Cash control is the core protection.
  • The funding stack: transactional LCs and loans, borrowing bases, repos, syndicated revolvers, securitisation, bonds and asset recycling.
  • Hedged books still need liquidity: margin calls peak when prices rally.
  • Structured commodity finance lends against existing flows and stock. Project finance lends against an asset yet to be built.
