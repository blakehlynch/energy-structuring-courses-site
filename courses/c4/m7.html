Regulation, sanctions, fraud and workouts

Prerequisites

  • Modules 1–6.

Fraud: why trade finance attracts it

Wynne explains why trade finance offers fraudsters opportunity: many parties who rarely meet, cross-border documents that are hard to verify, and more incentive when credit is scarce. Wynne §12.3.3 Recurring patterns across the texts: Wynne §12.5.8 Moors & Rutten ch.5

  • Document fraud: forged or multiple warehouse receipts, forwarder's certificates of receipt issued without goods, and bills of lading for phantom shipments.
  • Collateral fraud: the same stock pledged to several banks, round-tripping through related "buyers" (Rutten's tin dross case), and over-valued non-exchange goods.
  • Trade-based money laundering: over- or under-invoicing, multiple invoicing, short- or over-shipping, and obscured goods descriptions.

Defences: know-your-customer due diligence on every party in the chain, independent verification of documents and goods, control of cash flows, red-flag screening of unusual instructions, and back-office capacity to monitor collateral daily. Wynne §12.3.3 Moors & Rutten ch.5

Bribery, sanctions and anti-money-laundering

  • Bribery. The UK Bribery Act 2010 criminalises giving and receiving bribes and bribing foreign officials, with no exemption for facilitation ("grease") payments. Its corporate offence of failing to prevent bribery by associated persons is defensible only by "adequate procedures". The US FCPA differs: it covers public-sector bribery only, carves out facilitation payments, and adds books-and-records rules. Wynne §12.4
  • Sanctions (UN, EU, UK, and US OFAC with extraterritorial reach) freeze assets and ban dealings, funds transfers, trade in goods and investment. UK sanctions offences are strict liability, with no risk-based defence. A trade financier can breach them by lending to a designated person, sharing a syndicate with one, financing restricted goods, or paying a designated beneficiary under an LC. Wynne §12.5
  • AML and KYC follow a risk-based approach, with enhanced due diligence for higher-risk counterparties and jurisdictions. Industry guidance includes the JMLSG trade finance chapter and the Wolfsberg Trade Finance Principles. Wynne §12.5.7–12.5.10
  • Local rules: stamp duties, notarisation, compulsory repatriation of export proceeds and central-bank registration of foreign loans can make a structure invalid or uneconomic if missed. Wynne §12.2.4 Wynne §5.19

Rutten adds a commercial example. EU sanctions on Iranian banks from 2010 meant previously acceptable buyers could no longer support export-receivables financing, because lenders couldn't receive funds from designated banks. Exporters turned to Asian buyers and financiers instead. Moors & Rutten ch.6

Bank capital and trade finance

A bank's appetite for a trade deal depends on its regulatory capital cost. Under the Basel framework capital equals exposure × credit conversion factor (for off-balance-sheet items) × risk weight × the minimum ratio. Wynne §12.6 Wynne explains the conversion factors: documentary LCs are likely to be drawn but well secured by documents (Basel I CCF 20%). Transaction-related standbys are less likely to be drawn but harder to recover (50%). Proposals in Basel III to apply 100% to all LCs alarmed the industry in 2011–12. Concessions waived the one-year maturity floor and the sovereign floor for certain trade instruments. Wynne §12.6.4, §12.6.7 Under the "slotting" approach for specialised lending, structured commodity deals are graded on transaction strength, country mitigation, the goods, the parties, controls and insurance. Wynne §12.6.5.2 Rutten notes that well-structured deals can be treated as short-term even when multi-year. That is one reason such facilities are cheap for good borrowers. Moors & Rutten ch.6

Worked example 1 · capital for a confirmed LCChecked by code

A bank confirms $100 m of LCs; the issuing bank risk-weights at 100%. Minimum ratio 8%.

CCF 20%: capital = 100 × 0.20 × 1.00 × 8% = $1.6 m CCF 100%: capital = $8.0 m, five times as much
Worked example 1b · Basel specialized lending (Commodities Finance) slotting RWAChecked by code

Under the Basel framework for Specialised Lending (Commodities Finance), banks on the internal-ratings-based (IRB) approach that cannot estimate default probabilities for a structured trade facility must use the supervisory slotting criteria instead (banks on the standardised approach use external-rating or specialised-lending risk weights). Slotting assigns weights based on structure strength (goods control, liquid collateral, offshore collection) and maturity (< 2.5 years):

Strong category (< 2.5 yrs maturity): risk weight = 50% → RWA on $100m = $50m → capital = $4.0m Good category (< 2.5 yrs maturity): risk weight = 70% → RWA on $100m = $70m → capital = $5.6m Satisfactory category: risk weight = 115% → RWA on $100m = $115m → capital = $9.2m Weak category: risk weight = 250% → RWA on $100m = $250m → capital = $20.0m

Moving a structured commodity deal from "Satisfactory" to "Strong" cuts regulatory capital consumption by over 56%, directly expanding the lending margin and ROC. Wynne §12.6.5.2 Synthesis

Worked example 2 · return on capitalChecked by code

Confirmation fee 0.60% a year; Wynne cites an average LC tenor of 115 days.

fee = 100 m × 0.60% × 115/360 = $191,667 annualised return on capital: 37.5% at CCF 20% vs 7.5% at CCF 100%

Before costs and expected loss, the conversion factor alone moves the business from very attractive to marginal. This is why the 2011–12 lobbying mattered, and why banks prefer structures with favourable capital treatment such as ownership, insurance and short effective tenors. Wynne §12.6.7 Moors & Rutten ch.5 Synthesis

Trade-finance capital and return

How instrument, rating and tenor drive return on capital

Minimum capital ratio 8%. Ignores funding cost, operating cost, expected loss and capital buffers, so treat results as gross return on regulatory capital.

When deals go wrong: waivers, workouts and enforcement

  • Waivers. Small breaches often surface first, such as a missed coverage ratio. A waiver should be in writing, specify the breach and its conditions, and preserve other rights. Syndicated deals need the right majority, and the lender must check its risk-transfer arrangements (participations, insurance, hedges). Wynne §13.2
  • Workout or enforce? Compare expected recovery from enforcing your own security with a consensual restructuring. Coordinate creditors early through a lead bank or committee. Workouts often recover more, but need most creditors aboard. Wynne §13.2–13.3
  • Enforcement realities: court auctions, perishable goods, warehouse liens and legal formalities can turn security into slow, partial recovery. Rutten cites liquidation losses of 30–40% of market value. Wynne §5.10 Moors & Rutten ch.5
Worked example 3 · enforcing on $30 m of stockChecked by code

With liquidation losses of 30–40%, a lender enforcing on $30 m of pledged stock recovers about $18–21 m before legal costs and delay. That is the benchmark a workout proposal must beat. Moors & Rutten ch.5 Synthesis

ALM analogy

Return on regulatory capital, slotting criteria and credit-risk-mitigation recognition are the same machinery that prices any bank asset. Trade finance happens to be short-dated, self-liquidating and low-loss, so CCFs and maturity floors weigh unusually heavily on it. Workouts are the commodity version of loan restructurings: compare recovery values, keep the creditor group coordinated, and document everything. Synthesis

Dated: regulation, sanctions and fraud since the texts

Much has changed since 2012–14, all outside the texts. The final Basel III standards (2017) kept a 20% CCF for short-term self-liquidating trade LCs, with implementation phasing in across jurisdictions from 2023–25 (verify for your regulator). Sanctions on Russia since 2022, including the oil price cap, transformed compliance for oil trade finance. Frauds at several Singapore oil traders in 2020 cost banks billions and accelerated digital documents and stricter collateral checks. UK sanctions moved under the Sanctions and Anti-Money Laundering Act 2018 after Brexit. Outside the texts Check BCBS, OFAC, the EU and OFSI, FATF trade-based money-laundering guidance and the Wolfsberg principles.

Going deeper: a compliance-first origination checklist

Before any commercial work on a structured trade deal, clear five gates. Synthesis Wynne ch.12 Moors & Rutten ch.10

  1. KYC and beneficial ownership of every party, including the chain behind counterparties.
  2. Sanctions screening of parties, vessels, ports, goods and payment routes, rescreened during the deal.
  3. Anti-bribery review of agents, intermediaries and any official approvals.
  4. Local legal requirements: licences, registrations, stamp duty, repatriation rules.
  5. Capital and credit treatment of the proposed structure.

Only then design the economics. Rutten's dedicated due-diligence chapter and Wynne's regulatory chapter together make the case that in this business, structure and compliance come before price.

Key takeaways

  • Trade finance is fraud-prone: verify documents and goods, control cash, screen for red flags.
  • Sanctions are strict liability and fast-changing. Bribery and AML regimes reach agents and intermediaries.
  • Capital cost (CCF × risk weight × 8%) drives bank appetite. LC treatment was a major policy fight.
  • Waivers in writing, workouts coordinated, enforcement realistic: recoveries often run 60–70% of value.
