Inventory, warehouse and borrowing-base finance

Core≈ 60 min9 quiz questions10 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Structure inventory finance with genuine third-party control.
  • Recognise the main warehouse fraud and enforcement risks.
  • Compare pledge-based and ownership-based structures.
  • Compute a borrowing base and an RBL base.

Prerequisites

  • Modules 1–2.

Inventory finance: the commodity as collateral

In inventory (warehouse receipt) finance, the owner pledges stored commodities to a lender and repays from the sale proceeds, which buyers pay directly to the lender. Moors & Rutten ch.5 Rutten lists the basic flow: store the goods in an independently controlled warehouse, give the bank a security interest (typically a warehouse receipt), inspect, lend, sell to acceptable buyers, release against new security, collect proceeds in a bank-controlled account, repay, and release the surplus. Moors & Rutten ch.5

  • Who controls the warehouse is everything. Receipts the borrower issues on its own stock are little more than self-certification. Separation comes either from a field warehouse, where an independent collateral manager takes control of space on the borrower's premises, or a public warehouse run by an operator with no interest in the goods. Moors & Rutten ch.5
  • Collateral management agreement versus stock monitoring. Under a CMA the collateral manager takes on some liability for the goods. Under a stock monitoring agreement the inspector only reports what it sees. Wynne stresses that even a CMA is no guarantee the goods are there, and warns of hidden exclusions and caps. Moors & Rutten ch.5 Wynne §5.16–5.17
  • Financing ratio. Lend below market value, allowing for liquidation costs, volatility, perishability and tenor, with a higher ratio if buyers are already identified or the goods are hedged. Rutten cites practitioners estimating that 30–40% of value can be lost in a forced liquidation. Moors & Rutten ch.5
  • Coverage tests and top-ups. Value the goods realistically (Wynne suggests using a worst-case local-sale price), test them against the loan, and require extra collateral or prepayment within a set time if cover falls short. Wynne §5.11
  • Enforcement. Some legal systems allow quick self-help sale. Others require court orders and public auctions that can take months. Know this before structuring, and consider ownership structures if enforcement is weak. Wynne §5.10

How inventory deals go wrong

Rutten's account of a Singapore tin refiner shows the classic failures. The bank lent against tin dross, a by-product with no exchange price, using a formula valuation. The borrower "sold" dross to a shell company it controlled, using the bank's own money, and re-pledged the same material. When the receiver took possession, the dross turned out to be contaminated and worth less than nothing. The court found the bank had largely itself to blame. Moors & Rutten ch.5, Box 5.2 Other risks are multiple pledging of the same goods, forged or multiple forwarder's certificates of receipt (FCRs, which are not documents of title), warehouse liens for unpaid storage fees that can rank ahead of the bank's pledge, and commingling. Moors & Rutten ch.5 Wynne §5.15, §5.18

Ownership-based finance and repos

Instead of a pledge, the bank can own the goods by buying them and agreeing to resell them later at a price that includes its funding cost (a repo). The advantages are stronger rights in default (taking the goods becomes theft, not a contract dispute), sidestepping negative pledges, and better capital treatment. The disadvantages are the risks of ownership itself: environmental liability, licensing to trade the commodity, tax, and the risk that a court in the client's insolvency recharacterises the "sale" as a secured loan. Moors & Rutten ch.5 Trafigura CD ch.11 Refineries have used this structure to finance crude and product stocks, processing as agent for the bank. Moors & Rutten ch.20

Worked example 1 · a 30-day inventory repoChecked by code

A refiner sells $50 m of crude stocks to a bank with a 5% haircut and buys them back in 30 days at a 6% funding rate (actual/360). Rates are illustrative.

bank pays = 50 × 0.95 = $47.50 m; refiner repurchases at $47.7375 m; funding cost $237,500

The haircut is the borrower's equity in the stock, as in a securities repo. The bank usually hedges the price risk and has a backup buyer if the refiner fails to repurchase. Moors & Rutten ch.5

Borrowing-base facilities

A borrowing base is a revolving facility whose available amount moves with the value of a changing pool of collateral: goods in storage or transit, receivables and cash in the collection account. Each asset class carries its own advance rate reflecting how long, how risky and how costly it is to turn into cash. Wynne §2.6 Trafigura CD ch.11 Inventory and receivables are reported regularly, and the base is recalculated. If it falls below the amount drawn, the borrower must repay or add collateral.

Worked example 2 · an oil trader's borrowing baseChecked by code

Collateral with illustrative advance rates: hedged inventory $200 m at 90%, unhedged inventory $50 m at 70%, LC-backed receivables $150 m at 95%, credit-insured receivables $100 m at 85%, other receivables $60 m at 60%, cash $20 m at 100%.

gross collateral $580 m → borrowing base = $498.5 m drawn $470 m → headroom $28.5 m unhedged stock falls 30% → base $488.0 m (hedged stock and invoiced receivables hold value)

Advance rates reward exactly the risk mitigants discussed elsewhere: hedging (Course 3), LCs (Module 2) and credit insurance (Module 5). Unhedged, uninsured exposure attracts the deepest haircut. Wynne §2.6 Synthesis

Obligor concentration caps & IFRS 15 / ASC 606 repo accounting

In practice, banks do not allow open-ended receivables without eligibility filters: (1) past-due exclusion (invoices overdue > 60 days are written down to zero advance), and (2) obligor concentration caps (typically no single non-investment-grade buyer may exceed around 20% of the eligible pool). On the accounting side, IFRS 15 / ASC 606 say that if the seller has an obligation (a forward) or a right (a call) to repurchase the goods, the buyer has not obtained control, so there is no sale. Where the repurchase price is at or above the original price, as in a repo at cost plus funding, the deal is a financing: the inventory stays on the trader's balance sheet with a matching liability. Whether other inventory monetisation structures (market-price repurchases, substitutable barrels, supply-and-offtake deals) achieve sale treatment depends on a detailed control analysis and auditor judgement. Moors & Rutten ch.5 Wynne §2.6 Outside the texts

Worked example 2b · concentration caps and overdue haircutsChecked by code

A trader submits $100 m of gross receivables across 3 buyers to its borrowing-base agent (overdue > 60 days disqualified; single-obligor cap 20% of the pool left after that; 80% advance rate): Buyer A has $35 m current; Buyer B has $25 m current and $15 m past-due (75 days); Buyer C has $25 m current.

past-due exclusion (>60 d, Buyer B) = $15.0 m → current pool $85.0 m single-obligor cap = 20% × 85 = $17.0 m concentration exclusions (A: $18m, B: $8m, C: $8m) = $34.0 m eligible collateral (A: $17m, B: $17m, C: $17m) = $51.0 m net borrowing base (80% advance) = $40.8 m

Out of $100 m face value, $49 m is excluded by the eligibility filters, leaving a borrowing base of $40.8 m. A pool this concentrated in three buyers is exactly what the caps are designed to penalise. Without concentration limits, a single distressed buyer could collapse the entire lending syndicate. Wynne §2.6 Synthesis

Reserve-based lending

Upstream companies borrow against oil and gas still in the ground. Rutten describes reserve categories discounted for the risk of producing them on time and on budget, not for doubt that they exist: proved developed producing reserves at 0–10%, proved developed non-producing at 40–60%, and proved undeveloped at 60–80%. Price floors are often hedged. Moors & Rutten ch.20 Clews notes that the base is usually redetermined every six months with a full update of the financial model, often sized on cover ratios such as loan-life cover (Course 9). Clews §14.5, ch.16–17 The facility limit is set above the initial base so availability can grow as reserves are proved up. Moors & Rutten ch.20

Worked example 3 · an RBL baseChecked by code

Reserve values at the bank's price deck: PDP $300 m, PDNP $120 m, PUD $200 m. Apply discounts of 5%, 50% and 70%.

base = 300 × 0.95 + 120 × 0.50 + 200 × 0.30 = $405 m

Most of the capacity comes from producing barrels. Drilling a PUD location and bringing it online moves it from a 70% to a roughly 5% discount at the next redetermination. Moors & Rutten ch.20 Synthesis

ALM analogy

Advance rates are haircuts, as in a repo or a central-bank collateral schedule: deeper for less liquid, more volatile or harder-to-seize assets. A borrowing-base redetermination is a periodic collateral revaluation with a margin call when cover falls short. Synthesis

Dated: warehouse risk

The texts predate major inventory frauds such as the 2014 Qingdao metals scandal (the same metal pledged several times) and the 2020 collapses of several Singapore-based oil traders. These prompted banks to tighten collateral management, demand digital warehouse receipts and cut lines. US regulators also curbed bank ownership of physical commodities after 2013. Outside the texts Check ICC and industry guidance on warehouse receipts and current bank trade-finance policies.

Going deeper: setting an advance rate

A simple way to see an advance rate is as the share of today's value the lender can expect to recover in a forced sale after an adverse price move over the liquidation period. Roughly: one minus a price haircut (volatility × √(days to liquidate) × a confidence multiplier), minus liquidation costs, minus a buffer for fraud and operational loss. Synthesis Outside the texts Hedging removes most of the price haircut, which is why hedged stock gets a higher advance rate. Liquidation costs and legal enforceability, which Rutten and Wynne emphasise, often matter more than price volatility. Moors & Rutten ch.5 Wynne §5.10–5.11

Key takeaways

  • Inventory finance depends on genuine third-party control: field or public warehouses, collateral managers, and release only on the lender's order.
  • Fraud and enforcement are the main risks. Value non-exchange goods conservatively and know local enforcement law.
  • Ownership and repo structures strengthen the lender's position but bring the risks of owning the goods.
  • Borrowing bases apply asset-specific advance rates. RBL discounts reserves by development stage and redetermines every six months.
