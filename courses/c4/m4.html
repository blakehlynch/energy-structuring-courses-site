Pre-export finance and prepayments

Core≈ 65 min9 quiz questions7 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish PXF from prepayment structures.
  • Allocate risks under limited-recourse prepayments.
  • Size a prepayment by breakeven price and stress it.
  • Explain how structures lighten with track record.

Prerequisites

  • Modules 1–3.

Two structures, one idea

Both structures finance a producer against its future exports. The difference is who borrows. Wynne §2.2.4 Moors & Rutten ch.6

  • Pre-export finance (PXF). The producer borrows, assigns its export contracts and receivables to the lenders, and buyers pay into a lender-controlled collection account, usually offshore. The lender has full recourse to the producer, but its comfort comes from the sales flow. Wynne §2.3 Moors & Rutten ch.6
  • Prepayment. The buyer (a trader or an SPV) borrows from banks and pays the producer in advance for future deliveries. Each delivery reduces the prepayment. Because the buyer owns the goods once delivered and there is no loan to the producer, the structure can avoid withholding tax on interest, negative pledges and some restructuring risk. Wynne §2.4 Moors & Rutten ch.6

Rutten traces PXF to late-1980s deals by Bankers Trust and UBS for African producers. Assigning export proceeds from prime buyers to offshore accounts mitigated country risk, leaving lenders mainly with the producer's performance risk. Moors & Rutten ch.6 Rutten's account of Ghana's Cocobod facilities shows the result over two decades: margins fell from 1.25% over LIBOR to below 0.2%, and receivables cover from 150% to about 100%, as performance built trust. Spreads rose again in 2009. Moors & Rutten ch.6, Box 6.1

Trader-originated prepayments

In Prepayments Demystified, Trafigura explains why trading houses now originate many of these deals. The trader is both buyer and financial intermediary. It structures the prepayment, signs the supply contract and syndicates the loan to banks, typically keeping up to 10% of the risk as "skin in the game". Trafigura PD p.8, p.12 The loan is limited recourse: banks lend to the trader but rely mainly on the producer delivering. The producer gets dollars without currency conversion risk, one counterparty, a committed buyer and funding often cheaper than its own bank loans or bonds. Trafigura PD p.8–11 Wynne notes that financers typically ask the offtaker to keep 10–20% of production risk so it keeps pushing for deliveries. Wynne §2.4

Rutten sorts the risks that a limited-recourse prepayment usually leaves with the trader. Always shared: export-licence cancellation, confiscation and repudiation by the exporter. Sometimes shared: commercial non-performance, force majeure, insured political losses and duty increases. Almost never shared: price declines and delays without a shared-risk event. Never shared: the trader's own non-performance. Moors & Rutten ch.6

ALM analogy

A prepayment is an amortising secured loan with over-collateralisation, much like a covered bond. Deliverable volume times price is the cover pool, the contract volume exceeds what debt service needs, and a "top-up" obligation restores cover when the pool's value falls. Lenders watch a debt-service cover ratio month by month. Synthesis

Sizing a prepayment

Trafigura illustrates over-collateralisation with a $120 m prepayment against a contract worth about $180 m at signing, so that the producer can still perform even if prices fall 20%. If prices fall further or for longer, the contract provides for extension, extra volume ("top-up") or early cash repayment. Trafigura PD p.10, p.13 Rutten adds hedging. Sonangol's 1990s facilities used strings of put options so it could borrow about 85% of each cargo's expected value instead of roughly two-thirds. For a 950,000 bbl cargo expected at $75, that is about $60.6 m instead of roughly $47 m. Moors & Rutten ch.14 Synthesis

Worked example 1 · cover and breakeven priceChecked by code

A trader prepays $200 m to a producer, repaid over 24 months at 7% from deliveries of 150,000 bbl a month priced at the monthly average, assumed $75. Figures are illustrative.

monthly debt service (annuity) = $8.955 m; delivery value = $11.25 m → cover 1.256× contract value at signing = $270 m breakeven price = 8.955 m / 150,000 = $59.70 → prices can fall 20.4% before cover drops below 1×

The ratio of contract value to prepayment ($270 m vs $200 m) overstates the cushion, because interest must also be paid. The breakeven price is the right test. Synthesis Trafigura PD p.10

Worked example 2 · top-up after a 30% price fallChecked by code

If prices drop 30% to $52.50, deliveries of 150,000 bbl no longer cover debt service. Under a top-up clause the producer must deliver extra barrels each month:

extra volume = 8.955 m / 52.50 − 150,000 = 20.6 thousand bbl per month

A top-up works only if the producer has unencumbered production to spare, and delivering extra barrels at lower prices depletes the physical reserve faster, compressing the reserve tail (Course 9, Module 5). Trafigura names the volume of unencumbered production as a key consideration when structuring. Trafigura PD p.11, p.13 Wynne §2.4 Synthesis

Prepayment cash waterfall

Structure the deal, then stress the price

Prices fall linearly to the shocked level by month 6 and then stay there. Top-up volume is capped at the chosen share of contract volume. Any remaining shortfall would fall to the reserve account, an extension, or the risk-sharing terms.

Accounts, SPVs and evolving structures

  • Collection and reserve accounts. Buyers pay into a collection account. A reserve account holds the next debt service (and sometimes hedge settlements), and the excess goes back to the producer. Borrowers dislike cash idling at low interest. Strong borrowers negotiate "evidence accounts", where flows pass through only to prove they exist, which moves the deal towards balance-sheet lending. Moors & Rutten ch.6
  • SPVs can sit between producer and lenders as buyer, as in Sonangol's 2004 "Nova Vida" vehicle. The producer then sells as the SPV's agent to a list of eligible buyers instead of under rigid term contracts, with a price floor replacing cargo-by-cargo hedges. Moors & Rutten ch.6, Box 6.4 Trafigura notes SPVs for the largest deals, with the trader lending alongside the banks. Trafigura PD p.12
  • Lightening over time. Rutten's Sonangol history runs from offshore trusts, through SPV prepayments, to lightly structured receivables facilities, and finally to unsecured loans. Better track records bought flexibility rather than lower margins. Moors & Rutten ch.6, Box 6.4
  • Refinery prepayments. A trader can prepay a refinery for future products and sell it crude, financing the refinery's working capital. Glencore's 2013 prepayment to Morocco's Samir refinery is one example. Moors & Rutten ch.20
Dated: scale and scrutiny

Trader prepayments grew rapidly in the late 2010s; Trafigura reported more than $5 bn outstanding in 2019. They also drew scrutiny over sovereign debt transparency, especially oil-backed loans to state companies in heavily indebted countries. EITI and IMF work on disclosure of resource-backed lending, and several restructurings since 2020, lie outside the texts. Outside the texts Check EITI guidance, IMF and World Bank work on resource-backed loans, and trading houses' recent annual reports.

Going deeper: whose risk is it?

Breaking a prepayment into risk components shows what each party holds. Synthesis

  • Price risk: the producer and lenders through the cover ratio, mitigated by over-collateralisation, top-ups and floors. The trader usually doesn't bear it.
  • Performance risk: the lenders, with a trader slice of 10–20%.
  • Political risk: often insured (Module 5).
  • Payment risk on end-buyers: the trader, which earns its trading margin and supply security.

Trafigura's point that the trader's main motivation is reliable long-term supply, with banks earning the lending margin, follows from this allocation. Trafigura PD p.13 For an originator, the tool below shows the trade-off between cover (safety) and the amount advanced (the producer's appetite).

Key takeaways

  • In PXF the producer borrows against assigned exports. In a prepayment the buyer borrows and pays in advance for deliveries.
  • Lenders take performance risk. Payment risk sits with creditworthy buyers or the trader, and limited-recourse terms allocate the rest.
  • Size on breakeven price, not contract value. Use top-ups, floors and reserve accounts for downside.
  • Structures lighten as track records build: trusts, SPVs, receivables facilities, unsecured loans.
