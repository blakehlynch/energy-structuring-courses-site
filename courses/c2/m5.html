Crude differentials and value-in-use

Foundation≈ 60 min9 quiz questions12 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Decompose a physical crude price into marker and differential components.
  • Compute a crude's value-in-use and its breakeven differential versus a marker at a given refinery.
  • Show that the differential's sensitivity to each product price is the yield difference.
  • Explain how quality supply, conversion capacity, fuel-oil value, logistics and season move market differentials.
  • Outline a defensible long-term crude pricing formula for a producer.

Prerequisites

  • Modules 1–4.

Marker plus differential

Hundreds of crude grades are produced, but only a few are liquid enough to act as benchmarks (markers): Brent for much of the seaborne market, WTI in the US, and Dubai/Oman for Middle East sour crude sold to Asia. Most other grades are priced as marker ± differential. Synthesis Schofield ch.5 Long §4.1–4.2 Edwards ch.2.3 Kaiser Box 4.2 Kaiser lists what a benchmark needs: large volumes, consistent quality, secure supply, many sellers and broad acceptance by refiners. Kaiser Box 4.2

A floating physical price is typically built from: Edwards ch.2.3 Schofield ch.5

  • an index: a futures contract, a calendar-month average (CMA) of the prompt future, or a price-reporting-agency assessment averaged over a pricing window around the loading date;
  • a grade differential for quality versus the marker;
  • a location differential for the cost of getting the oil to (or from) the marker's delivery point;
  • any other negotiated premium or discount.

Some producers set official selling prices (OSPs) as differentials, and Schofield notes that Saudi Arabia has priced by destination (against WTI for the US, Brent for Europe) with restrictions on resale. Schofield ch.5 Course 3 covers pricing windows, partials and paper markets in detail. Here the question is what the differential should be.

Worked example 1 · an invoice on a floating formulaChecked by code

A 1,000,000 bbl cargo is priced at the average of Dated Brent over five days around the bill-of-lading date, minus $3.20. The five-day average is $81.35.

price = 81.35 − 3.20 = 78.15 $/bbl invoice = $78,150,000

The marker carries the flat-price risk, which is large and hedgeable. The differential carries the quality and location value, which is smaller, specific to the crude, and where an originator earns or loses money.

Value-in-use: what a crude is worth to one refinery

Modules 1–4 give everything needed to value a crude at a specific plant. The most a refiner can pay and break even is its gross product worth on that crude, less the crude-specific costs of getting it there and processing it. Kaiser §1.3, §1.6 Long §2.6.8

\[ V_{\text{crude}} = \underbrace{\sum_i y_i P_i}_{\text{GPW at this plant}} - c_{\text{processing}} - c_{\text{freight}}, \qquad \Delta_{B\text{ vs }A} = V_B - V_A \]

Kaiser uses this logic three ways: a simple refinery choosing between a light sweet and a medium sour crude (and inferring a value per degree API); a complex refinery solving for the price at which a sour crude matches a sweet crude's netback; and a published marker (Platts' Eagle Ford Marker) that prices a new shale stream from the product value of its yields relative to Louisiana Light Sweet. Kaiser §1.3, §1.6.1, §1.10 It is also the producer's view. A seller needs good models of refinery yields, product prices and competitors' crudes to know what discount wins the cargo. Kaiser §1.3, §1.7

Worked example 2 · pricing a heavy sour crude into a coking refineryChecked by code

A coking refinery currently runs a light sweet marker-linked crude. A producer offers a heavy sour grade. Illustrative yields, costs and product prices ($/bbl):

Heavy sour (B)3%36%7%33%13%10%3.701.40

*Coke credited at $8 per barrel-equivalent of crude, for illustration.

GPW_A = 98.66 breakeven V_A = 98.66 − 2.40 − 2.10 = 94.16 GPW_B = 90.29 breakeven V_B = 90.29 − 3.70 − 1.40 = 85.19 value-in-use differential Δ = V_B − V_A = −8.97 $/bbl if A trades at 76.50 (same price basis), the refinery is indifferent to B at 76.50 − 8.97 = 67.53

At any discount wider than about $9/bbl, the heavy crude improves this refinery's margin. A producer should therefore expect to clear the cargo somewhere near that level if this is the marginal buyer. A refinery without a coker would turn far more of B into fuel oil and value it much lower.

The differential as a replicating portfolio

Because GPW is linear in product prices, the value-in-use differential's sensitivity to each product price is just the yield difference between the two crudes:

\[ \frac{\partial \Delta_{B\text{ vs }A}}{\partial P_i} = y_{B,i} - y_{A,i} \]
Worked example 3 · what moves this differential?Checked by code
yield differences (B − A): LPG −0.01 gasoline −0.12 jet −0.03 diesel 0.03 fuel oil 0.05 coke 0.10 fuel oil falls $10/bbl → Δ changes by 0.05 × (−10) = −0.50 → new Δ = −9.47 $/bbl implied value per degree API (39° vs 24°): 8.97 / 15 = 0.60 $/bbl per °API

This heavy crude is "short gasoline, long fuel oil and coke" relative to the light marker. A weaker fuel-oil market or a stronger gasoline crack widens the discount. At a cracking refinery without a coker, B's fuel-oil yield would be much larger, so the same fuel-oil move would hit the differential several times harder.

The implied $/°API is specific to this crude pair, this refinery and these prices; it also bundles sulfur and other effects into the API variable. Kaiser's simple-refinery example arrives at a different figure for the same reason. Kaiser §1.3 Use per-degree escalators as contract mechanics, not as a valuation model.

ALM analogy

The yield-difference vector is the differential's exposure profile, like key-rate durations for a liability: each component tells you how much the differential moves when one point of the "product curve" moves. Hedging a differential exposure means holding the offsetting product spreads. The residual is model risk in the yields, much like prepayment or lapse model risk in ALM.

From one refinery's value to the market differential

A market differential is not any single refinery's value-in-use. It settles where the marginal buyer is indifferent: the refinery that needs the last cargo to clear. When heavy sour supply is plentiful, the marginal buyer is a less suitable refinery with a lower value-in-use, and discounts widen. When it is scarce, coking refiners compete for it and discounts narrow. The books describe the drivers from different angles. Synthesis Kaiser §1.7, §4.4.6 Long §4.1 Clews §7.3.1

  • Supply of each quality. Long's 1990s narrative shows light-heavy differentials widening abruptly when Saudi Arabia and others replaced lost Iraqi and Kuwaiti supply with heavier crude, then narrowing as North Sea light output grew. Long §4.1 Producer decisions about which grades to cut or add feed straight into differentials.
  • Conversion capacity. Refineries that cannot run heavy sour crude bid up light grades. More coking capacity raises demand for heavy crude and narrows its discount. Kaiser frames the light-heavy spread as a signal of relative availability and demand. Kaiser §1.7
  • Product cracks and fuel oil. Worked example 3 showed the mechanism: a weak residual-fuel market or strong light-product cracks widen discounts on residue-rich crudes. That is why changes in marine fuel rules matter for sour crude values. Kaiser §2.5, Box 15.2 Synthesis
  • Logistics and location. Kaiser shows WTI and Brent decoupling in 2010–2015 and again in 2018 because of storage and transport constraints around Cushing. Kaiser Box 4.2 Pipeline-bound inland grades can trade far below seaborne equivalents until infrastructure catches up.
  • Season. Schofield notes that a gasoline-rich crude can command a wider premium in summer, and a heating-oil-rich one in winter. Schofield ch.5
  • Market factors dominate. Kaiser argues that quality ("private factors") explains differentials of a few dollars to roughly ten dollars a barrel and changes slowly, while market factors move flat prices far more. Kaiser §4.4.5–4.4.6 Within that band, though, the differential is the originator's profit pool.
Producer netbacks

A producer can sell into several markets. For each it computes a netback, the delivered value less freight, losses and other costs back to the loading port, and sells where that is highest. Kaiser frames this as producers using refining margins to decide where to sell, what discounts to offer and how to compete. Schofield recounts how 1980s crude netback pricing, which guaranteed refiners a margin, encouraged refiners to run flat out, oversupplied product markets and fed a broad fall in product and crude prices. Kaiser §1.7 Schofield ch.5 Course 3 builds the full arbitrage and netback calculation with freight.

Dated: benchmarks and differentials

Schofield describes Brent as the BFOE basket (Brent, Forties, Oseberg, Ekofisk). It later added Troll and, in 2023, US WTI Midland delivered into Europe. US crude exports, banned until late 2015, now link WTI and Brent through export arbitrage. The specific differentials in Long (1990s) and Kaiser (to 2018) are history. Schofield ch.5 Long §4.1 Kaiser Box 4.2 Outside the texts Check the current Platts and Argus methodology guides, the EIA's crude price and spread data, and OIES papers on crude pricing (e.g. Fattouh) before relying on any benchmark mechanics.

Putting it together on the desk

A producer client brings a new stream and asks for a long-term pricing formula. A structurer would:

  1. Get a full assay (Module 1) and check spec-critical properties for target markets (Module 2).
  2. Identify candidate refineries by configuration (Module 3) and compute value-in-use for each (this module), using forward product cracks (Module 4) rather than today's spot.
  3. Estimate the marginal buyer and therefore the likely differential, then net back freight to compare markets.
  4. Write the formula as marker + differential, with quality escalators (per °API, per 0.1% S, TAN or metals limits), a pricing window, and possibly a periodic differential re-opener tied to an agreed basket of cracks. Hedge the flat price on the marker. The differential risk is what you are being paid to take.

Key takeaways

  • Physical crude is priced as marker ± differential. The marker carries flat-price risk; the differential carries quality and location value.
  • Value-in-use = GPW at a specific refinery − crude-specific costs. The differential between two crudes is the difference in value-in-use.
  • The differential's sensitivity to each product price equals the yield difference, so heavy crudes are effectively short light products and long residue.
  • Market differentials settle at the marginal buyer and move with quality supply, conversion capacity, fuel-oil value, logistics and season.
