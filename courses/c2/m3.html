Refinery configuration and complexity

Learning objectives

  • Describe what crude, vacuum, FCC, hydrocracker, coker, reformer, hydrotreating and alkylation units do and why each matters commercially.
  • Distinguish carbon-rejection from hydrogen-addition conversion.
  • Classify a refinery and compute its Nelson index and conversion capacity.
  • Explain the limits of complexity indices.
  • Value an incremental barrel at its marginal yield.

Prerequisites

  • Modules 1–2.

What a refinery does: separate, convert, finish

Every refinery does some combination of three jobs. Synthesis Kaiser §3.1 Long §2.6.2 Downey ch.7 Clews §7.2.1

  1. Separate. The crude unit (atmospheric distillation, ADU) heats crude to roughly 350–400 °C and splits it by boiling range into gases, naphtha, kerosene, gasoils and atmospheric residue. It cannot go hotter without cracking the oil uncontrollably. A vacuum unit (VDU) distils the residue again at low pressure, recovering vacuum gas oil (VGO) and leaving vacuum residue, the heaviest part of the barrel. Long §2.6.3 Kaiser §3.2
  2. Convert. Change the size or shape of molecules so that low-value heavy streams become gasoline and distillates. Kaiser §3.3
  3. Finish. Treat (mainly remove sulfur), upgrade octane, and blend components into on-spec products. Kaiser §3.4 Long §2.6.4, §2.6.6

Distillation alone leaves the refiner at the mercy of the crude. If a crude contains 20% gasoline-range molecules, a refinery with only a crude unit makes about 20% gasoline-range product. Downey ch.7 Kaiser §8.2 Conversion is what breaks that link.

Conversion: two routes to a lighter barrel

Heavy molecules are carbon-rich and hydrogen-poor. To make lighter products you either throw carbon away or add hydrogen. Downey ch.7 Kaiser §3.1

The finishing units matter just as much commercially. The catalytic reformer turns low-octane heavy naphtha into high-octane reformate and is usually the refinery's main source of hydrogen. Hydrotreaters consume hydrogen to remove sulfur. Alkylation and isomerization build or reshape light molecules into high-quality gasoline components. Kaiser §3.4 Long §2.6.5 Downey ch.7 When reformer hydrogen is not enough, which is usual for a refinery with a hydrocracker or heavy desulfurization duty, extra hydrogen comes from steam-methane reforming of natural gas. Downey ch.7 Kaiser §8.3 That makes natural gas a refining cost and one link between oil and gas economics. Synthesis

Measuring complexity: the Nelson index

Wilbur Nelson's idea (1960s) was to summarise a refinery in one number weighted by capital intensity. Each unit gets a complexity factor: its construction cost per barrel of capacity relative to the crude unit's. The refinery's index adds up the factors weighted by each unit's capacity relative to crude capacity. Kaiser §7.2–7.4 Clews §7.2.1

\[ \text{CI} = 1 + \sum_{i \neq \text{ADU}} \frac{Q_i}{Q_{\text{ADU}}}\,\text{CF}_i \qquad\qquad \text{Conversion capacity} = \frac{Q_{\text{FCC}} + Q_{\text{HC}} + Q_{\text{coker}}}{Q_{\text{ADU}}} \]

The Oil & Gas Journal factors Kaiser cites include, for example, 2 for vacuum distillation and hydrotreating, 5 for reforming, 6 for catalytic cracking, coking and hydrocracking, and 10 for alkylation. Kaiser §7.3.2 Kaiser's rough classes are below 2 topping, 2–5 hydroskimming, 5–14 complex, and above 14 very complex. Kaiser §8.1 Other books give rougher anchors. Downey cites about 2 for hydroskimming, 9 for cracking and 15 for full conversion. Clews describes the least complex refineries as around 5 and the most complex as 10 to 15. Treat all such bands as conventions. Synthesis Downey ch.7 Clews §7.2.1

Worked example 1 · computing a Nelson indexChecked by code

A 200 kb/d refinery has: vacuum unit 100, FCC 60, reformer 35, hydrotreating 150, alkylation 12 (all kb/d). Use the factors above.

CI = 1 + (100/200)×2 + (60/200)×6 + (35/200)×5 + (150/200)×2 + (12/200)×10 = 1 + 1.00 + 1.80 + 0.875 + 1.50 + 0.60 = 6.775 conversion = 60/200 = 30% add a 40 kb/d coker (factor 6): CI = 7.975 conversion = 50% instead expand the crude unit to 250 kb/d with the coker: CI = 6.58

Adding a coker lifts both complexity and conversion. Debottlenecking only the crude unit lowers the index, because every downstream unit is now smaller relative to crude capacity. Kaiser makes the same point with the "complexity equation": the index is linear in each unit's capacity but falls non-linearly as crude capacity grows. Kaiser §7.6

What the index does and does not tell you

  • It measures capital intensity, hence replacement cost and, roughly, insured value, and so it carries information for asset valuation. Kaiser §7.2.3
  • It does not measure margin, utilisation, how well units are matched to each other, or the specific technology. Kaiser §7.4 Kaiser shows that the published factors have barely been updated for decades and that factors computed from 2010–2012 US Gulf Coast project costs come out 2–4× higher for several units. Use the index for comparison, not for cost estimates, without fresh data. Kaiser §7.3.2, §7.3.5
  • Very large refineries are not necessarily complex. Kaiser's list of the world's biggest plants includes several with low conversion. Kaiser §7.5.3 In Europe, conversion is often expressed as "FCC-equivalent" capacity instead. Kaiser §7.5.5
ALM analogy

Complexity buys optionality. A coking refinery holds an embedded option on the light-heavy crude spread and on the product-minus-fuel-oil spread. It pays a high fixed premium (capital) and earns when those spreads are wide. A hydroskimmer is the short-optionality position: cheap to hold, exposed when fuel oil is weak. This is like comparing a block of policies with valuable guarantees to one without: you value it by the distribution of the spread, not its average. Courses 7 and 8 make this formal.

Yield flexibility and the incremental barrel

A complex refinery can shift its slate by changing cut points and unit severity: more gasoline in summer, more distillate in winter. Long §2.6.7 Downey ch.7 Downey puts the US seasonal swing at only about 2 percentage points of yield, so the flexibility is real but small. Downey ch.7 Long's illustrative tables show that changing the crude moves yields far more in a simple refinery, and that a complex refinery can cut fuel-oil output by more than half on the same crude. Long §2.6.7

A subtle point from Long matters for deal evaluation. Many complex refineries have more crude-unit capacity than their conversion units can absorb. Up to the point where conversion is full they earn complex yields; every extra barrel beyond that gets hydroskimming yields. That makes access to traded intermediates (VGO, atmospheric residue, naphtha) a source of flexibility in itself. Long §2.6.7

Worked example 2 · the incremental barrelChecked by code

A refinery's conversion units are full at 200 kb/d of crude, where it makes 85% gasoline plus distillate. Its crude unit can run 250 kb/d. Extra crude would see only hydroskimming yields of about 55% gasoline plus distillate (illustrative numbers).

average light-product yield at 250 kb/d = (200×85% + 50×55%) / 250 = 79%

The average still looks respectable, but the marginal 50 kb/d earns a hydroskimmer's margin, which can be negative when fuel oil is weak. Price incremental crude, or a processing deal, on its marginal yield, not the refinery average. Buying VGO to fill an under-used cracker can be worth more than running extra crude.

Operating realities

  • Capacity units. Barrels per stream day (bpsd) is the maximum rate on a running day. Barrels per calendar day (bpcd or b/d) allows for downtime and is often 85–95% of the stream-day rate. Utilisation is crude charge divided by capacity. Kaiser §1.2, §7.1 Long §2.6.1
  • Turnarounds. Major maintenance happens every few years and is concentrated in the spring and autumn "shoulder" seasons between the heating and driving peaks. Downey describes regional timing offsets between the US, Europe and Asia. Traders watch published turnaround schedules in their supply-demand models. Downey ch.7 Long §2.6.1
  • Location and logistics. Access to crude hubs, water and pipelines widens the set of crudes a plant can buy and markets it can reach. A landlocked plant fed by one pipeline is captive. Kaiser §1.1 Clews §7.3.2 Long §2.6.7
Why this matters for structuring

Clews notes that project lenders treat simple refineries with great caution because they can endure long periods of negative margins. Configuration is the main determinant of whether a refinery can earn sustainable gross margins. Clews §7.2.1, §7.3.1 Trafigura describes the industry consolidating toward export-oriented "super refineries" that can take dozens of crude types, while smaller plants in developed markets close or become terminals. Trafigura CD ch.8 Both trends shape who your counterparties are and which assets can be financed.

Dated: capacity and closures

Kaiser's capacity statistics and refinery rankings are as of 2018, and the OGJ complexity factors were last published publicly in 1998. Kaiser §7.3.2, §7.5 Since then, large new refineries have started up in Asia, the Middle East and Africa, and many older plants in Europe, the US and Australia have closed or converted. Outside the texts For current figures check the EIA Refinery Capacity Report (US), the IEA Oil Market Report's refining section, and company filings.

Key takeaways

  • Distillation sorts molecules; conversion (rejecting carbon or adding hydrogen) changes them; finishing makes them saleable. Hydrogen balance links refining to natural gas.
  • Configuration decides which crudes a plant can buy and how much fuel oil it makes. Coking refineries are built for heavy sour crude.
  • The Nelson index measures capital intensity, not profitability. Use it to compare plants, with caution about stale factors.
  • Value deals at the marginal barrel. Spare crude capacity beyond full conversion runs at simple-refinery yields.
