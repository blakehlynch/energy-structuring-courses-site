Refined products and specifications

Foundation≈ 55 min9 quiz questions16 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Rank the main products by value and explain why fuel oil and LPG face price caps.
  • Name the specifications that matter for gasoline, jet, diesel and fuel oil.
  • Convert $/t and ¢/gal to $/bbl using density.
  • Apply volume-linear and index-based blending rules (octane, RVP).
  • Explain how seasonal specs and quality giveaway create cost and trading opportunities.

Prerequisites

  • Module 1 (density and blending basics).

The product barrel

Refined products are grouped by volatility, from the lightest (most volatile) to the heaviest. Long §2.2 Kaiser ch.2 Downey ch.9

GroupMain usesWhat competes with it
LPG (propane, butane)Heating, cooking, petrochemicals, gasoline blending (butane)Natural gas and NGLs from gas processing, so LPG rarely earns a "lightest product" premium
Naphtha and gasolineMotor fuel; petrochemical feedFew substitutes in road transport today (see the dated note below)
Kerosene and jet fuelAviation, some heatingAlmost none in aviation
Gasoil, diesel and heating oilTrucks, rail, farm and marine engines; home heatingNatural gas in heating

Specialties: bitumen, lubes, wax, cokeRoads, lubricants, industrial carbonNiche markets with their own logistics

Two economic facts follow from this table. Synthesis Long §2.2–2.3 Kaiser §1.7 Clews §7.2.2

  1. Transport-fuel demand is inelastic in the short run. There are few substitutes, so a supply shock moves gasoline, jet and diesel prices a lot. Fuel oil and LPG face substitutes, so their prices are capped by coal, gas and other energy. That is why fuel oil normally trades below crude and why refiners invest to destroy it.
  2. Products are co-produced. Long compares a refinery to a butcher cutting a carcass: you cannot make the cuts you want without also making the ones you don't. Relative product prices can swing widely, but together they must support the crude price, because refiners will not run for long at negative margins. Long's emphasis is worth keeping: supply and demand act on products first, and crude prices follow product values, not the other way round. Long §2.3 Edwards adds that forcing more of one product out of the refinery floods the market with the others, which can push product prices in opposite directions. Edwards ch.2.3

Specifications: what makes a product saleable

A specification is a list of test results the product must meet, usually a maximum, a minimum or a range, measured by standard methods (ASTM, Energy Institute and others). Between traders, specs are negotiable. But the oil ends up in a fuel whose spec is usually fixed by law or by the end user, so in practice the final spec binds all the way up the chain. Long §2.4.1 Kaiser ch.16 A naphtha with the right boiling range still cannot be sold as gasoline unless it meets every gasoline requirement. Kaiser §4.9

Gasoline

  • Octane measures resistance to knock (premature ignition). Labs report a research octane number (RON) and a tougher motor octane number (MON). US pumps post their average, \((R+M)/2\), also called AKI or PON. Octane is about ignition behaviour, not energy content; ethanol raises octane but lowers miles per gallon. Kaiser §17.2 Edwards ch.2.3
  • Volatility, measured as Reid vapour pressure (RVP, in psi). Gasoline must vaporise to start a cold engine, but too much vapour causes vapour lock and smog-forming evaporation. Specs therefore cap RVP more tightly in summer than in winter, roughly from 7 to 13.5 psi depending on season and region. Kaiser §17.3, §20.2 Trafigura CD ch.8
  • Composition limits on sulfur, benzene and aromatics, plus oxygenate rules. In the US, refiners ship a blendstock for oxygenate blending (RBOB, or the conventional CBOB). Ethanol is added at the truck rack ("splash blending") because it picks up water in pipelines. Kaiser §20.7 Trafigura CD ch.8 Downey ch.7

Jet fuel

The key specs are freezing point, flash point, smoke point (burn quality) and very tight contamination control. Handling costs are higher, and kerosene can also go into gasoline or be used as a cutter, which partly explains why jet usually trades above diesel. Kaiser ch.18 Long §2.3 Physical jet is often priced as gasoil plus a "jet differential" and hedged with gasoil futures. Schofield ch.5

Diesel, gasoil and heating oil

The key specs are cetane number (ignition quality, the diesel counterpart of octane), sulfur, flash point, and cold-flow limits (cloud point, cold filter plugging point). Diesel and heating oil are essentially the same distillate, with diesel held to tighter sulfur and cetane limits. Kaiser ch.19 Edwards ch.2.3 Paraffinic gasoils have excellent cetane but poor cold flow; aromatic ones the reverse. Long §2.5.2

Fuel oil

Fuel oil is sold mainly on viscosity, sulfur and density. It is blended from residues and lighter "cutter stocks" to hit a viscosity and sulfur grade. A visbreaker earns its keep partly by reducing how much valuable distillate has to be used as cutter. Long §2.6.5–2.6.6 Marine bunker grades are traded by viscosity class (for example IFO-380), and the international sulfur limit on bunkers is set by the IMO under MARPOL Annex VI. Kaiser §2.5

Dated: specifications move

Long's product chapter dates from 1996–1998, when lead was being phased out of gasoline and 0.05% sulfur diesel was the coming standard. Today's road-fuel sulfur limits are ultra-low (on the order of 10–15 ppm in the US and EU). For marine fuel, Kaiser (written in 2019) anticipates the IMO's move from a 3.5% to a 0.5% sulfur cap on bunker fuel in 2020. It expects ships to switch to costlier low-sulfur fuel or marine gasoil, or to fit scrubbers, and expects pressure on visbroken fuel oil. Long §2.7 Kaiser §2.5, Box 15.2, ch.24 How the market actually adjusted after 2020 lies outside the texts. Outside the texts Verify current limits in ASTM D4814/D975/D1655, EN 228/EN 590 and IMO MARPOL Annex VI. The growth of electric vehicles is also eroding gasoline's "no substitute" status over the long run.

Units: per tonne, per barrel, per gallon

Europe and Asia quote most products in dollars per metric tonne, the US in cents per gallon, and crude almost everywhere in dollars per barrel. Contracts usually adjust for measured density and correct volumes to a standard temperature (15 °C or 60 °F). Long's example shows a gasoil cargo whose measured density came in slightly high: the cargo weighed the same but held fewer cubic metres, and the price adjustment was worth thousands of dollars. Long §2.3 Schofield ch.5

\[ \text{bbl per tonne} \approx \frac{6.2898}{\text{SG}} \qquad \$/\text{bbl} = \frac{\$/\text{t}}{\text{bbl per tonne}} \qquad \$/\text{bbl} = 42 \times \$/\text{gal} \]
Worked example 1 · comparing product prices across unitsChecked by code

Gasoil is offered at $720/t (density 0.845) and gasoline at $780/t (density 0.745). Which is dearer per barrel?

gasoil: 6.2898 / 0.845 = 7.444 bbl/t → 720 / 7.444 = $96.73/bbl gasoline: 6.2898 / 0.745 = 8.443 bbl/t → 780 / 8.443 = $92.39/bbl

Gasoline is dearer per tonne but cheaper per barrel, because a tonne of light product fills more barrels. Crack spreads (Module 4) are quoted per barrel, so convert before you compare. The 6.2898 factor treats SG as tonnes per cubic metre, which is close enough for a desk estimate. Contract conversions use the exact measured density at standard temperature.

Blending to specification

Few products leave a process unit ready to sell. Gasoline is typically blended from six to ten components (butane, light naphtha, isomerate, reformate, FCC naphtha, alkylate, ethanol and others), each strong on some properties and weak on others. Kaiser §20.1–20.2 Long §2.6.6 Downey ch.7 The refinery's goal is the cheapest recipe that just meets every spec. Overshooting a spec ("quality giveaway") gives away value for free. Kaiser §20.1 Trafigura CD ch.8

  • Some properties blend roughly linearly by volume: density, sulfur, and octane once you use blending octane numbers. Long §2.6.6 Kaiser §20.4
  • Others do not, notably vapour pressure, viscosity, flash point and cold-flow points. These use blending indices. A common index for RVP raises each component's RVP to the 1.25 power, averages by volume, then inverts. Kaiser §12.3.2, §20.3
\[ \text{RVP}_{\text{blend}} = \Big(\sum_i v_i\,\text{RVP}_i^{1.25}\Big)^{1/1.25} \]

Butane is the classic swing component: cheap, high octane, but very volatile. Refiners put in as much as the RVP cap allows, so the winter spec lets them blend in more cheap butane than the summer spec does. That is one reason summer-grade gasoline costs more to make, and gasoline cracks show a seasonal pattern. Kaiser §20.2, Box 1.1 Trafigura CD ch.8

Worked example 2 · how much butane fits under the winter cap?Checked by code

A terminal holds 100,000 bbl of gasoline blendstock at 7.8 psi RVP and 86.6 AKI. The winter spec allows 11.5 psi. Butane is 52 psi, about 91–92 AKI (an illustrative blending value), and costs $52/bbl against $88/bbl for the blendstock. How much butane can go in?

indices: 7.8^1.25 = 13.04 52^1.25 = 139.64 11.5^1.25 = 21.18 butane W = 100,000 × (21.18 − 13.04) / (139.64 − 21.18) = 6,873 bbl check: blend RVP = 11.50 psi ; blend AKI = 86.95 value: 6,873 bbl × ($88 − $52) ≈ $247,439 of cheap volume added

The extra volume is worth about a quarter of a million dollars on one tank, and it also lifts octane slightly. When the spec flips to summer this opportunity disappears. That seasonal pattern is how a trader who owns blending tankage earns money from the RVP calendar.

Worked example 3 · fixing an octane shortfallChecked by code

A regular-grade pool is 30% reformate (blending AKI 92.5), 35% FCC naphtha (85.0), 20% alkylate (94.0) and 15% light naphtha (70.0). The spec is 87.5 AKI. Is it on spec, and how much light naphtha must be swapped for alkylate?

pool AKI = 0.30×92.5 + 0.35×85.0 + 0.20×94.0 + 0.15×70.0 = 86.80 → 0.70 short each bbl swapped light naphtha → alkylate adds (94 − 70)/100 = 0.24 AKI per 100 bbl swap needed = 100 × 0.70 / 24 = 2.92 bbl per 100 bbl of pool

Because blending octane numbers are linear by volume, an octane shortfall can be priced as the cost of the cheapest swap that closes it. Desks call this the market value of an octane point; refinery planners call it a shadow price.

ALM analogy

Product specs work like eligibility rules for admissible assets or collateral: fail one test and the product cannot be sold under that name, however good its other properties. The blend recipe is a constrained optimisation, and each binding spec has a shadow price, much like the cost of a binding capital or duration-matching constraint. Quality giveaway is like holding capital above the requirement for no reason.

Non-linear fuel-oil viscosity blending (Refutas index)

Kinematic viscosity (\(\nu\), in centistokes at 50 °C) is strongly non-linear: a small dose of light cutter stock drops the viscosity of heavy vacuum residue dramatically. Refinery blenders linearise viscosity via the Refutas Viscosity Blending Index (VBI), average the index, and invert. Strictly, the Refutas index is blended on mass fractions; for components of similar density, volume fractions are a close approximation and are used below. Kaiser §20.5 Long §2.6.6 Outside the texts

\[ \text{VBI}(\nu) = 14.534\,\ln\!\big(\ln(\nu + 0.8)\big) + 10.975, \qquad \text{VBI}_{\text{blend}} = \sum_i v_i\,\text{VBI}(\nu_i), \qquad \nu_{\text{blend}} = \exp\!\Big(\exp\!\Big(\frac{\text{VBI}_{\text{blend}} - 10.975}{14.534}\Big)\Big) - 0.8. \]
Worked example 4 · cutter stock saving in 380 cSt bunker fuelChecked by code

A blender cuts vacuum residue (\(\nu_{\text{res}} = 5{,}000\text{ cSt}\) at 50 °C, $55/bbl) with light cycle oil cutter stock (\(\nu_{\text{cut}} = 4.0\text{ cSt}\), $95/bbl) to meet the IFO-380 marine bunker cap (\(\nu_{\text{target}} = 380\text{ cSt}\)). How much cutter stock is required under the Refutas VBI versus a naive linear viscosity average?

VBI(5000) = 42.11 VBI(4.0) = 17.52 VBI(380) = 36.88 Refutas cutter share = (42.11 − 36.88) / (42.11 − 17.52) = 21.3% (vs naive linear 92.5%) Refutas blend cost = 0.787 × 55 + 0.213 × 95 = $63.51/bbl

Because viscosity blends double-logarithmically, just 21.3% cutter stock brings 5,000 cSt residue down to 380 cSt—saving over 70 percentage points of expensive distillate compared with a linear estimate. Long §2.6.6 Synthesis

Regional markets and arbitrage limits

Because specs differ by country and even by US state, products are less fungible than crude. Edwards describes refined products as largely regional, with each region setting its own crack spread. Edwards ch.2.3 Trafigura describes the US gasoline map as "balkanised" into many local formulations, which raises costs but creates trading opportunities. Trafigura CD ch.8 Kaiser stresses the other side: shipping products between major hubs costs only a few dollars a barrel, so regional gasoline and diesel prices move together. Kaiser §4.9

Reconciling the books

Both are right at different horizons. Over weeks to years, arbitrage keeps hub prices within a transport-and-spec-conversion band, as Kaiser says. Inside that band, local spec rules, pipeline schedules and outages can open wide, persistent spreads, as Edwards and Trafigura describe. A physical originator is often paid to bridge exactly that band, by blending to a local spec, holding tankage or chartering a cargo. Edwards's claim that products trade internationally only in small volumes is likely dated; seaborne product trade has grown with export-oriented "super refineries" Trafigura CD ch.8. Check current product trade flows in IEA and EIA data. Outside the texts

Key takeaways

  • Transport fuels have inelastic short-run demand and few substitutes; fuel oil and LPG are capped by competing fuels. This price ordering is why refiners invest in conversion.
  • A product is saleable only if it meets every spec. Specs differ by region and season, and that fragmentation is both a cost and a trading opportunity.
  • Convert $/t and ¢/gal to $/bbl using density before comparing anything.
  • Blending is a constrained optimisation: cheap components such as butane are used up to the binding spec, and seasonal spec changes shift costs and margins.
