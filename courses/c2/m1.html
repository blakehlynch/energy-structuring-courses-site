Crude quality and the assay

Foundation≈ 50 min9 quiz questions13 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Convert between specific gravity and API gravity and classify a crude.
  • Explain how sulfur, acidity, metals and carbon residue affect which refinery can use a crude.
  • Read the distillation (TBP) profile of an assay and explain cut points.
  • Blend crudes correctly: SG and cuts by volume, sulfur by mass, never API directly.
  • Explain why an assay is an estimate and how to protect a contract against quality drift.

Prerequisites

  • None: this is the course entry point.
  • Comfort with weighted averages.

Why a barrel of crude has no single price

Nobody burns crude oil directly in volume. A refiner buys it for the molecules inside, which become gasoline, jet fuel, diesel, fuel oil and the rest. So a crude grade is worth what its products sell for, less what it costs to separate, convert and clean them. Two grades loaded on the same day can differ by several dollars a barrel because their molecule mix differs. Kaiser §12 intro Long §2.5 Downey ch.2

A physical originator meets this in every deal: a producer wants a price for a new stream, a refinery asks whether a cargo fits its plant, a trader blends two grades to hit a buyer's spec. The language of all three is the assay, the lab analysis of a crude grade. This module teaches you to read one.

ALM analogy

Think of a crude as a liability block and the assay as its cash-flow projection. Its value is the value of the "replicating portfolio" of products it decomposes into, net of processing cost. Single summary numbers (API gravity, sulfur) are like duration: useful, but they lose information. Two crudes with the same API gravity can yield very different product slates, just as two portfolios with the same duration can have very different cash-flow shapes.

Density and API gravity

Density is the first property any trader quotes, because heavier (denser) crude contains bigger molecules, and bigger molecules mean less gasoline and diesel from simple distillation and more residue that needs expensive upgrading. Kaiser §12.1.2 Downey ch.2

The US industry quotes density as API gravity, an inverted and stretched version of specific gravity (SG, density relative to water at 60 °F):

\[ \text{API} = \frac{141.5}{\text{SG}} - 131.5 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \text{SG} = \frac{141.5}{\text{API} + 131.5} \]

Higher API means lighter oil. Water sits at 10 °API. Most crudes have SG between about 0.80 and 0.90, and the API scale spreads that narrow range over roughly 26 to 45 degrees, which is easier to talk about. Kaiser §12.1.2

Worked example 1 · converting densityChecked by code

A Gulf of Mexico stream is assayed at SG 0.855. Another grade is quoted at 22.0 °API. Convert each.

API = 141.5 / 0.855 − 131.5 = 34.00 °API → medium SG = 141.5 / (22.0 + 131.5) = 0.9218 → heavy

No universal cut-offs. The books draw the light / medium / heavy boundaries in different places. Kaiser notes the thresholds are "not universal" and uses roughly >35–40 °API for light and <23–27 °API for heavy. Downey and Trafigura's guide each give their own bands. Synthesis Kaiser §4.7.1.1, §12.2 Downey ch.2 Trafigura CD ch.8 On a desk, quote the number rather than the label.

Price rises with API, then falls

Holding sulfur constant, crude value generally climbs a few dollars per degree of API up to roughly 45–50 °API, then turns down. Very light condensates boil in a narrow range: they give lots of naphtha but little diesel or jet. Kaiser reports US Gulf Coast condensate selling at large discounts to light crude, and posted-price schedules that deduct value above a gravity threshold. Kaiser §4.7, §1.10, Box 4.1

Where the books disagree

Downey calls condensates especially valuable, the "champagne" of crudes, because they refine easily into light products. Kaiser shows that the market often prices them at a discount. Downey ch.3 Kaiser §4.8 Both are true in context. Condensate is excellent naphtha feed (petrochemicals, gasoline blending) but a poor fit for a refinery that needs middle distillates. Its value depends on who is buying, which is the theme of Module 5.

Sulfur: sweet and sour

Sulfur must be removed to meet fuel specifications and to protect catalysts. Removing it needs hydrotreating capacity and hydrogen, so high-sulfur ("sour") crude needs more processing than low-sulfur ("sweet") crude and sells at a discount, all else equal. Kaiser §4.7.1.2, §12.1.3 Long §2.5.2

  • Thresholds. Below about 0.5 wt% is usually called sweet and above about 1 wt% sour, with "medium sour" in between. These conventions also vary by source. Synthesis Kaiser §4.7.1.2 Downey ch.2
  • Where it sits. Sulfur concentrates in the heavier, higher-boiling fractions. A crude can have clean naphtha but a sulfur-laden vacuum gas oil. Removing sulfur from distillates is fairly easy; removing it from residue is hard. So making low-sulfur fuel oil generally requires starting from a low-sulfur crude. Kaiser §4.7.1.2 Long §2.5.2
  • Correlation, not law. Heavy crudes tend to be sour and light crudes sweet, but the relationship is noisy. Heavy sweet grades exist and so do light sour ones. Kaiser §4.7.1.2, §4.8
A naming trap

Grade names are marketing. Saudi "Arab Light" is around 33 °API, medium rather than light. Kaiser §1.6, §4.8 Downey ch.2 The books even disagree on its sulfur class. Downey lists Arab Light among sweet examples, but Kaiser gives about 1.8–1.9 wt% sulfur, which is sour by every threshold above. Synthesis Downey ch.2 Kaiser §1.3, §4.8 Always read the assay, not the name.

Reading an assay

An assay is prepared by or for the producer to market the grade. It reports whole-crude properties plus properties of each distillation cut. Downey ch.2 Kaiser §13–14

The distillation (TBP) curve

Heat crude step by step and record the share that has boiled off at each temperature. The result is the true boiling point (TBP) curve, the crude's fingerprint. Slice it at cut points and you get the fractions a simple distillation would yield: LPG, light and heavy naphtha, kerosene, gasoil, vacuum gas oil (VGO) and residue. Cut points are not standardized; each lab and refiner sets its own, so check them before comparing two assays. Kaiser §3.2 Long §2.5.2

Long's comparison shows why API alone is not enough: grades with similar gravity can have very different distillate yields, and only crudes of the same family track API closely. Long §2.5.2 Assays also report yields by volume and by weight. They differ most at the ends of the barrel, because light ends are less dense and residue is denser. European refiners have tended to work in weight and US refiners in volume. Long §2.5.2

An assay is an estimate, not a specification

Most traded grades are blends of several fields sharing a pipeline and terminal. As field output rises and falls, the blend drifts, and the published assay may not be updated. Long §2.5.3 Downey ch.2 Crude contracts usually describe quality loosely, so a drift can stay "within contract" yet still cost a refiner money. Long §2.5.3 Crude that misses its usual range ("off-spec") can need re-routing or blending, and a cargo with no ready buyer becomes a distressed cargo that trades at a steep discount. Downey ch.2

Blending crudes: what adds up and what doesn't

Refiners blend crudes to feed a stable diet, to use a preferred grade without exceeding equipment limits, or to hit a product quality. Traders blend to manufacture a grade a buyer wants. Trafigura's guide describes blending heavy and light streams into a substitute for a scarce grade, and producers blending diluent into heavy oil so it can move by pipeline. Kaiser §12.3 Trafigura CD ch.8 Long §2.6.7

  • Additive on a volume basis: specific gravity (density) and distillation cut yields.
  • Additive on a mass basis: sulfur and other weight-percent impurities. Weight each component by its volume × SG.
  • Not additive: API gravity (the scale is non-linear), viscosity, pour point, flash point and vapour pressure. These use blending indices or test blends. Kaiser §12.3.1–12.3.2 Long §2.6.6

There is also a stability risk. Mixing some crudes can make asphaltenes drop out of solution and foul tanks and equipment, so a blend that works on paper can fail in the tank. Kaiser Box 12.5

Worked example 2 · blending a light sweet and a heavy sour gradeChecked by code

A trader blends 60,000 bbl of a light sweet grade (44.0 °API, 0.15 wt% S) with 40,000 bbl of a heavy sour grade (22.0 °API, 2.60 wt% S). What is the blend's API gravity and sulfur?

SG_light = 141.5 / (44.0 + 131.5) = 0.8063 SG_heavy = 141.5 / (22.0 + 131.5) = 0.9218 SG_blend = 0.6 × 0.8063 + 0.4 × 0.9218 = 0.8525 API_blend = 141.5 / 0.8525 − 131.5 = 34.48 °API (naive average of APIs would give 35.20 °API — wrong) S_blend (mass-weighted) = (0.6×0.8063×0.15 + 0.4×0.9218×2.60) / 0.8525 = 1.21 wt% (a volume-weighted average would give 1.13 wt%)

The blend is a medium sour crude. Averaging API directly overstates its lightness by about 0.7 degrees, and weighting sulfur by volume understates it. On a quality-escalator contract (price adjusted per degree API or per 0.1% sulfur) those errors are real money. If the two assays give cut yields, those blend linearly by volume. With illustrative yields of 34/14/22/22/8 and 10/7/16/27/40 vol% (naphtha/kero/gasoil/VGO/resid), the blend gives 24.4 / 11.2 / 19.6 / 24.0 / 20.8 vol%.

Outside the texts

Key takeaways

  • A crude's value is the value of its product slate less processing cost. The assay is the document that lets you estimate it.
  • API gravity and sulfur are the headline numbers, but cut yields, acidity, metals and carbon residue decide which refinery can use the crude and at what discount.
  • Blend SG and cut yields by volume and sulfur by mass. Never average API directly.
  • Assays are estimates of a drifting blend. Write quality adjustments into contracts rather than relying on the grade name.
