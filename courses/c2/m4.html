Refining margins and crack spreads

Foundation≈ 70 min10 quiz questions12 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Compute GPW, gross margin, net margin and netback, and say what each includes.
  • Compute 3-2-1, 2-1-1 and single-product cracks and explain why a crack is not profit.
  • Explain the main drivers of cracks, including seasonality and mean reversion.
  • Design a refiner's crack hedge and identify residual basis risks.
  • Value the right to idle as a spread option and connect it to tolling structures.

Prerequisites

  • Modules 1–3.
  • Normal distribution basics (for the option section).

Margin vocabulary

A refinery is a spread business. It buys one commodity, sells a basket of others, and lives on the difference. The terms below are standard, but every company draws the cost lines slightly differently, so always ask what is inside a quoted margin. Synthesis Kaiser §1.1–1.5 Long §2.6.8 Clews §7.3.1

TermDefinition used in this courseNotes across the books
Gross product worth (GPW)\(\sum_i y_i P_i\): each product's yield (fraction of crude volume) times its priceYields can sum to more than 100% by volume because cracking expands volume. Kaiser §1.4 Downey ch.7
Gross refining margin (GRM)GPW − delivered crude costLong treats refinery fuel as a yield loss inside the GRM. Clews nets selling and distribution costs off product prices first. Long §2.6.8 Clews §7.3.1

Crack spreads: the market's margin proxy

A crack spread is a stylised gross margin: a fixed recipe of products sold against crude bought, computed from benchmark prices. Notation X-Y-Z means X barrels of crude make Y barrels of gasoline and Z of distillate. Kaiser §1.8 Edwards ch.2.3 Schofield ch.5

\[ \text{3-2-1 crack} = \frac{2\,P_{\text{gasoline}} + 1\,P_{\text{distillate}} - 3\,P_{\text{crude}}}{3} \quad (\$/\text{bbl}) \]

Common recipes include 3-2-1 (the classic US complex-refinery proxy), 2-1-1, 5-3-2 and 6-3-2-1 (adding fuel oil). Single-product cracks, such as gasoline minus crude or gasoil minus Brent, are quoted too. Kaiser §1.8 Edwards ch.2.3 Schofield ch.5 Each region has its own benchmark set: US Gulf Coast products against WTI or LLS, Northwest Europe against Brent, Singapore against Dubai. Each region's crack reflects its own product balance. Kaiser §1.9 Edwards ch.2.3 Schofield ch.5

A crack is not a profit

A crack ignores operating cost, the plant's real yields, by-products such as fuel oil, coke and LPG, and the crude actually run. It is a hedgeable index of margin conditions, not a refinery's P&L. Kaiser warns that it is commonly misread as profitability. Kaiser §1.8

Worked example 2 · computing cracksChecked by code

WTI is $72.00/bbl, Gulf Coast gasoline $2.45/gal, ULSD $2.70/gal.

gasoline = 2.45 × 42 = 102.90 $/bbl ULSD = 2.70 × 42 = 113.40 $/bbl 3-2-1 = (2×102.90 + 113.40 − 3×72.00)/3 = 34.40 $/bbl 2-1-1 = (102.90 + 113.40 − 2×72.00)/2 = 36.15 $/bbl gasoline crack = 30.90 diesel crack = 41.40

The 2-1-1 is higher than the 3-2-1 here only because diesel is the stronger product. A refinery whose real slate is diesel-heavy is better tracked, and better hedged, by the recipe closest to its yields.

What moves cracks

  • Short-run product supply and demand. Transport-fuel demand is inelastic, so outages, hurricanes and unexpected turnarounds spike cracks. Kaiser §4.9 Edwards ch.2.3 Downey ch.7
  • Seasonality. US gasoline cracks tend to firm into the summer driving season. The tighter summer RVP spec makes summer gasoline costlier to produce, as Module 2 showed. Heating-oil demand lifts distillate in winter. Kaiser Box 1.1 Edwards ch.2.3 Schofield ch.5
  • The investment cycle. High margins draw new capacity and imports; overcapacity then compresses margins until closures restore balance. Kaiser §4.9 Clews §7.2.2
  • Co-production. Squeezing out more of one product floods the market with the others, so individual products can move against each other even while the overall margin holds. Edwards ch.2.3 Long §2.3
  • Mean reversion. Because refiners can raise or cut runs and capacity eventually adjusts, Edwards argues that crack spreads tend to oscillate around the cost of conversion rather than wander freely. That makes mean-reverting models, rather than random walks, the natural starting point. Edwards ch.3.6 Synthesis

Who is long the crack, and how it is hedged

A refiner is naturally long the crack: it gains when products rise relative to crude. To lock in margin it sells the crack: buy crude futures or swaps and sell product futures or swaps, usually with product legs dated a little later to reflect processing time. Product consumers (airlines, truckers) are short products. A crack trade can also let a product user lock a spread without taking a large outright crude position. Edwards ch.2.3 Schofield ch.5

  • Exchange cracks. Crack spreads trade as packaged strategies with lower margin requirements than the separate legs, because the legs offset each other's risk. Edwards ch.2.3
  • OTC margin swaps. A refiner can swap its own product basket, weighted by its yields, against its crude benchmark, receiving a fixed margin and paying the floating one. This covers products, such as naphtha and fuel oil, that have no liquid futures. Schofield ch.5
  • Basis risk remains. Futures deliver at Cushing and New York Harbor while the refinery buys and sells on the Gulf Coast. Grades differ from the contract specification. Plant yields differ from the 3-2-1 recipe. Each mismatch is a residual exposure. Schofield ch.5
Worked example 3 · a crack swap hedgeChecked by code

In spring a refiner sells a 3-2-1 crack swap for next quarter at $28.00/bbl on 1.5 million bbl per month, receiving fixed and paying the monthly average floating crack.

month A: floating crack 22.50 → swap pays refiner (28.00 − 22.50) × 1.5m = $8,250,000 month B: floating crack 33.00 → swap pays refiner (28.00 − 33.00) × 1.5m = $−7,500,000 (refiner pays) hedged crack on the hedged barrels = 22.50 + 5.50 = 28.00 (and likewise 33.00 − 5.00)

On the hedged volume the refiner earns $28 whatever happens. It gives up the $33 month to be protected in the $22.50 month. What it still owns is the gap between its actual margin and the 3-2-1 index: crude quality, real yields, location and timing basis.

ALM analogy

Hedging a refinery margin is like hedging the net interest margin of a spread business. You protect the spread between what you earn on assets and pay on liabilities, not the level of rates. Basis risk is the mismatch between the hedge index (the benchmark curve, the 3-2-1 recipe) and your actual book (your yields, your locations). The hedge ratio plays the role of how much duration gap you choose to close.

The refinery as a spread option

Long observes that 85–90% of a refinery's cash operating costs are fixed. Once the plant exists, it keeps running as long as the margin covers the small variable cost, because any surplus contributes to fixed costs. Long §2.6.8 Turn that around: the owner has the right, not the obligation, to process crude each period, and runs only when the crack beats variable cost. That is a strip of spread options on the crack, struck at variable cost. Tolling contracts in power and refining are valued the same way. Courses 7 and 8 develop the models, following Mahoney's treatment of tolling as an operational option (bounded from below by simple spread-option strategies) and Swindle's chapter on tolling deals. Mahoney §4.2 Swindle ch.14 Edwards ch.3.6 Synthesis

If next month's crack \(C\) is roughly normal with mean \(m\) and standard deviation \(s\), and \(k\) is variable cost, the expected value of running only when profitable is the Bachelier-type formula

\[ \mathbb{E}[\max(C-k,0)] = (m-k)\,\Phi(d) + s\,\varphi(d), \qquad d = \frac{m-k}{s}, \qquad s = \sqrt{(F_{\text{prod}}\sigma_{\text{prod}})^2 + (F_C\sigma_C)^2 - 2\rho\,F_{\text{prod}}F_C\sigma_{\text{prod}}\sigma_C}\sqrt{T}. \]

A normal (not lognormal) model suits a spread, because spreads can be negative. Notice how the spread standard deviation \(s\) depends on the correlation \(\rho\) between product and crude returns: because the refiner is long product and short crude, a drop in crude–product correlation \(\rho\) widens \(s\) and increases the option value of the refinery (the plant is short correlation). Edwards ch.3.6 Mahoney §4.2 Synthesis

Worked example 4 · what is the right to idle worth & correlation vega?Checked by code

Next month's plant-specific crack is uncertain with mean $12 and standard deviation $6. Variable cost is $9/bbl.

always run: m − k = 12 − 9 = 3.00 $/bbl d = (12 − 9)/6 = 0.50 Φ(0.5) = 0.6915 φ(0.5) = 0.3521 run only if profitable: 3 × 0.6915 + 6 × 0.3521 = 4.19 $/bbl value of flexibility = 4.19 − 3.00 = 1.19 $/bbl Monte Carlo check (400,000 draws, fixed seed) = 4.20 $/bbl (within sampling error) on 150 kb/d for a year: 1.19 × 150,000 × 365 ≈ $65.0 million two-leg correlation sensitivity (F_prod=$92, σ_prod=32%, F_C=$80, σ_C=30%, T=1/12 yr): at ρ = 0.90: crack std dev s = $3.77/bbl → run option = $3.46/bbl at ρ = 0.50: crack std dev s = $7.83/bbl → run option = $4.85/bbl

The flexibility value grows with volatility and falls with crude–product correlation: when product decouples from crude (\(\rho\) drops from 0.90 to 0.50 during a refining outage or product squeeze), \(s\) nearly doubles and the monthly option value jumps from $3.46 to $4.85/bbl. This is why a deep-conversion plant facing volatile cracks is worth more than a static average-margin calculation suggests. Edwards ch.3.6 Synthesis

ALM analogy

This is the embedded-guarantee calculation from life insurance turned around. The plant owner holds a floor at variable cost (losses are capped by idling), so expected value exceeds "value at the expected crack" by a convexity term that rises with volatility. That gap is the time value of a guarantee, valued with the same machinery.

Mean-reverting crack (Ornstein–Uhlenbeck), monthly swaps sold at their fair forward level

Model: the monthly crack follows \(dC = \kappa(\mu - C)\,dt + \sigma\,dW\), simulated exactly with a fixed seed. Each month's swap is sold at the model's expected crack for that month, so hedging changes risk but not the expected margin. Parameters are illustrative. Course 6 covers calibration.

Volume: 150 kb/d ≈ 4.56 million bbl per month for 12 months; 2,000 simulated paths. The plant runs every month (no idling) so the hedge effect is isolated.

Merchant versus tolled

A merchant refinery buys crude and sells products on short-term or spot terms and keeps the whole crack exposure. In a tolling (processing) agreement, the owner processes a customer's crude for a fee, typically with fixed and variable components covering costs and a return on capital. The customer supplies crude, takes the products and keeps the price risk. Clews explains why lenders prefer tolled structures: they make cash flows predictable, so the toll should run at least as long as the debt, and lenders need the customer to be creditworthy and termination rights to be tightly limited. Clews §7.4, §7.4.6

Structuring view

A toll is a sale of the plant's spread option to the customer, in exchange for a fixed premium (the fee). The fee should reflect the option value from Worked example 4, not just the expected margin. A toll priced on the average crack gives the customer the flexibility for free. Tolls in power and refining share this logic. Course 7 values them and Course 9 finances them. Synthesis Clews §7.4.6 Mahoney §4.2

Dated: margins, costs and contracts

Long's refinery cost figure (about $2.50/bbl cash plus depreciation in 1998), Schofield's margin chart (1992–2004) and Kaiser's regional margins to 2017 are historical. Contract names have also moved on: NYMEX's heating-oil contract now settles on ULSD and ICE gasoil is a low-sulfur grade. Long §2.6.8 Schofield ch.5 Kaiser §1.9 Outside the texts Current crack and margin levels are in the EIA's Short-Term Energy Outlook and refinery data, the IEA Oil Market Report's margin series, and refiners' 10-K filings, which report their own indicator margins.

Key takeaways

  • Know the stack: GPW → gross margin → net margin → netback. Ask what each quoted margin includes.
  • Crack spreads are standardised, hedgeable margin indices. They differ from any real plant's margin by yields, quality, location and timing.
  • Refiners are long the crack and hedge by selling it, using exchange cracks or OTC margin swaps. Basis risk is what remains.
  • Because most costs are fixed, a refinery is a strip of spread options on the crack. Flexibility has value that rises with volatility, which is the bridge to tolling, structured products and real options.
