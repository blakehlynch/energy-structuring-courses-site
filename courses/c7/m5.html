Swing and variable-volume contracts

Learning objectives

  • Value a swing option with limited rights and explain its bounds.
  • Explain take-or-pay penalties, nominations and uneconomic exercise.
  • Compute the uplift on a load-following price from load–price correlation.
  • Describe unit-contingent risk and why it resists hedging.

Prerequisites

  • Modules 1–4; Course 6 Module 3.

Volumetric options

Many gas contracts let the buyer vary the volume taken. Eydeland and Wolyniec describe three related rights. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.8 (pp.308–309, 320)

  • Swing: the right to move daily volume within a band (say 5,000–15,000 MMBtu around a 10,000 nomination) a limited number of times, paying a fixed or index-linked price.
  • Recall: the supplier's right to interrupt a few times, for example during a supply emergency.
  • Nomination: the right to reset the base volume for the rest of the period, a few times a year.

Contracts often add take-or-pay limits on total volume, with penalties or make-up obligations for breaches, and ratchets on how fast daily volume can change. Kaminski, Gibner and Pinnamaneni give a typical example: a 30-day contract with a daily range of 5,000–15,000 and a monthly range of 200,000–300,000 MMBtu. Kaminski (ed.) ch.3 Combined, these rights form a compound option, because the right nomination depends on the value of the swing and recall rights. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.8 (p.309)

Valuing swing: many exclusive American options

If the swing rights equal the number of days, the swing is just a strip of daily European calls. With fewer rights you face the American-option trade-off: exercise now, or keep the right for a better day. The exercise boundary depends on both time and the rights left. Arrive at day 25 with five rights and you exercise whenever in the money. Arrive with one, and you are pickier. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.8 (pp.310–311) Because the payoff is linear in volume, it is optimal to swing fully each time (all the way to the band limit). The value also scales with the contract size. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.8 (p.311) The classic algorithm is a "forest" of binomial or trinomial trees, one for each number of rights used, solved by backward induction. Monte Carlo with Longstaff–Schwartz regression handles richer price processes. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.8 (pp.311–314) Kaminski (ed.) ch.3

Two bounds help. Per right, a swing option is worth at least a daily European call and at most an American call on the whole period. With as many rights as days it equals the European strip, and with one right it equals the American (Bermudan) option. A quick estimate interpolates between them. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.8 (pp.316–317)

one right (American bound): $0.3037; 5 rights: $0.2808 per right; 10 rights: $0.2535; 31 rights (European strip): $0.1545 per right five rights × 10,000 MMBtu swing volume = $14,038

With mean reversion, a high price today is likely to fade, so exercising early pays. Rights are worth far more than the average daily European when they are scarce. Selling five rights "flat to daily options" would give away almost half their value. Eydeland and Wolyniec warn about exactly this for power interruptibles, whose value comes mainly from spikes. What drives swing value

Eydeland and Wolyniec stress the net convenience yield. With no reversion and a flat or rising curve, the early-exercise premium is zero. Value then comes from exercising at the end of the period, where volatility has built up most (the end-of-period premium). Strong reversion or a backwardated month makes early exercise worthwhile. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.8 (pp.315–317) In gas the American–European gap is usually small (at most about 10%) for typical parameters. In power, spikes dominate: almost all value comes from the number and size of spikes, and a simple threshold rule (exercise whenever price clears a level well above normal) is nearly optimal. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.8 (p.317) Market quotes for swing could be as much as 50% above daily options for the same period. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.8 (p.317)

Hedging and exercise behaviour

A strip of daily options for the full swing volume always super-replicates a swing, but that is expensive when rights are few. In practice desks hedge the end-of-period part with prompt-month options, and the early-exercise part with BOM and index or daily options. The first works much better than the second. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.8 (p.318) Customers often exercise for operational need rather than price ("uneconomic exercise"). If exercise follows demand only, the contract is a load-serving obligation with caps and floors, not an option, and must be valued as such. Most counterparties mix the two, so valuation depends on knowing the customer. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.8 (pp.309, 320–321)

Load-following and variable-quantity deals

Retail suppliers and utilities sell power or gas at a fixed price for whatever the customer consumes. Demand is positively correlated with price, so the supplier buys more exactly when prices are high. That correlation works against the holder, and the fair fixed price is above the volume-weighted forward. Swindle ch.15 (pp.376–377) Volume is uncommoditised: derivatives on load don't trade, so the residual risk is managed econometrically and priced with a risk adjustment. Swindle ch.15 (p.377) With an exponential stack \(p = p^* e^{\lambda L}\) and normal load, the uplift is \(1 + \lambda\sigma_L^2/\mu_L\). Historically PJM uplifts were around 1.03. Swindle ch.15 (pp.386–388)

Worked example 3 · load-following upliftChecked by code

Load averages 2,000 MW with a 300 MW standard deviation. The stack doubles price for about every 1,155 MW (λ = 0.0006 per MW), and the forward is $50.

uplift = 1 + 0.0006 × 300² / 2,000 = 1.027 → fair fixed price $51.35/MWh before risk premium

The hedge needs more than the expected volume, because delta exceeds expected notional. Shaping coefficients estimated from a few years of prices carry estimation error that Swindle puts near 10%, so calibrating to temperature with long weather histories is better. Swindle ch.15 (pp.385, 402)

Two more volume risks deserve respect. Load growth: in 2008–09 weather-normalised PJM peak load fell often more than 10% below estimates while prices fell about 60%, leaving suppliers long. Swindle reckons 1,000 MW of peak load lost over $5m for every 1% drop. Swindle ch.15 (pp.396–397) Unit-contingent sales deliver whatever a specific plant produces. Outages are rare, long and poorly estimated from history, and the only remedies are diversification and outage insurance, which is usually capped. Swindle ch.15 (pp.399–402)

Worked example 4 · unit-contingent availability surpriseChecked by code

You bought 800 MW unit-contingent for a year at $60/MWh and hedged by selling expected output at 92% availability. Prices fall to $40, and the unit runs at 96%.

800 × 8,760 × 0.04 × (40 − 60) = $−5.61m on the unhedged extra 4%

Higher-than-expected availability hurts when prices have fallen, and an outage hurts when they have risen. Volume risk is always wrong-way relative to the price move since inception. Swindle ch.15 (p.401) Synthesis

Joint Volumetric & Price Risk: Renewable Quanto Floors

Renewable projects face joint price and volumetric uncertainty. Because wind and solar generation depress the wholesale price (cannibalization effect), output volume \(V\) is negatively correlated with market power price \(P\) (\(\rho_{PV} < 0\)). A traditional fixed-volume floor contract ignores this covariance. The damaging case is high output when prices are low: the floor is in the money on more MWh than its fixed notional covers. (Low output in high-price hours is a natural hedge for revenue, but the fixed-notional floor then pays nothing.) A quanto option pays \(\max(0, K_P - P) \cdot V\). Under the pricing measure with volume \(V\) as numeraire, the effective forward price is adjusted by the covariance drift: \[ F_P^* = F_P \cdot e^{\rho_{PV}\,\sigma_P\,\sigma_V\,T} \] Because \(\rho_{PV} < 0\), the effective forward price is shifted downward (\(F_P^* < F_P\)), significantly increasing the value and protection of downside put options. Outside the texts Synthesis

Worked example 5 · valuing a wind farm quanto floor vs vanilla hedgeChecked by code

A 100,000 MWh wind farm seeks a 1-year (\(T = 1.0\) yr, \(r = 4\%\)) downside floor at strike \(K_P = \$40.00/\text{MWh}\). Market forward \(F_P = \$45.00/\text{MWh}\), power price vol \(\sigma_P = 40\%\), volume vol \(\sigma_V = 25\%\), and negative cannibalization correlation \(\rho_{PV} = -0.40\):

quanto covariance drift adjustment factor = exp(−0.40 × 0.40 × 0.25 × 1.0) = 0.9608 quanto effective forward power price F_P* = $43.24/MWh vanilla put option premium (assuming zero correlation, F_P = $45) = $4.35/MWh ($434.55k total) quanto floor option premium (capturing negative correlation, F_P* = $43.24) = $4.90/MWh ($490.20k total) quanto protection wedge (unhedged volume-price covariance loss) = +$55.66k (+12.8%)

A vanilla put valued on expected volume underprices the downside risk by $55.66k because it assumes average price applies to peak generation. The quanto floor pays on the volume actually produced, so it protects revenue rather than a notional quantity. Outside the texts Synthesis

ALM analogy

A swing option is a policyholder's partial-surrender or flexible-premium option: limited exercises, timing at the holder's choice, and value that depends on whether exercise is rational (price-driven) or behavioural (need-driven). Load-following deals are like mortgage prepayment risk: volume correlates with rates against you, so you hedge more than the expected notional and hold reserves for behavioural model risk. Swindle draws this comparison himself, between retail customer attrition and mortgage prepayment. Swindle ch.15 (p.379) Synthesis

Dated: contract forms

Kaminski's swing and take-or-pay examples are late-1990s US gas, and Eydeland and Wolyniec's quotes are from 2001. Long-term take-or-pay remains central in LNG supply contracts (often with annual contract quantity, downward quantity tolerance and make-up rights). European gas supply flexibility clauses have been renegotiated since 2022. Check current LNG master agreements (for example AIPN and GIIGNL resources) and regulator publications. Outside the texts

Going deeper: splitting multi-period swing

For swings spanning several months, Eydeland and Wolyniec note you rarely need a full dynamic program. When forward contracts exist for each sub-period, the problem splits into a forward optimisation across months plus an inside-the-month optimisation, the same structure as storage (Module 4) and cumulative plant constraints (Module 3). Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.8 (p.319) The forward market turns a hard backward-induction problem into a sequence of easier ones. Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • Swing, recall and nomination rights are exclusive American-style options. Value per right lies between the daily European and the American.
  • Mean reversion and spikes make scarce rights much more valuable than daily options.
  • Know your customer: need-driven exercise makes the deal a load obligation, not an option.
  • Load-following carries wrong-way volume risk (uplift, load growth), and unit-contingent risk barely hedges.
