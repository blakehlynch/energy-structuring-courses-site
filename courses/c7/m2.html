Spark and crack spread options

Core≈ 60 min8 quiz questions5 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Value a spark spread option with a variable-cost strike.
  • Explain why spark optionality concentrates in shoulder months.
  • Compute a 3-2-1 crack spread.
  • Convert a power position to gas-equivalent delta by price ratio and by regression.

Prerequisites

  • Module 1; Course 6 Module 5.

Conversion assets are spread options

A gas plant converts gas to power, so its margin is the spark spread, the power price minus heat rate × gas price, less variable costs. A refinery's margin is the crack spread, product prices minus crude. Swindle calls these conversion structures the main natural source of optionality in energy, alongside storage (time spreads) and pipelines (location spreads). Swindle ch.9 (pp.187–189) Eydeland and Wolyniec go further. Through the stack, power itself behaves like a fuel derivative, so even a pure power position has fuel sensitivities, for example through gas-driven power volatility. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.9 (pp.384–385)

Adding a strike: Kirk's approximation

Margrabe's formula (Course 6) values a zero-strike spread. Real tolls have a strike, the variable O&M cost \(V\) per MWh, so the payoff is \(\max(P - H G - V, 0)\). The standard desk approximation, due to Kirk, treats \(HG + V\) as a single lognormal asset with volatility scaled by \(HG/(HG+V)\), and then applies Margrabe. Outside the texts

\[ \hat\sigma^2 = T\left(\sigma_P^2 + w^2\sigma_G^2 - 2\rho\,w\,\sigma_P\sigma_G\right),\qquad w = \frac{HG}{HG+V} \]
Worked example 1 · one month's spark option and its deltasChecked by code

Three months out: on-peak power $45, gas $4.40, heat rate 7.5, VOM $3. Volatilities are 45% (power) and 35% (gas), ρ = 0.75 and r = 4%.

intrinsic = 45 − 33 − 3 = $9.00; Kirk value = $9.09/MWh (zero strike: $11.92) deltas: 0.933 MWh of power and −6.85 MMBtu of gas per MWh of option

Deep in the money, the option is nearly a forward spark spread. The gas delta of −6.85 is close to −7.5 × 0.93. Hedge it by selling about 0.93 MWh of power and buying 6.85 MMBtu of gas per MWh of capacity. Synthesis

Where the optionality sits in the year

Monthly power and gas curves have different seasonal shapes. Power peaks in summer, when gas is cheapest, so the market heat rate peaks in summer. A plant's strip of monthly options is therefore deep in the money in peak months and near or out of the money in the shoulders. Swindle finds that for typical generation hedges the extrinsic value sits in the shoulder months, not the summer. Summer volatility is high, but the high market heat rates put the option far in the money. Swindle ch.14 (p.346)

Worked example 2 · a 12-month spark stripChecked by code

Illustrative monthly forwards: on-peak power runs from $38 to $66 (peaking in July and August), and gas from $3.90 to $4.80 (peaking in winter). Heat rate 10, VOM $3, volatility and correlation as in Example 1.

average value $6.33/MWh, of which intrinsic $4.06: extrinsic share 35.9% August $21.68 (intrinsic $21.39), March $2.03 (intrinsic $0) same strip with a 7.5 heat rate: $13.67/MWh, extrinsic only 3.9%

An efficient plant is mostly a forward spark spread, so the hedge is linear and correlation hardly matters. A less efficient unit is mostly optionality, so value depends on volatility and correlation and the hedge needs constant re-balancing. These monthly options also understate value, because a real plant can choose day by day (Module 3). Swindle ch.14 (p.346) Synthesis

Spark-spread strip

Monthly value split into intrinsic and extrinsic

VOM $3/MWh, r = 4%. Lower the heat rate and the strip turns into a forward spread. Raise it and value moves into the summer and becomes nearly all optionality.

Which correlation?

For daily tolls, Swindle found that historical spot correlation between power and gas averaged around 0.38 over a calendar year. Broker toll trades implied correlations in the low 0.90s, which cut extrinsic value by about 40%. Swindle ch.14 (p.344) He attributes the high implied correlations partly to liquidity frictions, since dealers need a low entry price. He warns that raising correlation in a lognormal model also forces heat-rate behaviour that contradicts the data: heat rates usually fall as gas rises, partly because generators bid to recover costs beyond fuel. Swindle ch.14 (pp.354–355) Structural stack models (Course 6, Module 6) reproduce that behaviour naturally. Swindle ch.14 (pp.364–367)

Crack spreads

Crack spread options pay on product prices minus crude, often for a basket such as 3-2-1 (3 barrels of crude into 2 of gasoline and 1 of distillate). They are usually monthly or Asian and traded in standard form rather than tailored to a refinery. Swindle ch.9 (p.190)

Worked example 3 · a 3-2-1 crackChecked by code
crude $80/bbl, gasoline $2.40/gal, ULSD $2.65/gal → (2 × 2.40 × 42 + 2.65 × 42 − 3 × 80) / 3 = $24.30/bbl

Convert products from $/gal to $/bbl (× 42) before netting. A refinery that sells this crack forward locks its gross margin. Course 3 covers the physical side. Synthesis

Converting power exposure into gas

Long-dated power is illiquid, so desks often hedge power first with gas and then swap the gas into power as liquidity allows. A common shortcut converts power delta to gas using the market heat rate \(F/G\). Swindle shows this overstates the hedge when heat rates fall as gas rises, because \(dF/dG = H + G\,dH/dG\) and the second term is negative. In his example a regression on calendar strips gave a hedge about 30% smaller. Swindle ch.6 (pp.129–131)

Worked example 4 · gas-equivalent hedge for a power saleChecked by code

You buy 300 MW of 5×16 power for a future calendar year: 300 × 16 × 255 = 1,224 thousand MWh. Power trades at $48 and gas at $4.00 (market heat rate 12.0). A regression of strip power on gas gives a slope of 8.5.

price-ratio hedge: sell 1,224,000 × 12.0 / 365 / 10,000 = 4.02 lots/day of gas regression hedge: 2.85 lots/day, about 29% less

Neither number is "right". The regression reflects how heat rates have responded to gas recently, and that can change with the stack (coal-to-gas switching, new capacity). Use it, but stress the hedge across regimes. Where power trades as a heat-rate product (gas price × agreed heat rate), the gas hedge is already embedded. Swindle ch.6 (p.132) Synthesis

ALM analogy

A spark spread option resembles an option on a credit spread or a basis between two curves. Its value depends on the volatility of the difference, so correlation drives it. A deep in-the-money spread option is close to a linear basis position, and an out-of-the-money one is a volatility position. Converting power deltas into gas is like hedging a corporate bond book with Treasuries using an empirical beta rather than duration alone. Synthesis

Dated: heat rates and fuel mix

Swindle's examples come from PJM 2010–11, at the start of coal-to-gas switching. Since then, coal retirements, renewables and carbon prices have changed how heat rates respond to gas. In Europe, clean spark and dark spreads (net of carbon) are the relevant measures. Check current ISO fuel-mix data and exchange heat-rate products. Outside the texts

Going deeper: the dual-fuel lower bound

A plant that can burn gas or oil has payoff \(\max(P - \min(H_1G_1, H_2G_2), 0)\). Rather than model three commodities jointly, desks often book the higher of the two single-fuel tolls each month and rebook when the ranking changes. Taking the maximum inside the expectation shows this is a rigorous lower bound, and it keeps the risk looking like a familiar single-fuel toll. Swindle ch.14 (p.369) The same "take the best of simple structures" logic gives lower bounds for storage in Module 4. Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • Generators and refineries are spread options. Kirk's approximation handles a variable-cost strike.
  • Efficient plants are mostly intrinsic, and peakers mostly extrinsic. Optionality concentrates where the market heat rate is near the unit's.
  • Implied toll correlations have exceeded historical ones. Raising correlation in a lognormal model distorts heat-rate behaviour.
  • Gas-equivalent hedges from the price ratio overstate delta when heat rates fall with gas. Use empirical slopes and stress them.
