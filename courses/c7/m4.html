Storage valuation

Core≈ 70 min8 quiz questions7 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Compute the intrinsic value of a storage facility by dynamic programming.
  • Explain extrinsic value and estimate it by rolling intrinsic simulation.
  • Represent storage as a portfolio of calendar spread options.
  • Explain why correlation inputs dominate storage valuation.

Prerequisites

  • Modules 1–2; Course 6 Modules 1 and 4.

What a storage contract gives you

Storage moves gas through time: buy and inject when it is cheap, withdraw and sell when it is dear. Swindle calls storage valuation one of the major unresolved valuation problems in the business. Swindle ch.13 (p.297) The three main physical forms are aquifers, depleted reservoirs and salt caverns. Salt caverns behave most like simple tanks and allow high injection and withdrawal rates. Geology is only partly observable, and base gas must stay in place to maintain pressure, so contracts are written within conservative limits. Swindle ch.13 (pp.298–299) A contract specifies:

  • Capacity: the maximum working inventory.
  • Rates: maximum injection and withdrawal per day, which often shrink as the facility nears full or empty ("ratchets").
  • Inventory conditions: start and end levels (commonly empty to empty from April to March), plus intermediate minimums or maximums to force cycling.

The number of full cycles a year (turns) is a useful summary. Aquifers and reservoirs are roughly one turn. Salt caverns can turn several times, and more turns mean more option value. Swindle ch.13 (p.299) Leases come in other shapes too. A lessee may take the facility empty and return it empty, or full and return it full, and the owner may keep peaking rights. Eydeland and Wolyniec's example combines a summer fill requirement and a winter minimum with the owner's right to withdraw. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.8 (p.354)

Intrinsic value: optimise against today's curve

Intrinsic value is the best schedule you can lock in today with forward trades: buy the injection months and sell the withdrawal months, subject to the constraints. It is a model-free lower bound. Swindle ch.13 (p.309) With linear costs it is a linear program over spreads between delivery dates. More generally it is a dynamic program over inventory levels, working backward from the end condition. Swindle ch.13 (pp.310–311) Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.8 (pp.355–357) Once the hedge is on, the cash flow is locked whatever happens to prices later. Physical injection decisions still follow the spreads at the time, not the hedge. In liquid markets, hedges don't change operations. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.8 (p.359)

Worked example 1 · intrinsic value of a one-turn facilityChecked by code

A 1,000,000 MMBtu facility, April–March, empty to empty. It injects up to 10,000/day and withdraws up to 20,000/day, with $0.02 per MMBtu each way. Illustrative forwards run from $3.20 in April to $4.60 in January.

dynamic program: inject 300k in each of Apr, May and Jun, 100k in Jul; hold; withdraw 380k in Dec and 620k in Jan intrinsic value = $1,218k = $1.218 per MMBtu of capacity a faster facility (fill in a month, empty in 20 days): $1,360k

The schedule injects in the cheapest months it can reach and withdraws into the two most expensive. Faster rates let it concentrate on the extremes. Beyond a point, extra speed adds little intrinsic value because the curve's slope is bounded. Swindle ch.13 (p.312)

Extrinsic value: the right to re-optimise

If the curve moves after you hedge, you can unwind part of the schedule and lock in a better one. That right is an option on the shape of the curve. Its value grows with volatility, with lower correlation between contracts, with flatter curves and with more operational flexibility. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.8 (pp.360, 362) Storage is "rougher curves pay", and storage capacity in turn smooths the curve. Swindle ch.13 (p.300)

The rolling intrinsic method captures this. Lock in intrinsic value, then at each rebalancing date re-solve against the current curve and switch whenever the new schedule is worth more than the old one at today's prices. Every switch is a non-negative gain, so the total is a lower bound on value, and the method needs no model of forward dynamics to run. Many practitioners back-test it on historical curves. Swindle ch.13 (pp.323–324) Mahoney adds a caution. Rolling intrinsic is not risk-free: hedges swing between 0% and 100% of volume, transaction costs can eat the extrinsic value, and cash flows are more variable than under incremental delta hedging. Mahoney §3.1.4 (pp.70–71)

Worked example 2 · rolling intrinsic by simulationChecked by code

Same facility. We simulate 200 paths of the forward curve with two exponential factors (a slow 30% factor and a fast 80% factor decaying at 1.5/yr) and re-optimise monthly.

extrinsic (average rebalancing gain) = $88.5k, 7.3% of intrinsic; total $1,306k with a stronger, faster-decaying front factor (100%, decay 3/yr): extrinsic $209.4k = 17.2%

Extrinsic value depends heavily on how the front of the curve decorrelates from the back. Swindle finds that changing one mean-reversion rate can change virtual storage value by 50%. Model sensitivity is greatest for high-turn facilities, while one-turn storage stays close to its model-free intrinsic value. Swindle ch.13 (pp.308, 320) This example re-optimises monthly. Daily re-optimisation within the cash month would add value that a monthly model misses. Synthesis

Storage as a portfolio of spread options

The simplest storage right is a calendar spread option (CSO): buy month i, store, sell month j if the spread beats carrying costs. Swindle ch.13 (p.300) A facility can be approximated by a portfolio of CSOs and forward spreads, chosen by linear programming so that the physical limits hold whichever options end up exercised. This gives a conservative lower bound that many practitioners trust. Swindle ch.13 (pp.324–326) Mahoney §4.3.1 (p.128) With no injection or withdrawal costs, Eydeland and Wolyniec show the spread-option formulation is exact. With costs it is a tight lower bound, and far faster than full simulation. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.8 (p.362) Swindle warns that the optimal portfolio jumps as inputs change, because LP solutions are not smooth. Greeks computed on a static portfolio can therefore shift without warning when the system re-optimises. Swindle ch.13 (p.326)

Worked example 3 · one calendar spread optionChecked by code

An option to buy August ($3.50) and sell January ($4.60), with a $0.04 round-trip cost. Expiry is 0.4 years, volatilities 35% (August) and 45% (January), correlation 0.85.

intrinsic 4.60 − 3.50 − 0.04 = $1.060; option value $1.053

Deep in the money, the option is worth about its intrinsic value (slightly less, from discounting). Inter-season spreads are mostly intrinsic. The extrinsic value lies in near-the-money spreads within a season. Synthesis

The real problem: correlation

Swindle argues that the choice of numerical method (dynamic programming, Longstaff–Schwartz regression, rolling intrinsic or CSO portfolios) matters less than the correlation structure, which the market barely reveals. His broker-chat example from November 2010 shows CSO quotes almost entirely on October–January spreads. Those are inter-season spreads where value is mostly intrinsic and correlation hardly matters. Correlation matters within seasons, where nothing traded. Swindle ch.13 (pp.331–333) Inventory feeds back too: storage owners optimise much like one another, so the right correlations depend on aggregate inventory. Swindle ch.13 (p.333) Dynamic programming is exact up to the dynamics, but in practice it is limited to one or two factors. Longstaff–Schwartz handles richer dynamics at heavy computational cost. Swindle ch.13 (pp.320–323)

Least-Squares Monte Carlo (LSMC) for Gas Storage

While dynamic programming requires a low-dimensional grid, commercial storage with inventory ratchets and multi-factor forward curves is priced on trading desks via Least-Squares Monte Carlo (LSMC), adapted from Longstaff & Schwartz (2001) by Boogert & de Jong (2008). In backward induction across simulated price paths, the continuation value of each end-of-period inventory level is estimated by regressing realised discounted future cash flows onto polynomial functions of the spot price: \(\hat{C}(P_t, I) = \beta_0(I) + \beta_1(I) P_t + \beta_2(I) P_t^2\). The regression only chooses the action (maximise immediate injection or withdrawal cash plus \(\hat{C}\)). Each path is then valued with the cash flows it actually realises under that policy, which stops in-sample fitting from biasing the estimate upward. Because the intrinsic schedule's realised value has a known expectation (the intrinsic value), it also serves as a control variate that removes most of the Monte Carlo noise from the extrinsic estimate. Swindle ch.13 (pp.320–323) Outside the texts

The price dynamics matter more than the algorithm, which is Swindle's point above. If spot is a single geometric Brownian motion, every forward scales with spot, so the best action never depends on the price level and LSMC returns exactly the intrinsic value. Extrinsic value needs spreads that move. Here spot is lognormal around the forward curve with a mean-reverting (OU) factor, so a high price today is expected to fade relative to later months. Synthesis

Worked example 4 · LSMC polynomial regression storage valuationChecked by code

A gas storage facility (100k MMBtu capacity, 50k MMBtu a month injection and withdrawal, $0.02/MMBtu cost each way) values a 6-month curve [$3.30, $3.20, $3.25, $3.60, $4.00, $4.10] with 60% spot volatility and mean reversion \(\kappa = 4\)/yr, on 20,000 simulated paths, 4% discounting and an 11-level inventory grid:

intrinsic value (same grid, today's curve) = $74.11k LSMC value (realised cash flows, control variate) = $75.68k → $0.757/MMBtu of capacity extrinsic value = $1.57k same inputs with GBM spot (κ = 0): extrinsic = $0.00k

Extrinsic value is about 2% of intrinsic here. Capacity is tight relative to the injection rate and the summer–winter spread is wide, so the facility follows its intrinsic schedule on most paths and deviates only when a large spot move makes an early withdrawal or a delayed injection pay. Faster facilities, flatter curves and more re-optimisation dates raise the extrinsic share. Different seeds move the extrinsic figure by roughly ±$0.15k. Synthesis

ALM analogy

Intrinsic storage value is like locking in a static cash-flow-matched portfolio. Extrinsic value is the option to rebalance into a better-matched portfolio as the curve moves, which resembles the value of a dynamic ALM strategy over a buy-and-hold one. Rolling intrinsic is a "rebalance only when it helps" rule, and its result depends on curve-shape volatility, the commodity equivalent of non-parallel yield-curve moves. Synthesis

Dated: storage markets

Swindle's working problems use 2010 Henry Hub curves, and Eydeland and Wolyniec's examples date from around 2000. US working gas capacity, the summer–winter spread and salt-cavern share have all changed, and European storage now carries regulatory fill mandates (introduced in 2022) that constrain operation. Outside the texts Check EIA weekly storage, AGSI+ (GIE) for Europe, and current tariff sheets for injection and withdrawal rates and ratchets.

Going deeper: rolling intrinsic and local time

Mahoney links rolling intrinsic to a classic puzzle. "Hold the intrinsic position of an option, rebalancing only when it crosses the strike" seems to replicate the option for its intrinsic cost, which would be an arbitrage. The resolution is that the strategy is not self-financing: each crossing costs money, and in expectation those costs add up exactly to the option's time value. Applied to storage, rolling intrinsic exposes you to how often and how far the curve's optimal schedule flips. That realised quantity can differ from what a delta-hedging strategy would capture, even when both average the same. Mahoney §3.1.4 (pp.68–71)

Key takeaways

  • Intrinsic value is the best schedule against today's curve, found by dynamic or linear programming. It is model-free and locked by forward hedges.
  • Extrinsic value comes from re-optimising as the curve moves. Rolling intrinsic gives a lower bound.
  • Storage can be approximated by a CSO portfolio, exactly so without costs. LP solutions make greeks jumpy.
  • Correlation structure, barely observable within seasons, is the dominant valuation risk, especially for high-turn storage.
