Linear instruments do most of the work

Options get the attention, but swaps, forwards and futures carry most of the risk transfer in energy. Swindle notes that the maths of swaps looks dull, yet managing a large swaps book is where much of the real complexity lives. Swindle ch.6 (pp.118–119) This course starts there, and then turns to the main option forms built on top of swaps.

Pricing a strip swap

A fixed-for-floating swap exchanges a fixed price for a floating index (a monthly index or an average of daily prices) over a series of periods. A utility buying a winter swap receives index minus fixed each month. It is protected from high prices but gives up the benefit of low ones. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.8 (p.332) The fair fixed price makes the present value zero. It is the forward curve averaged with weights equal to volume × discount factor:

\[ K^* = \frac{\sum_i Q_i\, d_i\, F_i}{\sum_i Q_i\, d_i} \]
Worked example 1 · a winter strip swapChecked by code

An LDC wants to fix Nov–Mar gas at 10,000 MMBtu/day. The forwards are 4.10, 4.55, 4.70, 4.45 and 3.95, with day counts 30, 31, 31, 28 and 31, discounted at 4%.

fixed price = $4.3499/MMBtu (simple average $4.3500; volume and discount weights barely move it here) if the whole curve then rises $0.20, the LDC's swap gains $293.5k

Weights matter more when volumes are shaped (heavier in January) or the curve is steep. Pricing with a simple average then puts the error into the book from day one. Synthesis

Two common variations follow. A differential swap pays the difference between two prices, such as a spark spread, a crack or a location basis. A generator uses one to lock in its margin. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.8 (p.333) For an illiquid location, a desk prices the swap as the benchmark swap, plus forward basis to a nearby liquid hub, plus the cost of transport capacity, so the price depends on the assets the desk controls. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.8 (pp.332–333) A participation swap lets the fixed payer keep part of any fall in price. It is a swap plus a strip of puts, so the fixed price is set above the plain swap level until value is zero. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.8 (p.334)

Worked example 2 · a 50% participation swapChecked by code

The same strip, with the buyer keeping half of any fall below the reference price R. Put volatility is 45%.

solve Σ Qd(F − R) + 0.5 Σ Qd·Put(R) = 0 → R = $4.8161 versus $4.3499 for the plain swap

The client pays about 47 cents more on every MMBtu in exchange for keeping half the downside. Show them that trade-off explicitly. A participation swap is a swap with a bought put, financed through the fixed price. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.8 (p.334) Synthesis

Energy options are mostly Asians

Most energy exposures settle on averages: a monthly index, daily prices over the month, or a utility's average fuel cost. Kaminski, Gibner and Pinnamaneni note that average-price options were embedded in energy contracts long before they had a name. Producers and utilities budget on averages, and averaging makes the payoff hard to move with a single day's settlement. Kaminski (ed.) ch.3 An LDC worried about January but hoping prices fall can buy a call on the January daily average struck at today's forward. It is protected if a disruption sends prices up and pays little if prices fall. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.8 (pp.321–322) Even monthly futures options are close to Asians, because the futures contract settles against the month's average spot. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.8 (p.329)

The arithmetic average of lognormal prices is not lognormal, so there is no exact formula. Desks use approximations. The geometric-average option has a closed form and bounds the arithmetic one (Vorst), Curran's conditioning method is very accurate, and Monte Carlo with the geometric option as a control variate is the fallback. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.8 (pp.324–328) Levy's approach, discussed by Kaminski et al., matches the first two moments of the average to a lognormal and then uses Black's formula. Kaminski (ed.) ch.3 With fixings at times \(t_i\):

\[ E[A] = F,\qquad E[A^2] = \frac{F^2}{n^2}\sum_{i,j} e^{\sigma^2 \min(t_i,t_j)},\qquad \sigma_A^2 T = \ln\frac{E[A^2]}{E[A]^2} \]
Worked example 3 · a January Asian callChecked by code

January gas forward $4.70, strike $4.70, 50% volatility. Averaging over 31 daily fixings starts in eight months, the payoff is made in nine, and r = 4%.

Levy approximation: $0.7523 (equivalent volatility 48.1%); Monte Carlo, 40,000 paths: $0.7669 European option expiring at the end of January: $0.7813 the same Asian with averaging starting tomorrow: $0.1611 (equivalent volatility 29.6%) versus a one-month European at $0.2721

When averaging is far off, most of the variance builds up before averaging begins, so the Asian is almost a European. When averaging starts now, the average has roughly √(1/3) of the volatility and the option is worth about 40% less. The moment-matched value sits about 2% below the simulation here. That is typical of these approximations and a reason to test them against Monte Carlo for the deal at hand. Kaminski (ed.) ch.3 Synthesis

Asian versus European

How averaging and its timing change the premium

Forward $4.70, r = 4%, payment one month after the averaging start. With 1 averaging day the Asian is a European.

Hedging averages: time basis and BOM

An average-price option settles at month end, but the futures contract that hedges it expires before the month starts. The gap between the futures settlement and the realised average (time basis) can be very large in power. Where a balance-of-month (BOM) market is liquid, it lets the hedge follow the average through the month. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.8 (p.324) For the writer, an Asian is easier to manage than a European. As the average sets, gamma falls toward zero, rather than spiking at expiry for an at-the-money European. Kaminski (ed.) ch.3 Eydeland and Wolyniec suggest dynamic forward and BOM hedging with static index-option positions as a practical recipe. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.8 (p.324)

ALM analogy

A strip swap is an interest-rate swap: the fixed rate is the PV-weighted average of forward rates. A participation swap is a swap with an embedded floor sold back to the payer, like a callable or capped structure, and pricing it means solving for the coupon that makes the package worth par. An Asian is like an option on an average rate (a crediting rate set on a moving average), with lower volatility and less pin risk at expiry. Synthesis

Dated: products and liquidity

Eydeland and Wolyniec and Kaminski describe early-2000s and late-1990s US gas and power markets. Since then, average-price options (APOs) and calendar-strip options have been listed and cleared on CME and ICE across crude, products and gas. BOM and daily markets have deepened at major hubs, and cleared OTC has replaced much bilateral credit risk. Outside the texts Check exchange product lists (CME, ICE, EEX) for what clears today.

Going deeper: why the average's volatility is about σ/√3

If averaging runs continuously from now to T, each fixing \(t\) shares the variance accumulated up to \(\min(t, t')\) with every other fixing. Integrating \(\min(t,t')\) over the square gives \(T/3\), so the average carries about \(\sigma^2 T/3\) of variance, or a volatility of \(\sigma/\sqrt{3}\). Moment matching with logs gives slightly more. Once averaging starts later, the variance accumulated before the start is shared fully by every fixing, and the ratio heads to one. This is the same idea as integrated variance in Course 6: what matters is the variance each fixing actually sees. Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • A swap's fixed price is the volume- and discount-weighted average forward. Differential swaps lock margins, and participation swaps embed puts.
  • Most energy options are Asians. Approximate them by moment matching or conditioning, and check against simulation.
  • Averaging that starts later behaves like a European. Averaging that starts now cuts volatility by roughly √3.
  • Time basis between futures and the realised average is a real hedging risk. BOM markets help.
