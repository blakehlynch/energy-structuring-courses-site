Hedging structured books

Core≈ 60 min8 quiz questions6 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Classify book risk into price level, time spread, basis and cross-commodity.
  • Aggregate deltas up a hedging hierarchy and attribute P&L.
  • Size minimum-variance hedges with unhedgeable basis.
  • Explain market versus corporate price of risk and structured versus price desks.

Prerequisites

  • Modules 1–5.

Four linear risks

Swindle sorts what swaps and forwards transfer into four risks. Swindle ch.6 (p.119)

  • Price level: the overall level of the benchmark, usually hedged first with benchmark contracts.
  • Time spread: changes in curve shape (backwardation and contango).
  • Basis: location differences between less liquid points and the benchmark.
  • Cross-commodity (conversion): spark, dark and crack spreads between a benchmark and what it is converted into.

A structured deal decomposes into these risks plus whatever optionality it contains. Hedging usually runs in that order: neutralise the level with the benchmark at once, then move the hedge out in tenor (stack-and-roll), across to the right location (basis) and into the right commodity (conversion) as liquidity allows. Swindle ch.6 (pp.119–123, 129–135)

Stack and roll in practice

For a long-dated benchmark purchase, the first hedge is a sale in a liquid near tenor such as the next calendar strip, sized by a minimum-variance or first-factor (PCA) calculation. The hedge is then rolled out as calendar spreads trade. Calendar spreads are quoted with backwardation as a premium. Swindle ch.6 (pp.119–122) Real books never line up neatly. Client trades follow seasons, planning years (June–May in many power markets) or odd stubs, so delta profiles get rough. Part of a portfolio manager's job is to keep mopping up delta debris at the monthly level as exposures roll into liquid tenors. Swindle ch.6 (pp.122–123)

For basis, Swindle shows that a TETCO M3 position should be hedged with somewhat more than one-for-one Henry Hub, about 1.04 from rolling calendar-strip returns. Locational relationships come from physical infrastructure costs and change when infrastructure does. Swindle ch.6 (p.123) Module 2 covered conversion hedges with heat rates.

Organising risk as a hierarchy

A book with a few benchmarks, a dozen gas basis points and many power zones over five years can have well over a thousand contract-month exposures, with large offsetting positions. A spreadsheet of deltas explains nothing. Swindle's answer is to organise risk the way hedges are built: a tree with the benchmark (NYMEX gas) at the top, then regional gas hubs, then smaller basis points and power hubs, then zones and nodes. Swindle ch.6 (pp.132–133) Each child is linked to its parent by a regression-style relationship \(F_I = \alpha + \beta F_{\pi[I]} + \epsilon\). Deltas roll up the tree by the chain rule, and daily P&L splits into the benchmark move plus each node's unexplained move times its total delta. Swindle ch.6 (pp.133–135)

\[ \Delta^{\text{total}}_{\text{parent}} = \Delta_{\text{parent}} + \sum_{\text{children}} \beta_c\,\Delta^{\text{total}}_c,\qquad \text{P\&L}_I = \Delta^{\text{total}}_I\left(dF_I - \beta_I\, dF_{\pi[I]}\right) \]
Worked example 1 · rolling up deltas and attributing P&LChecked by code

A book holds 1,000,000 MMBtu long TETM3 (β = 1.10 to Henry Hub), 50,000 MWh short PJM West peak (β = 8.0 MMBtu/MWh to Henry Hub) and 600,000 MMBtu short Henry Hub futures. Today Henry Hub rises $0.20, TETM3 $0.30 and PJM $1.50.

Henry Hub-equivalent delta = −600,000 + 1.10 × 1,000,000 − 8.0 × 50,000 = 100k MMBtu long P&L = −600k × 0.20 + 1,000k × 0.30 − 50k × 1.50 = $105k attribution: benchmark 100k × 0.20 = $20k; TETM3 basis 1,000k × (0.30 − 0.22) = $80k; PJM conversion −50k × (1.50 − 1.60) = $5k

Most of today's P&L came from basis, not the outright move. The head trader sees at once that the risk to manage is TETM3 basis, not Henry Hub. Swindle ch.6 (pp.133–135) Synthesis

Book P&L attribution

Benchmark, basis and conversion components of a day's P&L

Book: long 1,000k MMBtu TETM3 (β 1.10) and short 50k MWh PJM (β 8.0). Set the hedge to −700k to make the book benchmark-neutral, and the remaining P&L is pure basis and conversion.

The hierarchy also exposes a weakness in VaR. Prices low in the tree often aren't independently marked every day. They move with their parents except for occasional remarks, so large exposures there barely show in historical-simulation VaR. Limits on the rolled-up delta at each node are the practical defence. Swindle ch.6 (p.135)

Hedging when not everything trades

In liquid markets, Eydeland and Wolyniec's principle is that values and hedges are additive. You can value a power plant and a nearby storage facility separately, because the opportunity cost of using your own gas is the spot price. Operations don't depend on hedges, only cash flows do. In illiquid markets, joint optimisation matters, and the gain from it is bounded by the bid-ask spread. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.8 (pp.381–383) When part of an exposure can't be hedged, split it into a hedgeable part and an independent residual (basis). Value the residual with an explicit corporate risk adjustment. The optimal hedge then depends on that adjustment. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.10 (pp.454–457) For linear positions with constant volatilities and correlation, the minimum-variance hedge is simply \(\rho\,\sigma_P/\sigma_G\). Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.10 (p.457)

Worked example 2 · minimum-variance cross hedgeChecked by code

A local price moves with a $0.90 daily standard deviation, the liquid hub with $0.60, and their correlation is 0.8.

hedge ratio = 0.8 × 0.90 / 0.60 = 1.20 hub units per local unit → variance cut by 64.0% (one-for-one: 62.2%)

The best hedge removes ρ² = 64% of the variance. The remaining 36% is basis you carry or price in. Small hedge-ratio errors cost little in variance, but large ones do, and the ratio itself moves with regime. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.10 (p.457) Synthesis

Second-Order Greeks: Cross-Gamma, Correlation Vega & P&L Explain

On options desks holding multi-asset spread positions (spark spreads, crack spreads, locational basis options), first-order delta hedging is insufficient. When both legs move simultaneously, the second-order Taylor expansion explains daily P&L attribution: \[ \Delta\Pi \approx \sum_i \Delta_i\,\Delta S_i + \frac{1}{2}\sum_i \Gamma_{ii}\,(\Delta S_i)^2 + \sum_{i < j} \Gamma_{ij}\,\Delta S_i\,\Delta S_j + \mathcal{V}\,\Delta\sigma + \mathcal{V}_\rho\,\Delta\rho + \Theta\,\Delta t \] The cross-gamma \(\Gamma_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S_i \partial S_j}\) measures how the delta of commodity \(i\) changes as commodity \(j\) moves. For spread call options \((S_1 - H S_2 - K)^+\), the cross-gamma is strictly negative (\(\Gamma_{12} < 0\)). When power and gas rally together, the negative cross-gamma destroys P&L, while changes in implied correlation \(\rho\) generate substantial mark-to-market exposure via correlation vega \(\mathcal{V}_\rho = \frac{\partial V}{\partial \rho} < 0\). Eydeland & Wolyniec app.B (p.473) Outside the texts Synthesis

Worked example 3 · daily P&L explain with cross-gamma and correlation moveChecked by code

A power tolling desk holds a long spark spread book: power delta \(\Delta_P = +50\text{k}\) MWh, gas delta \(\Delta_G = -350\text{k}\) MMBtu, own-gammas \(\Gamma_{PP} = +4\text{k}\), \(\Gamma_{GG} = +30\text{k}\), cross-gamma \(\Gamma_{PG} = -10\text{k}\), vega \(\mathcal{V} = \$120\)k per vol point (1%), correlation vega \(\mathcal{V}_\rho = -\$800\text{k}\) per unit correlation, and theta \(\Theta = -\$15\text{k}\)/day. Over 1 day (\(dt = 1\)), power jumps \(\Delta S_P = +\$4.00/\text{MWh}\), gas jumps \(\Delta S_G = +\$0.50/\text{MMBtu}\), implied vol rises \(\Delta\sigma = +0.2\) vol points, and correlation rises \(\Delta\rho = +0.05\):

linear delta P&L (+$200k power − $175k gas) = +$25.00k own-gamma P&L (0.5 × 4k × 4² + 0.5 × 30k × 0.5²) = +$35.75k cross-gamma P&L (−10k × 4.0 × 0.5) = $−20.00k volatility vega P&L (120k × 0.2) = +$24.00k correlation vega P&L (−800k × 0.05) = $−40.00k calendar theta decay P&L = $−15.00k total second-order Taylor explained P&L = +$9.75k

Delta and own-gamma suggested a gain of +$60.75k. Negative cross-gamma (−$20k) and the correlation move (−$40k) gave back $60k of it, leaving +$9.75k after vega and theta. A desk explaining P&L with deltas and own-gammas alone would show a $51k unexplained residual. Structuring desks that ignore cross-gamma and correlation vega will systematically misattribute P&L and blow through risk limits. Outside the texts Synthesis

Pricing what you can't hedge

A single risk-adjusted discount rate can't price options consistently, so risk adjustment works by changing the drift of the underlying (the price of risk). Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.10 (pp.434–436) Where enough instruments trade, the market price of risk is implicit and you never need it. Where they don't, Eydeland and Wolyniec argue a desk should price with its corporate price of risk, set by its risk appetite and capital measures (for example RAROC), rather than chase an unobservable market price for a market that doesn't exist. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.10 (pp.437–438) Many firms split structured desks, which hedge to the best available tradables at internal prices, from price desks, which make the internal market and may hold views, for example not buying "expensive" options as static hedges. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.10 (p.438) For the illiquid tail of long-dated assets, the right measure is the distribution of terminal value, not VaR. Value changes there come from new information about models (regulation, stack projections), not from tradable prices. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.10 (p.454)

ALM analogy

This module is what an ALM desk already does: key-rate durations rolled up to a few factors, hedges with liquid futures then rolled into swaps, basis between asset and liability curves, and P&L explained by factor. The hierarchy is a factor model built in the order hedges are executed. Corporate price of risk is your cost of capital applied to unhedgeable insurance risk. The structured and price desk split mirrors a treasury transfer-pricing curve with an investment desk taking views. Synthesis

Dated: risk infrastructure

Swindle's and Eydeland and Wolyniec's risk discussions predate today's clearing mandates, initial margin rules for uncleared derivatives and the FRTB capital framework for banks. Margin funding is now a first-order hedging cost (Course 4), and position limits apply to many energy futures. Check CFTC position-limit rules, EMIR and REMIT in Europe, and your clearing house's margin methodology. Outside the texts

Going deeper: why benchmark-first hedging is a liquidity choice

A desk could, in principle, hedge each exposure exactly in its own contract month and location. It doesn't, because liquidity sits at the benchmark and the near tenor. The hedge path (benchmark, then roll, basis and conversion) is a sequence of liquidity trades, each paying a bid-ask spread and leaving a residual. The hierarchy is valuable because it mirrors that path: risk reports, P&L explain and limits then all line up with how risk can actually be reduced. A risk system organised any other way describes positions the desk can't trade out of. Swindle ch.6 (pp.133–135) Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • Book risk breaks into price level, time spread, basis and conversion. Hedge the benchmark first, then roll, basis and convert.
  • A hedging hierarchy rolls deltas up by betas and attributes P&L to each node's unexplained move.
  • Liquid markets make values and hedges additive. Illiquid ones require joint optimisation, bounded by bid-ask.
  • Price unhedgeable risk with an explicit corporate risk adjustment, and measure illiquid tails by terminal distributions.
