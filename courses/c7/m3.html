Tolling and plant valuation

Core≈ 70 min8 quiz questions5 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Describe a daily toll's payoff and exercise mechanics.
  • Show how start costs, minimum load and runtime constraints cut value relative to a spread-option strip.
  • Compare a simple dispatch rule with perfect-foresight dispatch.
  • Convert toll value into a monthly capacity payment and describe the hedge.

Prerequisites

  • Module 2; Course 5 Modules 2–3.

What a toll is

A tolling agreement gives the buyer the right to convert fuel into power through a specific plant, or a financial copy of it. Strictly, "tolling" means the buyer controls physical dispatch, schedules gas and sells power. The financially settled equivalent is a heat-rate option. Swindle ch.14 (pp.335–336) Each day's payoff is the bucket's power price minus heat rate × gas minus VOM (typically $3–6/MWh), if the holder exercised. Exercise is usually by phone the business day before, by around 10 a.m., so weekends and Mondays involve longer lags. Swindle ch.14 (p.336) Tolls exist mainly to support the financing of plants. Swindle notes they are socially useful but have also caused some of the largest losses on commodity desks. Swindle ch.14 (p.335)

Real plants are not spread options

A strip of daily spread options assumes the plant can switch on and off for free each day at a single heat rate. Real units come with a bundle of constraints. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.9 (pp.385–389)

  • Heat curve. Efficiency depends on output, and the incremental heat rate drives dispatch at the margin.
  • Minimum load and start costs. Minimum stable output is roughly 30–50% of capacity for steam units and more for some turbines. Starts cost fuel, power and maintenance, ranging from cold to hot starts. Eydeland and Wolyniec note that high start costs create significant gamma.
  • Ramp rates and minimum up and down times. These limit how quickly the unit can follow prices.
  • Outages. Forced outage rates range from a few per cent to over 20%. One badly timed outage while you are short power in a price spike can be catastrophic.

These constraints cut value most where time value is a large share of total value, which means peaking and cycling units. Eydeland and Wolyniec show that lengthening a peaker's minimum downtime from 8 to 72 hours roughly halves its value, while a deep in-the-money baseload unit loses at most around 10%. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.9 (pp.412–415)

Start costs as an option premium

With a start cost, the decision to commit becomes like exercising an American option. You start only when the expected spread clears a shadow price that depends on price distributions, the unit's characteristics and the operator's risk premium. The plant's value decomposes into a spread option whose strike is the shadow price, minus a digital-like term for the start cost. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.9 (pp.392–394) The consequences for hedging are uncomfortable. Plants with large start costs can show negative vega, deltas above 100% and unstable gammas, depending on the dispatch rule. Eydeland and Wolyniec frame the choice as heroic (maximise value and chase gamma), stoic (maximise value and hedge statically) or safe (choose an easily hedged rule and accept lower value). They lean toward the stoic option. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.9 (pp.395–396)

Worked example 1 · one day's economicsChecked by code

A 400 MW CCGT has a 7.2 heat rate at full load and 8.0 at its 160 MW minimum, $3 VOM and a $25,000 start cost. Monthly forwards: on-peak $45, off-peak $30, gas $4.50.

forward on-peak spark spread = 45 − 7.2 × 4.50 − 3 = $9.60/MWh; overnight at minimum load = 30 − 8.0 × 4.50 − 3 = $−9.00/MWh staying on overnight costs 8 h × 160 MW × $9 = $11,520, less than a $25,000 restart, so a hot unit should usually stay on overnight

This is the core trade-off of cycling a unit: lose money at minimum load overnight, or pay to restart. Mahoney frames the three operating strategies (run at minimum overnight, start and stop daily, start for blocks of days) as alternative payoffs to compare. Mahoney §4.3.2 (p.129)

Valuing dispatch: perfect foresight versus a rule

A full valuation is a stochastic dynamic program (SDP) over daily, or hourly, decisions. Eydeland and Wolyniec caution that SDP hedges can be unstable and dangerous if followed closely. Spread-option formulations give more usable hedges, so they use both approaches. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.9 (p.406) Here we use two benchmarks on simulated price paths. The first is perfect-foresight dispatch, solved backwards along each path, which is an upper bound for any operator. The second is a myopic rule using only that day's prices, which is what a cash desk can actually do with day-ahead information. Synthesis

Worked example 2 · a month of dispatchChecked by code

Using Example 1's unit, we simulate 300 paths of 21 weekdays. Daily on-peak power varies 30% around its forward, off-peak 25% and gas 10%, with power–gas correlation 0.5. The total is 134,400 on-peak MWh.

spread-option strip (free starts, no minimum load): $1,434k = $10.67/MWh perfect-foresight dispatch with start costs and overnight minimum load: $1,224k = $9.11/MWh: 14.6% below the strip about 15.4 run days and 4.3 starts a month; the non-anticipative myopic rule earns $1,189k, or 97.1% of perfect foresight

For an efficient unit, the operating constraints cost about 15%, and a non-anticipative day-ahead rule loses little when daily shocks are independent across days (under persistent multi-day cold snaps or heat waves, serial correlation makes staying hot through positive-spread streaks even more valuable). Whatever rule you use in operation, use the same one in forward valuation, or the hedges will mislead. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.9 (p.406) Synthesis

Near the money, flexibility is most of the value, so constraints and start costs (the "premium" for exercising) remove a much larger share. A lender looking at a peaker valued as a spread-option strip is looking at an overstatement. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.9 (pp.412–415) Synthesis

Hedging a plant or toll

On execution, the first job is to neutralise delta. Power is often too illiquid at size, so desks sell gas first and convert to power as liquidity allows. Swindle ch.14 (pp.346–347) Deltas vary by month, but liquidity sits in calendar strips, so a cal-strip hedge leaves monthly mismatches. These are worse for high heat-rate units, whose delta concentrates in a few months. Swindle ch.14 (pp.347–348) Selling vanilla power calls against a long toll looks tempting but mismatches badly, because both prices move. Swindle ch.14 (p.347) Eydeland and Wolyniec's experience is that forward hedges alone remove 30–40% of cash-flow volatility, rising to 50–60% with volatility hedges and to over 80% with BOM and daily options inside the month. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.9 (pp.417–418)

Lenders usually want asset hedges paid as monthly capacity payments. These limit credit exposure to an asset owner who may be unrated and has pledged the plant. Swindle ch.9 (pp.189–190) When a hedger asks for a more realistic toll replica (ramp rates, multiple buckets, dual fuel), dealers value the extra flexibility at a lower bound. The closer replica therefore costs the hedger more than they may expect. Swindle ch.14 (p.368)

Worked example 4 · converting toll value to a capacity paymentChecked by code

A 5-year, 400 MW toll is worth $30m today. Monthly payments are discounted at 6%.

annuity = 30,000,000 / Σ DF = $578.7k a month → $1.447/kW-month

The toll buyer pays this each month whether or not the plant runs. The seller (plant owner) has turned an uncertain merchant margin into a fixed income stream that lenders can underwrite. Swindle ch.9 (p.190)

ALM analogy

A toll is like a portfolio of path-dependent options with behavioural exercise. The "start cost" works like a prepayment penalty that changes when exercise is optimal. Perfect-foresight value is an upper bound much as an optimal-prepayment model is, and the dispatch rule actually used plays the part of the behavioural model. The capacity payment turns option value into an annuity, like a fee-based guarantee on a variable annuity. Synthesis

Dated: market rules and fleet

Eydeland and Wolyniec describe early-2000s pool designs, such as three-part bids in New York and market or cost schemes in PJM. Swindle's tolls are 2010–11 PJM. Market rules on start-up and no-load compensation, capacity markets and fast-start pricing have changed since, and batteries now compete for the same intraday flexibility. Outside the texts Check the relevant ISO's current energy and capacity market manuals.

Going deeper: forward induction for cumulative constraints

Annual emission caps or run-hour limits link months together, so you can't value each month separately. Eydeland and Wolyniec show that when forward prices and volatilities trade, the dynamic program can be replaced by a forward procedure. At each date, rank the current spot option against the values of future months' options given today's forwards, and use an allowance only if today ranks among the best. As long as forward markets are reasonably efficient, this matches the backward-induction value on a risk-adjusted basis. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.9 (pp.426–428) The same idea underlies multi-period swing and storage valuation (Modules 4–5). Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • A toll is a strip of daily spread options in theory. In practice start costs, minimum load, runtimes and outages cut value.
  • Constraints matter most where optionality dominates (peakers and cycling units).
  • Value with the dispatch rule you will actually use. SDP values are upper bounds, and their hedges can be unstable.
  • Hedge gas first, then power. Accept calendar-strip mismatches, and use capacity payments to manage credit.
