Arbitrage and netbacks

Core≈ 55 min9 quiz questions5 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Compute the delivered margin of an arbitrage including financing and losses.
  • Value a barrel at origin by netback.
  • Express breakeven freight in Worldscale points.
  • Explain why destination flexibility has option value.

Prerequisites

  • Modules 2 and 5.

What an arbitrage is

In oil trading an "arb" is the price difference for the same or a similar grade in two regions. Downey notes that arbitrage spreads are mainly a function of transport costs. Downey ch.18 Moving the barrel is worthwhile when the destination value exceeds origin price plus every cost of getting it there. Trafigura calls this transformation in space. Trafigura CD ch.4, ch.6 Traders who move oil when the arb is open push the two prices back towards a gap equal to transport cost. Arbitrage therefore both ties regional prices together and is self-limiting. Trafigura CD ch.10 Downey ch.18

  • Crude: Downey calls the WTI–Brent spread the most traded oil spread. West African sweet grades, priced off Brent, are the swing barrels: they go west when the Brent discount to US coastal grades covers transatlantic freight, and east when Asian margins are better. Downey Appendix One
  • Products: Long describes gasoline flowing from Europe to the US East Coast when the arb is open, and surplus Asian products heading west to Europe, Latin America and California. Long §3.4.2, §5.4

Netbacks

A netback works backwards from what a barrel is worth delivered to what it is worth at the loading point: destination value less freight and other costs. Downey calls this freight netback pricing and notes its use for locations with little trading. Long shows how Asian and Gulf product quotes are derived as netbacks from Singapore or Japan. Downey ch.17 Long §5.4 The economic value of a cargo at origin is its best netback across feasible destinations. That is why one crude's differential can move when a market thousands of miles away changes. Synthesis

ALM analogy

Netbacks are funds transfer pricing for barrels. FTP charges a business line the market cost of its funding so that its margin is measured fairly. A netback credits the origin with the delivered value less the market cost of transport, so that each step of the chain is judged on its own margin. As with FTP, the answer depends on choosing the right reference market and cost curve. Synthesis

The cost stack of a delivered cargo

An arb calculation is only as good as its cost list. Long §12.7, §12.8 Trafigura CD ch.6, ch.9

  • Freight (Module 5), plus lightering or transhipment where the ship can't berth. Long's 2001 delivery options into the US Gulf ranged from about $1.70 to $2.50/bbl.
  • Insurance on cargo value, and in-transit losses. Long uses about 0.2% for lightering or transhipment.
  • Financing of the cargo for the voyage and payment terms (Course 4).
  • Demurrage, inspection, port and agency fees, and duties where relevant.
  • Timing: the destination price is realised weeks later, so both ends must be hedged or the arb is a flat-price bet (Module 2).
Worked example 1 · which way does the cargo go?Checked by code

A light sweet cargo costs $79.40 FOB. Option A is a US Gulf refiner valuing it at $82.60 delivered: freight $2.10, 40 days of financing. Option B is an Asian refiner at $83.80 delivered: freight $3.30, 55 days. Both use insurance 0.05% of FOB value, 0.2% in-transit loss, 5% financing and $0.05 other costs. All figures are illustrative.

A: insurance $0.040, loss $0.165, finance $0.441 → landed (ex-loss) $82.03 → margin $0.40/bbl B: loss $0.168, finance $0.607 → landed (ex-loss) $83.40 → margin $0.24/bbl

Here "landed" sums FOB, freight, insurance, financing (on an actual/360 money-market basis, the convention trade-finance desks use for SOFR-linked funding, as in Course 4 Module 2) and port fees, while the 0.2% in-transit loss ($0.165/bbl for A) reduces the out-turn revenue collected at destination ($82.60 − $0.165 − $82.03 = $0.40/bbl). Asia pays $1.20 more, but most of that goes on extra freight, and the longer voyage adds financing cost. The cargo goes west. Trafigura CD ch.9 Synthesis

Worked example 2 · breakeven freightChecked by code

How high can freight to the US Gulf go before Option A stops paying? Set freight to zero, compute the margin, and that margin is the breakeven freight. On an illustrative flat rate of $18.00/t:

breakeven freight = $2.50/bbl = WS 102.0, against today's $2.10 = WS 85.5

Expressing the cushion in Worldscale points lets the freight desk say how far rates can rise before the arb closes. If the cargo has not yet been fixed, buying an FFA protects it. Stopford §5.4 Trafigura CD ch.10 Synthesis

Arbitrage in practice

Long's shipping chapter walks through the options for a cargo bought FOB in West Africa and meant for the US: sell it FOB, ship it on a company vessel, charter in and charter out, or sell CFR, CIF or delivered. Each option adds shipping risk and opportunity, and each needs the crude hedged (on Brent with CFDs for the purchase, on a WTI-related basis for the sale). Long §12.7.1 Long also compares discharge choices in the US Gulf (LOOP, lightering, floating storage, Caribbean transhipment or storage) on cost per barrel. Long §12.7.5, §12.8.4 Trafigura's point is that such arbitrage is increasingly fleeting as information spreads. Firms that own storage, blending and logistics can act quickly enough to capture it. Trafigura CD ch.7, ch.9

Dated: flows and spreads

The texts predate today's trade map. US crude exports grew after the 2015 end of the export ban (Course 1, Module 6), which reversed the old Atlantic arb direction. WTI Midland now forms part of the Dated Brent basket, and sanctions since 2022 have rerouted Russian crude and products to Asia, lengthening voyages and changing freight. All of this is outside the texts. Outside the texts Check the EIA (trade flows, STEO), IEA Oil Market Report, PRA arb commentaries and Kpler or Vortexa tracking summaries for current flows.

Going deeper: destination flexibility is an option

A cargo that can still choose between destinations after you have bought it is worth more than its best netback today. It is worth the expected value of the better of the two later. If today's margins are $0.40 (A) and $0.24 (B), a fixed decision locks in $0.40. Keeping the choice open until the arb settles is worth $0.40 plus the expected gain from switching if B overtakes A. That gain grows with the volatility of the A–B spread and falls with their correlation. Structurally it is an option to exchange one delivered value for another (a spread option), the subject of Course 7. Synthesis Long §12.7.1 This is why traders value FOB cargoes, open-destination contracts and controllable logistics. It is also why producers' destination restrictions take value away from buyers. Long §3.2.2

Key takeaways

  • An arb pays when destination value exceeds FOB plus freight, insurance, losses, financing and other costs. Margins are thin, so each line matters.
  • Netbacks value a barrel at origin from its best destination. A grade's differential moves with distant markets.
  • Breakeven freight, expressed in WS points, tells the freight desk how much cushion the arb has.
  • Destination flexibility is a valuable option. Restrictions on destination or resale remove it.
