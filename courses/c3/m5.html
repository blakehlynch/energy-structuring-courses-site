The tanker fleet

Tankers are classed by deadweight (DWT). From small to large: product tankers (Handysize and MR), Panamax or LR1, Aframax or LR2, Suezmax (the largest that can transit Suez laden), VLCC (about 200–320 kt) and ULCC. Downey ch.11 Trafigura CD ch.6 Rough rules of thumb: about 7.5 bbl per DWT, a "boatload" of 500,000 bbl, a VLCC of about 2 million bbl. Clean tankers have coated tanks for light products and can't easily switch to dirty cargoes (crude, fuel oil). Downey ch.11 Big ships are cheaper per tonne but can't enter many ports, hence lightering, offshore terminals such as LOOP, and transhipment. Downey ch.11 Long §12.7.5

Trading houses mix time charters (flexibility and control, which they can relet) with voyage charters for spot opportunities. Their freight desks hedge freight and bunker costs. Trafigura CD ch.6 Fixing a ship involves brokers, a firm offer, "subjects" (a provisional booking), and finally a charter party, often on a standard form. Downey ch.11 Stopford §5.3

Worldscale

Spot tanker freight is quoted in Worldscale points. Each year the Worldscale Association publishes a "flat rate" (WS100) in $/tonne for every route. It is the cost of a standard 75,000 t tanker making the round voyage at a standard speed, fuel consumption and port time. A fixture at WS 60 pays 60% of the flat rate. Stopford §5.3 Downey ch.11 The convention lets owners compare earnings across routes and lets charterers keep load and discharge options open. Stopford §5.3

Worked example 1 · freight from WorldscaleChecked by code

A VLCC is fixed for 270,000 t on a long-haul route whose flat rate is an illustrative $19.80/t, at WS 60.

rate = 19.80 × 0.60 = $11.880/t → freight = $3.21 million → $1.62/bbl at 7.33 bbl/t

Larger parcels fix at lower WS points on the same route (Stopford notes a VLCC fixture at half the points of a 52,000 t products parcel). Long's rule of thumb: within a size band on one route, cargo size × WS is roughly constant, so a 125,000 t fixture at WS 70 implies about WS 67.3 for 130,000 t. Stopford §5.3 Long §12.7.4

Worked example 2 · voyage cost and time-charter equivalentChecked by code

For the same ship and voyage: 6,600 nm each way, 13 knots, 4 port days, 70 t/day of fuel at sea and 15 t/day in port, bunkers at $550/t, port costs $250,000.

sea days = 2 × 6,600 / (13 × 24) = 42.3; total = 46.3 days bunkers = $1.66 m; voyage cost = $1.91 m TCE = (3.21 − 1.91) m / 46.3 days = $27,981/day

The time-charter equivalent converts a spot fixture into the daily rate that owners compare with time-charter offers and with operating costs. Downey describes TCE as the bridge between spot and period markets. Downey ch.11 Stopford §5.3

Floating storage economics and charterparty options

When steep contango exceeds the carrying cost of land-based tanks, trading desks charter tankers as floating storage (e.g. 2 million barrel VLCCs). The carry hurdle must cover: (1) daily time-charter hire, (2) crude working-capital financing, (3) marine cargo insurance, and (4) operating costs such as bunkers burned at anchor for auxiliary power and cargo heating, and hull fouling (charterparty clauses often suspend the owner's speed and consumption warranties after 20–30 days idle at anchor, requiring hull-cleaning before redelivery). Downey ch.11–12 Stopford §5.3 Outside the texts

Worked example 2b · VLCC floating storage contango breakevenChecked by code

A desk considers chartering a 2 MMbbl VLCC for 6 months of floating storage. VLCC time charter hire is $45,000/day. Spot crude is $75.00/bbl, financing is SOFR+2% = 6.0% p.a., cargo insurance is $0.025/bbl-month, and bunkers at anchor plus hull cleaning are $0.02/bbl-month. What 6-month curve contango is needed to break even?

vessel hire per bbl-month = ($45,000 × 30.4167) / 2,000,000 bbl = $0.68/bbl-mo crude financing per bbl-mo = $75.00 × (6.0% / 12) = $0.38/bbl-mo insurance + other costs = 0.025 + 0.02 = $0.045/bbl-mo total carrying cost per mo = 0.684 + 0.375 + 0.045 = $1.10/bbl-mo required 6-month contango = 6 × $1.104 = $6.63/bbl

Unless the M6 − M1 forward spread exceeds $6.63/bbl (an annualised log forward yield of about 17%), floating storage destroys value. Desks often structure time charters with an optional storage rider at a reduced daily hire rate while stationary. Downey ch.11 Synthesis

The freight market and its derivatives

Stopford describes four linked shipping markets: freight, sale and purchase, newbuilding and demolition. Cash earned in the freight market drives orders, and deliveries a few years later depress rates, so the shipping cycle has the same lag-driven boom-bust shape as Course 1's oil cycle. Stopford §5.2 Freight therefore adds a volatile cost to every delivered trade. Forward freight agreements (FFAs) settle against Baltic Exchange or Platts route assessments (for example TD3 for a VLCC from the Middle East Gulf to the Far East). Charterers are natural buyers and owners natural sellers. Downey ch.11 Stopford §5.4 Trafigura CD ch.10 Bunker fuel, a large part of voyage cost, is hedged separately with bunker swaps. Trafigura CD ch.6

Vessel arbitrage. Long describes two plays. In a timing play you relet your own ship and charter another to fit a laycan. In a size play you charter out a smaller ship and charter in a slightly larger one at fewer WS points, then fill the extra space within the cargo tolerance. Long §12.7.4

ALM analogy

Worldscale is a quoting convention like pricing a spread as a percentage of a published reference curve: the reference (flat rate) resets annually and the market trades the multiplier. TCE is the shipping version of net interest margin per unit of capital per day. It strips out pass-through costs so that different deployments (spot voyage, time charter, storage) can be compared on one yardstick. Synthesis

Dated: freight and regulation

Rate levels, fleet data and fuel types in the texts are dated. Since then: IMO 2020 sulphur rules shifted bunkers to very-low-sulphur fuel oil and scrubbers; carbon-intensity rules (CII and EEXI) and the EU ETS now cover shipping; sanctions created a separate "shadow" tanker fleet; and route disruptions in the Red Sea and Black Sea have periodically lengthened voyages. All are outside the texts. Outside the texts Check the Worldscale Association, Baltic Exchange route definitions, Clarksons and the IMO for current rules.

Going deeper: how fast to sail

Stopford's cube rule says fuel burn at sea rises with roughly the cube of speed. Faster sailing shortens the voyage but burns disproportionately more fuel. With the fixture above, TCE is $32,563/day at 11 knots and $18,591/day at 15 knots. The TCE-maximising speed is about 9.8 knots, worth $33,248/day. Stopford ch.6 Synthesis

  • At WS 120 instead of 60, days are worth more and the optimum rises to about 14.1 knots.
  • At $800 bunkers, fuel is dearer and the optimum falls to about 8.2 knots.

This is why fleets slow-steam in weak markets and speed up in strong ones, effectively changing supply without changing the number of ships. Stopford counts slow steaming as part of the supply response. Stopford ch.4–6 In practice charter parties specify speed and consumption, and laycans and cargo deadlines constrain the choice. Stopford §5.3

Key takeaways

  • Size drives cost per tonne; port and canal limits drive which size you can use.
  • Voyage charter: the owner bears voyage costs. Time charter: the charterer does. TCE puts them on one footing.
  • Worldscale fixes are points on an annual flat rate; larger cargoes fix at fewer points.
  • Freight is a volatile, hedgeable cost (FFAs and bunker swaps). Speed choice is itself an economic lever.
