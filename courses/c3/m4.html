How oil contracts are made

Oil is mostly sold by phone or chat, and the deal is binding when agreed. Long describes the industry as running on "my word is my bond". Long §13.1.1 The agreed main terms (grade, quantity and tolerance, price formula, delivery window, Incoterm, payment) are then written into a confirmation that incorporates one party's general terms and conditions. Long reviews the express terms one by one: parties, quality, quantity, price, payment, title and risk, bookout, delivery, nominations, transport, inspection, force majeure, restrictions, duties and taxes, assignment, liability, notices, and governing law. Long §16.2–16.3

  • Title and risk pass at a defined point, usually as the oil passes the flange at the hose-to-manifold flange. They need not pass together. The buyer typically wants a warranty of title free of liens. Long §16.3.8
  • Quantity carries an operational tolerance, often ±1% to ±5% at one party's option. Volumes change with temperature and evaporation. Downey ch.11 Long §16.3.5
  • Force majeure must define qualifying events, notice and consequences. A seller in a chain needs its sale terms to mirror its purchase terms so it isn't left liable when its own supplier is excused. Long §16.3.14
  • Restrictions: some producers' formulas apply only in specified regions, and some forbid resale to traders. Long §3.2.2, §16.3.15

Incoterms: who ships, who insures, where risk passes

Incoterms are ICC rules that standardise trade terms and must be incorporated by name and edition. They cover delivery, costs and the passing of risk, but not title, force majeure or governing law. Long §16.5.1 In oil the common terms are FOB, CFR, CIF and delivered terms. Downey ch.11 The key subtlety of the C terms: the seller pays freight to destination, but risk passes to the buyer at the loading port. Long §16.5.1

Worked example 1 · building a CIF price from FOBChecked by code

A grade worth $78.40 FOB is offered CIF Rotterdam. Freight is $2.60/bbl. Cargo insurance costs 0.04% on 110% of the CIF value, a common convention Outside the texts.

CFR = 78.40 + 2.60 = $81.00 CIF = CFR / (1 − 1.1 × 0.0004) = $81.0357 (insurance ≈ $0.0357/bbl)

Selling CIF rather than FOB means the seller takes on the freight market (rates can move after the sale is priced), voyage timing and the insurance arrangements, and earns or loses on each. Long's shipping chapter weighs exactly this choice for a cargo bought FOB: sell FOB, or ship it yourself and sell CFR, CIF or delivered. Long §12.7.1

Nominations, laytime and demurrage

Under FOB the buyer nominates a vessel that the terminal must approve (age, double hull, vetting). The vessel must arrive within the laycan; if it is late the other party may cancel. Downey ch.11 On arrival the master tenders a notice of readiness (NOR). Laytime normally starts a fixed number of hours after NOR (six is common), or on berthing if that is earlier. Time spent shifting berths or waiting for reasons the contract excludes does not count. Long §16.3.12 Downey ch.11

  • Two layers of demurrage. Supply (contract) demurrage is between seller and buyer under the sale contract; Long cites 36 hours as typical laytime for a straightforward crude loading. Charter-party demurrage is between charterer and shipowner and covers the whole voyage's port time (typically 72 hours). Long §12.6
  • Rates come from the charter party or a referenced assessment (e.g. AFRA or the London Tanker Brokers' Panel). Claims are often time-barred after 60–90 days, so paperwork discipline is money. Long §12.6, §16.3.12
  • Despatch, a reward for finishing early, is common in dry bulk charters. Stopford gives the mechanics. Stopford §5.3
Worked example 2 · a laytime statementChecked by code

The vessel tenders NOR at hour 6. Laytime starts 6 hours after NOR. Allowed laytime is 36 hours, loading completes at hour 62, and 4 hours of shifting are excluded. The demurrage rate is $45,000/day.

laytime starts at hour 12; time used = 62 − 12 − 4 = 46 h; excess = 10 h demurrage = 10/24 × $45,000 = $18,750

Disputes are usually about the inputs: was the NOR valid (vessel actually ready, inside the port)? Which delays were excluded? Who caused them? Long §12.6, §16.3.12

Worked example 3 · supply and charter-party demurrage don't matchChecked by code

A shipper's supply contract allows 36 h at the load port; its charter party allows 72 h for load plus discharge, both at $40,000/day (Long's structure, own numbers).

case A: load 50 h (supplier's fault), discharge 20 h → claim from supplier 14 h = $23,333; owes owner $0 (70 h ≤ 72 h) case B: load 50 h, discharge 40 h → owes owner 18 h = $30,000; net after supplier claim $−6,667

In case A the shipper profits from demurrage, as Long notes can happen. In case B the receiver's slow discharge costs money unless the sale contract passes discharge demurrage on. Suppliers sometimes cap their liability at a share of charter-party demurrage. Long §12.6

ALM analogy

Demurrage chains behave like back-to-back hedges of a liability. The shipper owes the owner under one set of terms and recovers from suppliers and receivers under others. Any mismatch in allowances, rates, exclusions or time bars is residual basis that lands on whoever sits in the middle. Aligning terms across the chain is the oil-contract version of matching hedge terms to the hedged item. Synthesis

Dated: contract forms and rules

Long cites Incoterms 2000. Later editions (2010, 2020) dropped DES and DEQ in favour of DAP and DAT, then DPU, and revised insurance minimums. Modern oil GT&Cs (for example the BP and Shell terms, and Energy Institute model clauses) now carry sanctions, anti-corruption and war-risk provisions that the texts barely mention. Outside the texts Use the current ICC Incoterms rules and the counterparty's latest GT&Cs; take legal advice on governing law.

Going deeper: where value leaks in execution

Trafigura reminds readers that physical trading margins are thin, typically 3% or less per transaction. Execution costs such as demurrage, losses, off-spec claims and missed laycans can therefore erase a trade's margin. Trafigura CD ch.9 Long makes the same point about counterparties: a deal that looks profitable on paper can lose money through demurrage bills and quality problems. Long §13.2.3 For an originator, the lesson is to price execution risk into the differential and to align terms through the chain: laytime, demurrage caps, force majeure, inspection and payment triggers. Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • Deals are binding when agreed. The confirmation plus GT&Cs governs quality, quantity, title and risk, force majeure and law.
  • Incoterms allocate costs and risk, not title. In C terms the seller pays freight, but risk passes at loading.
  • Laytime runs from NOR plus notice (or berthing), less exclusions. Demurrage is paid for the excess at the agreed rate.
  • Supply and charter-party demurrage differ; mismatched terms leave residual exposure.
