Operations, credit and risk control

Prerequisites

  • Modules 3–4.

From deal to delivery: the operator's job

Once a trader closes a deal, an operator executes it. Trafigura lists the tasks: coordinate chartering, loading and discharge schedules to limit lost time; monitor demurrage; work with vessel brokers and port agents; appoint independent inspectors; track every payment and cost; check insurance; troubleshoot off-spec or short cargoes; and audit volumes at each stage. Trafigura runs over 2,000 voyages a year through such protocols. Trafigura CD ch.9 Long adds that shipping knowledge doubles as market intelligence: vessel movements reveal others' supply patterns. Long §12.7.1

  • Inspection. Inspectors witness loading, sample shore and ship tanks, and issue quantity and quality certificates. Long's model instructions ask for a protest when bill of lading and ship figures differ by more than 0.2%. Long App. 12.1 Trafigura CD ch.9
  • Bill of lading. The master's receipt and the document of title. A buyer cannot claim cargo without it, and LCs typically require the full set of originals ("3/3"). Long §13.3.3 Trafigura CD ch.9
  • Letter of indemnity (LOI). In long chains the originals often trail the ship. The seller then gives an LOI, often bank-countersigned, warranting title free of liens and indemnifying the buyer, and the ship discharges against it. Long notes chains of over 100 parties. Long §12.7.3, §13.3.5
Worked example 1 · re-allocating bills of lading after a two-port dischargeChecked by code

A ship loads 80,000 t of gasoil: 50,000 t for receiver A and 30,000 t for B. Out-turns are 49,820 t and 29,700 t (Long's method, own numbers).

total out-turn = 79,520 t → overall loss 0.60% on original bills: A shows −0.36%, B shows −1.00% re-allocated bills (pro rata to out-turn): A 50,121 t, B 29,879 t → each loses 0.60%

The ship's loss belongs to the cargo as a whole. Re-allocation shares it equitably instead of leaving it with whichever receiver discharged second (in practice, because original bills of lading are negotiable title documents that cannot be unilaterally altered after issue without surrendering the full set to the carrier, co-mingled parcel receivers settle this pro-rata shortage commercially via out-turn adjustment invoices or an agent's out-turn allocation certificate). Long §12.8.7 Synthesis

Credit: why it matters in physical oil

Long's credit chapter starts from the obvious. A paper profit is worthless if a counterparty, or anyone in the chain, fails to deliver, take delivery or pay. Payment often falls due 30 days or more after B/L. A single cargo is worth tens of millions of dollars, and new trading companies appear constantly. Long §13.1 Assessment weighs the amount at risk against the counterparty's size, country and political risk, reputation and experience, financial standing and existing exposures. Much of this is hard to observe for private traders. Long §13.2

Exposure has two phases. Synthesis

  1. Before delivery the risk is replacement cost. If the buyer walks away and prices have fallen, you resell lower.
  2. After B/L the risk is the receivable: the whole invoice until payment.

Security instruments target the second phase. Long §13.3

Worked example 2 · the cost of an LCChecked by code

A 1 million bbl cargo at $80 ($80 m). The LC fee is 0.12% and confirmation by the seller's bank adds 0.08% (illustrative, within and above Long's 0.10–0.15% range).

cost = $80 m × (0.12% + 0.08%) = $160,000, i.e. $0.16/bbl

On a trade earning well under $1/bbl, security is a material cost. Who pays for it, usually the buyer (with confirmation costs negotiated), is a commercial term worth negotiating. Long §13.3.3, §16.5.2

Worked example 3 · an exposure profileChecked by code

The same cargo is sold on 30 days' credit after a B/L on day 20. Prices fall $6 between deal and loading.

open credit: unsecured exposure peaks at $80 m (the receivable) until payment on day 50 with an LC: receivable covered; remaining peak = pre-delivery replacement cost $6.0 m

An LC protects the receivable, not the risk that the buyer walks away before loading in a falling market. That pre-delivery risk is managed with credit limits, performance guarantees or margining. Long §13.3 Synthesis

Credit exposure through a cargo's life

Choose security and terms

Stylised: pre-delivery exposure builds to the replacement loss by B/L; from B/L to payment the receivable is at risk. The "50% effective" guarantee is a teaching assumption, not a rule.

ALM analogy

This is the counterparty-credit split familiar from derivatives. Pre-delivery replacement cost is like potential future exposure on an unsettled trade. The post-B/L receivable is like settlement or current exposure. An LC substitutes a bank's credit for the buyer's, much as a guarantee or CSA does. Pricing the trade with expected loss and the cost of security mirrors credit valuation adjustments. Synthesis

Risk control on a trading desk

  • Hedge flat price systematically and run basis as the managed risk. Trafigura describes this as its core practice. Trafigura CD ch.10
  • Separate front, middle and back office, mark positions to market, and match each hedge to its physical deal. Long cites the Group of Thirty's 1993 derivatives recommendations and the tax and accounting push for marking to market. Long §13.1.3, ch.14
  • Legal capacity. Check that public-sector counterparties are actually authorised to trade derivatives. Long recalls municipalities that escaped losing deals by claiming they lacked authority. Long §13.1.3
  • Futures broker risk. The exchange protects the broker, not you. Watch the broker's finances and consider segregated accounts. Long §13.1.2
  • Limits and measurement: position, stop-loss, credit and VaR limits. Long's risk-management chapter introduces them. Long ch.18 Course 7, Module 6 shows how rolled-up delta limits on a hedging hierarchy work, and why historical VaR can understate illiquid exposures. VaR methodology itself lies outside these courses. Outside the texts
Dated: documents, compliance and fraud

Long's credit chapter is from 2002. Since then, UCP 600 (2007) replaced earlier LC rules. Electronic bills of lading and the UK Electronic Trade Documents Act (2023) are changing documentation. Sanctions, KYC and anti-money-laundering screening are now central. Several large commodity trade-finance frauds around 2020, involving duplicated or fictitious documents, led banks to cut lines to smaller traders. Outside the texts Course 4 covers these in depth. Check ICC rules, OFAC and EU sanctions guidance, and recent bank trade-finance reports.

Going deeper: is the LC worth it?

Compare the cost of security with the expected loss it removes. With exposure at default of $80 m, a 60% loss given default, a counterparty annual default probability of 2% and 30 days at risk (the receivable exists only from B/L on day 20 to payment on day 50; before B/L the exposure is the $6 m replacement cost, which the LC doesn't cover anyway):

\[ EL = EAD \times LGD \times \bigl(1-(1-PD)^{30/365}\bigr) \]

That gives EL ≈ $79,638, about $0.08/bbl. That is below the $160,000 LC cost, so for this counterparty an LC is expensive insurance, unless capital, limits or tail risk matter more than expected loss. At an 8% default probability EL is $327,833, and the LC is clearly worth buying. Synthesis Outside the texts Real decisions also weigh concentration, correlation with market moves (counterparties tend to fail when prices crash) and credit-line usage. Credit risk models appear in Course 4 and in Clewlow and Strickland's chapter on credit risk. Clewlow & Strickland ch.11

Key takeaways

  • Operations protect the margin: inspection, documents, demurrage, insurance and reconciliation.
  • B/Ls are documents of title. LOIs bridge missing originals but carry title and indemnity risk.
  • Credit exposure is replacement cost before delivery and the receivable after. LCs cover the receivable at a real cost.
  • Desk control rests on systematic hedging, marked-to-market books, segregation of duties and limits.
