Mean reversion, Schwartz models and jumps

Core≈ 65 min8 quiz questions9 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Simulate a mean-reverting log price and interpret its half-life.
  • Compute Schwartz one-factor forwards and forward volatilities.
  • Explain how the two-factor model adds a stochastic convenience yield.
  • Estimate mean reversion by regression and jumps with a recursive filter.

Prerequisites

  • Module 1; basic familiarity with Brownian motion and lognormal prices.

Why geometric Brownian motion is not enough

Black–Scholes assumes prices wander with no memory and a constant volatility. Energy spot prices behave differently. They are pulled back toward a level set by production cost, their volatility changes over time, and they jump, power most of all. Clewlow & Strickland §2.1 Clewlow and Strickland extend GBM in three directions (mean reversion, stochastic volatility and jumps) and simulate each with Monte Carlo, which is the standard way to understand and price with these models. Clewlow & Strickland §2.1, §2.13

Mean reversion and half-life

In the simplest model the log price \(x=\ln S\) follows an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process:

\[ dx = \alpha(\bar{x} - x)\,dt + \sigma\,dz \]

Above the long-run level the drift is negative, and below it the drift is positive. A random shock can still push the price further away in any short interval. Clewlow & Strickland §2.2 Ignoring the noise, the gap decays exponentially. The half-life, the expected time to close half the gap, is \(t_{1/2} = \ln 2/\alpha\). It is an average over many shocks, not a promise about any single one. Clewlow & Strickland §2.4

Worked example 1 · half-livesChecked by code
α = 8 per year → t½ = ln 2 / 8 = 31.6 days (a gas-like speed) α = 50 per year → t½ = 5.06 days (a power-like speed)

Clewlow and Strickland's Henry Hub estimate for 1999–2000 put the reversion speed near eleven, a half-life of a few weeks. Clewlow & Strickland §2.2, §2.9 When simulating, use time steps that are small relative to the half-life, because the simple Euler discretisation is exact only as the step shrinks; for large \(\alpha\Delta t\) (such as daily steps at \(\alpha=50\)), an exact Ornstein–Uhlenbeck transition uses decay factor \(e^{-\alpha\Delta t}\) and innovation variance \(\frac{\sigma^2}{2\alpha}(1-e^{-2\alpha\Delta t})\). Clewlow & Strickland §2.3 Synthesis

From spot model to forward curve: Schwartz one-factor

If the log spot is OU, the forward price follows in closed form. Note carefully how Schwartz (1997) parameterises the drift in levels, \[ dS = \alpha(\mu - \ln S)S\,dt + \sigma S\,dz: \] by Itô's lemma, the stationary log-mean is \(\bar{x} = \mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2\alpha}\). With reversion speed \(\alpha\), volatility \(\sigma\), drift parameter \(\mu\) and market price of risk \(\lambda\), Schwartz's formula is

\[ \ln F(t,T) = e^{-\alpha\tau}\ln S + \left(1-e^{-\alpha\tau}\right)\hat\alpha + \frac{\sigma^2}{4\alpha}\left(1-e^{-2\alpha\tau}\right) \] \[ \hat\alpha = \mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2\alpha} - \lambda = \bar{x} - \lambda \]

where \(\tau = T-t\). Short tenors start near spot, and long tenors flatten to \(\exp(\bar{x} - \lambda + \sigma^2/4\alpha)\), which equals \(\exp(\mu-\lambda-\sigma^2/4\alpha)\), which is not \(e^{\mu}\). Holding the log-mean \(\bar{x}=\ln L\) fixed, Jensen's convexity \(+\frac{\sigma^2}{4\alpha}\) lifts \(F(t,\infty)\) above \(L\) (to \(\$51.01\) at \(L=\$50\)); holding Schwartz's drift parameter \(\mu=\ln 50\) fixed instead lets the Itô drift pull \(\bar{x}\) down by \(-\frac{\sigma^2}{2\alpha}\), so net of convexity \(F(t,\infty)\) sits slightly below \(e^\mu\) at \(\$49.01\). Clewlow & Strickland §6.2 Forward volatility decays as \(\sigma e^{-\alpha\tau}\). That captures the empirical fact that long-dated forwards move less than spot, but it decays too far: the model sends long-dated volatility to almost zero, which markets never show. Clewlow & Strickland §6.2

Worked example 2 · a Schwartz forward curveChecked by code

Spot is $60 after a supply shock. The drift level is $50 (\(\mu=\ln 50\)), with \(\sigma\) = 40%, \(\alpha\) = 2 and \(\lambda\) = 0.

F(3 months) = $55.67; F(1 year) = $50.49; F(∞) = 50 × e^{−0.16/8} = $49.01 forward vol: 3 months 24.3%, 1 year 5.4%

A curve from a single spot model is backwardated after a positive shock and contango after a negative one. Only a one-hump shape is possible, and a 1-year volatility of 5% is far below typical market levels. In practice the analytical curve is replaced by market forwards, which amounts to letting the long-run level vary with time. Clewlow & Strickland §6.2

Schwartz one-factor curve

Spot, long-run level, speed and volatility → forward curve and forward volatility

Raise α and the curve snaps to its asymptote within months; lower it and the shock persists. With Schwartz's drift parameter μ held fixed, raising σ lowers the log-mean faster (−σ²/2α) than Jensen's convexity (+σ²/4α) lifts the forward, pulling F(∞) = e^(μ − σ²/4α) slightly below e^μ.

Two factors: a stochastic convenience yield

One factor forces every forward to move together and long-dated volatility to vanish. Gibson and Schwartz, Schwartz (1997) and Hilliard–Reis add a second factor: spot follows GBM, while the convenience yield \(\delta\) is itself mean-reverting and correlated with spot. Clewlow & Strickland §6.3 Forward volatility then becomes

\[ \sigma_F(\tau) = \sqrt{\sigma_S^2 + \sigma_\delta^2 H_\delta(\tau)^2 - 2\rho\,\sigma_S\sigma_\delta H_\delta(\tau)},\qquad H_\delta(\tau) = \frac{1-e^{-\alpha_\delta \tau}}{\alpha_\delta} \]

so long-dated volatility levels off instead of going to zero. With high positive spot–convenience correlation, a spot rally is partly absorbed by a rising convenience yield, which is backwardation in motion. Clewlow & Strickland §6.3 The curve shapes are richer, but the model still can't fit multiple humps. Desks therefore take forwards from the market and use the model only for dynamics. Clewlow & Strickland §6.3 Pilipovic's alternative lets spot revert to a long-term level that is itself random. Clewlow & Strickland §6.3

Jumps and spikes

Merton's jump-diffusion adds randomly timed jumps with frequency \(\phi\) per year and normally distributed log sizes. Clewlow & Strickland §2.7 Pure jump-diffusion fits power badly, because after a jump the price stays at the new level. Power spikes revert within hours or days, so Clewlow and Strickland combine jumps with very strong mean reversion, which resembles half-hourly pool prices. Clewlow & Strickland §2.8 They also note that energy jumps are positively skewed, so upward and downward jumps should have different distributions. Clewlow & Strickland §2.7

Estimating the parameters

  • Mean reversion by regression. Discretised, \(\Delta x_t = a_0 + a_1 x_t + \varepsilon\), with \(\alpha = -a_1/\Delta t\). Reversion is slow for most energy, so expect wide confidence intervals unless you have at least a year of daily data. Clewlow & Strickland §2.9, §2.12
  • Jumps by recursive filter. Estimate volatility, flag returns beyond (say) three standard deviations as jumps, re-estimate without them, and repeat until nothing new is flagged. Jump frequency is the count per year, and jump size and volatility come from the flagged returns. Maximum likelihood tends to lock onto the smallest, most frequent jump component, while the filter picks out the large, rare spikes that matter. Clewlow & Strickland §2.10–2.11
  • Seasonality. Let volatility, reversion speed and jump parameters vary by season. Power has enough intraday data to estimate them monthly or even weekly. Clewlow & Strickland §2.12
Worked example 3 · estimating a simulated spot seriesChecked by code

We simulate two years of daily prices with true \(\alpha\) = 8, a $50 level, 60% diffusion volatility and about six upward jumps a year (mean +0.25 in log terms). The path contains 8 jumps. Then we estimate the parameters as a desk would.

regression: α̂ = 8.69 (half-life 29.1 days), level $56.40, residual vol 83.2% recursive filter (3 sd): 10 jumps flagged (5.0 a year), diffusion vol 56.7%

Without accounting for jumps, the level is biased up by about 13% and volatility by about 40%, because the spikes are being read as diffusion. The filter recovers the diffusion volatility well but miscounts jumps: it flags some large diffusion moves and misses small jumps. Even with known parameters, two years of data leave real estimation error, which is a reason to calibrate to market prices wherever they exist. Clewlow & Strickland §2.9–2.10 Synthesis

ALM analogy

The OU log-price is the Vasicek short-rate model with prices in place of rates, and Schwartz's forward formula is the Vasicek bond price formula with the sign changed. The half-life is the decay of a rate shock, and forward volatility decaying with tenor is why long bond yields move less than short rates in a one-factor model. Like Vasicek, one factor can't fit an arbitrary initial curve. The fix is the same (a time-dependent level, as in Hull–White), and so is the remaining weakness: every tenor is perfectly correlated. Synthesis

Dated: negative prices

Clewlow and Strickland's models exclude negative prices and suggest shifting the series if negatives are rare. Clewlow & Strickland §2.1 Since then, negative power prices have become routine in markets with large renewable shares (Germany, California, South Australia, Texas), and WTI futures settled negative (−$37.63/bbl) on 20 April 2020. Log-price models can't produce them. Use shifted-lognormal, arithmetic (Bachelier) or regime-switching models where negatives matter, and check current exchange rules on negative-price settlement. Outside the texts

Option valuation with negative prices: Bachelier vs Shifted Black-76

When underlying forward prices \(F\) or strikes \(K\) can be zero or negative, standard geometric Brownian motion models fail because \(\ln(F/K)\) is undefined. Desks use two standard fixes: (1) Bachelier's normal model: \(dF_t = \sigma_N dW_t\), with call price \(C = e^{-r T} [(F - K)\Phi(d) + \sigma_N \sqrt{T}\phi(d)]\) where \(d = (F - K)/(\sigma_N \sqrt{T})\), and put by parity \(P = C - e^{-r T}(F - K)\); (2) Shifted Black-76 model: Displace prices by an arbitrary positive shift \(s\) such that \(F + s > 0\) and \(K + s > 0\), evaluating standard Black-76 on \(F' = F + s\) and \(K' = K + s\). When WTI futures approached zero in April 2020, CME switched the pricing model for its crude oil options (settlement and margining) to Bachelier so that negative underlying prices and strikes could be handled; other venues and products made similar changes. Outside the texts Synthesis

Worked example 4 · pricing an off-peak power option in negative territoryChecked by code

A solar-flooded off-peak power forward clears at \(F = -\$10.00/\text{MWh}\). We value a 3-month (\(T = 0.25\) yr, \(r = 4\%\)) call option struck at \(K = -\$15.00/\text{MWh}\) (in the money by $5/MWh):

Bachelier normal model (normal volatility σN = $20.00/MWh per √yr): normalized moneyness d = (−10 − (−15)) / (20 × √0.25) = +0.50 discount factor e^(−0.04 × 0.25) = 0.9900 Bachelier call option premium = $6.91/MWh Bachelier put option premium = $1.96/MWh Shifted Black-76 model (shift s = $50, so F' = $40, K' = $35; lognormal σ = 50%): shifted Black-76 call premium = $6.68/MWh

Both models price straight through zero. The premiums differ because a 50% lognormal volatility on the shifted $40 forward is only roughly a $20 normal volatility (50% × $40 = $20), and the choice of shift itself sets the skew: a smaller shift makes the model more lognormal. Calibrate the shift and volatility to traded options rather than treating either as a free input. Outside the texts Synthesis

Going deeper: forward volatility is integrated spot reversion

In the Schwartz model the volatility of a \(\tau\)-maturity forward is \(\sigma e^{-\alpha\tau}\), so the variance an option sees between today and expiry \(T\) on a forward maturing at \(s\) is the integral of \(\sigma^2 e^{-2\alpha(s-u)}\) over \(u\) from now to \(T\). Clewlow and Strickland show the option price then has Black's form, with that integrated variance in place of \(\sigma^2 T\). As a result \(\alpha\) and \(\sigma\) can be made time-dependent (seasonal) without changing the formula, only the integral. Clewlow & Strickland §6.2 Module 5 uses the same idea to value options with non-standard expiries.

Key takeaways

  • Energy spot needs mean reversion, time-varying volatility and jumps. Half-life = ln 2 / α.
  • Schwartz one-factor gives closed-form forwards and forward volatility σe^{−ατ}, which falls too steeply at long tenors.
  • Two-factor models add a stochastic convenience yield and keep long-dated volatility alive.
  • Estimate reversion by regression and jumps by recursive filtering. Ignoring jumps biases both level and volatility.
