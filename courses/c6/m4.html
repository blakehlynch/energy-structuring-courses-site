Curve dynamics and factor models

Core≈ 60 min8 quiz questions8 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Parameterise the volatility term structure with exponentials.
  • Run and read a principal-components analysis of forward returns.
  • Relate forward-curve models to spot mean reversion.
  • Size a minimum-variance hedge of a long-dated exposure with a liquid contract.

Prerequisites

  • Module 3; covariance matrices.

Model the whole curve, not just spot

Module 3 started from spot and derived forwards. Forward-curve models start from the observed curve and specify how every point moves. Each forward follows

\[ \frac{dF(t,T)}{F(t,T)} = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_i(t,T)\,dz_i(t) \]

There is no drift, because a forward costs nothing to enter and so has zero expected return under the pricing measure. The model fits today's curve by construction. Clewlow & Strickland §8.2, §8.4 The work lies in choosing the volatility functions \(\sigma_i\), from historical data or from option prices. Clewlow & Strickland §8.6 The two approaches are linked. A single factor \(\sigma e^{-\alpha(T-t)}\) implies the Schwartz mean-reverting spot of Module 3, with a time-dependent drift that fits the initial curve. Mean reversion in spot and decaying forward volatility are two views of the same thing. Clewlow & Strickland §8.5

Getting clean data first

Commodity contracts expire, so raw data is not stationary. Each contract's tenor shrinks every day, and the front contract disappears at expiry. Swindle ch.5 (p.93) There are two remedies. One is to compute returns per contract and join them into nearby series, never differencing across a roll. The other is to interpolate onto constant-maturity tenors. Each has flaws (nearby tenors drift through the month, and splines can jump at rolls), but they usually give consistent statistics. Swindle ch.5 (pp.93–94)

The Samuelson effect: volatility backwardation

Short-dated forwards are much more volatile than long-dated ones, and the effect is unusually strong in energy. Swindle ch.5 (p.103) Swindle shows that a single exponential can't fit WTI's volatility decay. He fits two exponentials, a fast-decaying component on top of a slowly decaying one:

\[ \sigma^2(\tau) = \sigma_1^2\left(e^{-2\beta_1\tau} + \lambda\,e^{-2\beta_2\tau}\right) \]

Swindle ch.5 (p.104) Clewlow and Strickland make the same point with gas. A single exponential fitted to the steep front end drives long-dated volatility toward zero and understates options on that part of the curve. Clewlow & Strickland §8.3 The fitted parameters are also not stable: the rate of volatility backwardation changed noticeably between 2001–04, 2005–08 and 2009–12. Swindle ch.5 (p.106)

Worked example 1 · a two-exponential volatility curveChecked by code

Take \(\sigma_1\) = 25%, \(\beta_1\) = 0.05 (slow), \(\beta_2\) = 1.5 (fast) and \(\lambda\) = 1.

1-month vol = 33.3%; 12-month = 24.4%; 24-month = 22.7%

The fast factor adds about 9 vol points at the front and almost nothing by year two. The slow factor keeps long-dated volatility well above zero. Parameters are illustrative. Swindle ch.5 (p.104)

The correlation term structure

Forward returns of the same commodity are highly correlated, but not perfectly. That is how a curve can be strongly correlated and still swing between backwardation and contango. Correlation rises as two contracts get closer in delivery, and contracts far out the curve move almost in lockstep. Swindle ch.5 (pp.91, 106–107) Seasonal commodities add structure. Gas traders believe contracts in the same season (two winters) are more correlated than contracts across seasons, and Swindle finds some evidence for this. Swindle ch.5 (p.112)

Principal components: shift, tilt, bend

PCA turns the covariance matrix of forward returns into independent factors. Each eigenvector, scaled by the square root of its eigenvalue, is a volatility function in the multi-factor model, and the eigenvalues show how much variance each factor explains. Clewlow & Strickland §8.3, §8.6.1 The usual pattern has three factors. The first is a shift (all prices move the same way, by different amounts). The second is a tilt (front and back move oppositely). The third is a bend (the middle moves against both ends). Clewlow & Strickland §8.3 For WTI, Swindle finds the first two factors explain over 97% of variance. Gas spreads variance over more factors, and seasonal patterns appear by the third. Attempts to remove seasonality before running PCA changed little. Swindle ch.5 (pp.109–110)

Worked example 2 · PCA of the two-factor curveChecked by code

Build the 24×24 instantaneous covariance matrix of monthly tenors from Example 1's two independent exponential factors, then decompose it.

variance share: factor 1 = 95.3%, factor 2 = 4.7% correlation of 1-month with 24-month returns = 0.784; 12-month with 24-month = 0.985

The first component is a shift that is largest at the front. The second is a tilt that changes sign around nine months. PCA doesn't recover the original exponential factors (those were not orthogonal across tenors). It finds the orthogonal combinations that explain the most variance. Clewlow & Strickland §8.6.1 Synthesis

Stack-and-roll hedging

Liquidity sits at the front, so long-dated exposures are often hedged with near contracts and rolled forward (stack-and-roll). How much front contract hedges one unit of a long-dated forward? The minimum-variance answer is

\[ w = \frac{F(t,T)}{F(t,S)}\,\frac{\sigma(t,T)}{\sigma(t,S)}\,\rho(t,S,T) \]

That needs exactly the volatility and correlation term structures above. Swindle ch.5 (pp.102–103)

Worked example 3 · hedging a 24-month exposure with the 2-month contractChecked by code

An airline hedge leaves you short 1 million barrels of the 24-month contract ($72). You hedge with the 2-month ($75), using Example 1's volatilities.

σ(2m) = 31.5%, σ(24m) = 22.7%, ρ = 0.819 w = (72/75) × (22.7/31.5) × 0.819 = 0.565 → buy 565 thousand bbl of the 2-month, not 960 thousand (value-matched) or 1 million

A barrel-for-barrel hedge would be heavily over-hedged against moves at the front. The residual is curve-shape risk: if the curve tilts, the hedge and the exposure diverge. Swindle ch.5 (p.102) Synthesis The best-known failure is Metallgesellschaft's 1993 programme. It hedged long-dated fixed-price sales with rolled front-month futures, and when the curve moved into contango and prices fell, the funding drain on the hedge forced its unwinding. Outside the texts

Exponential factor models in production

Swindle's Gaussian exponential models (GEMs) are sums of exponential factors with deterministic volatilities. Forward prices are jointly lognormal, and the whole curve is described by a few OU state variables, which makes path-dependent structures such as storage tractable. Two factors are typical, and three are rare in production. Swindle ch.11 (pp.244–245) He also corrects a common misconception about calibration. Desks don't refit every parameter to every price each day, because those optimisations are slow and unstable. Fast parameters (factor volatilities) are fitted daily to liquid options, and slower ones (decay rates, correlations) are adjusted occasionally as spread options or swaptions trade. Swindle ch.11 (p.246)

The Heath–Jarrow–Morton (HJM) Forward Curve Framework

Rather than modeling an unobservable spot price and inverting it, the Heath–Jarrow–Morton (HJM) / Clewlow–Strickland approach treats the entire forward curve \(F(t, T)\) as the primary traded state variable. Under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), forward prices are martingales, so the forward curve SDE is strictly drift-free: \[ \frac{dF(t,T)}{F(t,T)} = \sum_{k=1}^m \sigma_k(t, T) \, dW_k^{\mathbb{Q}}(t) \] Under the real-world physical measure \(\mathbb{P}\), a change of measure (Girsanov's theorem) adds a drift \(\sum_k \sigma_k(t, T) \lambda_k(t)\) where \(\lambda_k\) represents the market prices of commodity risk. When the volatility structure exhibits Samuelson decay \(\sigma(t, T) = \sigma_0 e^{-\alpha (T - t)}\), the cumulative variance of the forward over its life \([0, T]\) is given by the integrated volatility: \[ \bar{\sigma}^2(T) = \frac{1}{T} \int_0^T \sigma_0^2 e^{-2\alpha (T - t)} \, dt = \frac{\sigma_0^2}{2\alpha T} \left(1 - e^{-2\alpha T}\right) \] Clewlow & Strickland §8.2–8.4 Outside the texts Synthesis

Worked example 5 · HJM Samuelson integrated volatility across tenorsChecked by code

A natural gas forward curve has spot-month instantaneous volatility \(\sigma_0 = 60\%\) and mean-reversion speed \(\alpha = 1.20\). We compute the effective integrated volatility \(\bar{\sigma}(T)\) for a prompt 6-month (\(T = 0.5\) yr) contract versus a long-dated 2-year (\(T = 2.0\) yr) forward strip:

prompt 6-month contract integrated volatility (T = 0.5 yr) = 45.79% long-dated 2-year strip integrated volatility (T = 2.0 yr) = 27.27% dampening from 60% spot-month vol to 2-year integrated vol = 54.54% reduction

Mean-reversion cuts long-dated curve volatility by more than half (from 60% prompt to 27.27% at 2 years). Desks that ignore Samuelson dampening overprice long-dated options and mis-hedge calendar spreads. Clewlow & Strickland §8.2 Synthesis

From data, not formula

In practice the covariance matrix is estimated from daily returns. Clewlow & Strickland §8.6.1 Noise then spreads a little variance into extra components.

Worked example 4 · PCA on simulated returnsChecked by code

Simulate 500 trading days of returns from the same two factors, add 3% idiosyncratic noise per tenor, estimate the sample covariance and run PCA.

factor 1 = 94.7%; factors 1–2 = 98.8%; factor 3 = 0.08%

Two years of data recover the structure well, but the first-factor share moves by about half a point and the third component is just noise. With real data, a "bend" factor of a few percent may be signal or may be estimation error. Test its stability across sub-periods before building a hedge around it. Synthesis

ALM analogy

This is yield-curve risk management. Shift, tilt and bend are the level, slope and curvature factors of the term structure of rates. Stack-and-roll is hedging long-dated liabilities with short futures and rolling, which is the duration-matching problem with basis and curve risk. A GEM is a multi-factor Hull–White model for commodities. One difference matters: commodity volatility decays with tenor much faster than rate volatility, so the front of the curve dominates. Synthesis

Dated: data windows

Clewlow and Strickland's PCA uses NYMEX data from 1998–2000, and Swindle's uses data to 2012. Volatility backwardation has varied a lot since (2014–16 oversupply, 2020 negative prices, the 2022 energy crisis), so reestimate on recent windows and compare epochs. TTF and JKM now matter as much as Henry Hub for global gas correlation structures. Outside the texts

Going deeper: what PCA can't see

PCA treats the covariance as fixed and the returns as normal. Two energy features break that. First, volatility depends on calendar month as well as tenor (seasonality in \(T\) and in \(t\)). Gas volatility peaks for late-winter and hurricane-season contracts, and Swindle shows that simple seasonal normalisation doesn't remove the effect. Swindle ch.5 (pp.110–112) Second, correlations are higher in some states than others: a supply shock moves the whole curve, while a storage glut decorrelates the front. A hedge sized on average correlation can fail exactly when it is needed. Stress the hedge ratio with correlations from stressed periods. Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • Forward-curve models fit today's curve and specify volatility functions. One exponential factor is the Schwartz spot model in disguise.
  • Volatility backwardation needs at least two exponentials, and correlation falls as delivery dates diverge.
  • PCA gives shift, tilt and bend. For crude, two factors explain over 95% of variance, while gas needs more.
  • Minimum-variance stack hedges use volatility ratios and correlation, not barrel-for-barrel. The residual is curve-shape risk.
