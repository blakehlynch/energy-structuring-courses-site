Volatility, skew and correlation

Learning objectives

  • Price futures options with Black-76 and term-averaged volatility.
  • Value an option expiring before the listed expiry using a calibrated local-volatility curve.
  • Quote a costless collar under skew.
  • Value a spread option with Margrabe and back out implied correlation.

Prerequisites

  • Modules 3–4; Black–Scholes basics.

Black-76 and integrated variance

The workhorse for futures options is Black's (1976) formula. The forward replaces spot and there is no carry term. Clewlow & Strickland §6.2

\[ c = e^{-rT}\left[F N(d_1) - K N(d_2)\right],\qquad d_{1,2} = \frac{\ln(F/K) \pm \tfrac12 \bar\sigma^2 T}{\bar\sigma\sqrt{T}} \]

The key extension is that \(\bar\sigma^2 T\) is the integrated variance of the forward over the option's life. If local volatility varies deterministically with time, use its average variance. Swindle ch.7 (pp.143–144) Clewlow & Strickland §6.2 This one idea handles the Samuelson effect, seasonal volatility and the Schwartz model inside the familiar formula.

Options that don't expire on the listed date

Energy options have a structural limitation. Listed options expire just before their underlying future, so the market only tells you the volatility of contract \(T\) over the window from now to just before \(T\). Swindle contrasts this one-dimensional grid with the two-dimensional expiry × tenor grid of interest-rate swaptions. Swindle ch.7 (p.139) Desks are nevertheless asked for options that expire early: price holds on an acquisition hedge, options on index products, or a client wanting optionality only until a regulatory decision. Swindle ch.7 (pp.139–140)

The approach is to calibrate a local-volatility curve so that it reprices the listed option, then integrate it over the shorter window. With Module 4's two-exponential form, the variance from 0 to \(\tau\) for delivery \(T\) is

\[ \bar\sigma_\tau^2 = \frac{\sigma_1^2}{\tau}\left[\Phi(T,\tau\,|\,\beta_1) + \lambda\,\Phi(T,\tau\,|\,\beta_2)\right],\qquad \Phi(T,\tau\,|\,\beta) = \frac{e^{-2\beta(T-\tau)} - e^{-2\beta T}}{2\beta} \]

Set \(\sigma_1\) so that \(\tau = T^{(e)}\) returns the listed implied volatility, then evaluate at the early expiry. Swindle ch.7 (p.144) Because volatility rises as a contract approaches delivery, an option that stops early sees the quiet part of the contract's life. Its term volatility is lower than the listed implied volatility. Swindle ch.7 (pp.143–145)

Worked example 1 · an early-expiry straddleChecked by code

A December-delivery crude contract (2.05 years out) trades at $70. Its listed option (expiry 2.0 years) is at 32%. A client wants an ATM straddle on this contract expiring in 9 months. Take \(\beta_1\) = 0.05, \(\beta_2\) = 1.0, \(\lambda\) = 0.8 and r = 4%.

calibrated σ₁ = 30.8% (reprices 32% at 2.0 years) term vol to 0.75 years = 28.84% (ratio 0.901) straddle at 32%: $14.97; at 28.84%: $13.50 → difference $−1.47/bbl, or $147k on 100,000 bbl

Selling at the listed volatility overcharges the client, and a competitor who models backwardation will win the trade. Swindle warns, though, that this first cut uses only one contract's history. The calibration should be checked against the volatility of the contracts that will actually be near delivery during the option's life. Swindle ch.7 (pp.143–145)

Skew and the costless collar

Energy returns are far from normal, and implied volatility surfaces show strong skew. Swindle ch.8 (p.160) Natural gas traditionally had call skew, especially for winter months, when cold weather can push prices and volatility up together. Swindle shows put skew appearing in summer contracts around 2010 as shale supply changed the market. Swindle ch.8 (pp.161–163) Skew is also seasonal, so a strip of options on a seasonal curve sits at different moneyness each month. Swindle ch.8 (p.164)

The most common skew-dependent structure is the collar (fence): the client buys a put and sells a call, or the reverse, usually for zero premium. The dealer's margin sits in the strikes. Swindle ch.8 (pp.160–161) With call skew, the calls a producer sells are worth more per unit of distance from the money than the puts they buy. A skew-aware dealer can therefore give a put strike closer to the forward than a flat-volatility model would allow. Swindle ch.8 (p.165)

Worked example 2 · quoting a costless collar under call skewChecked by code

A producer sells a 1-year gas call struck at $4.25 (forward $3.50) and wants the put strike that makes the collar costless. ATM volatility is 45%. We use an illustrative smile \(\sigma(k) = 0.45 + 0.15k + 0.30k^2\) with \(k=\ln(K/F)\), and r = 4%.

call vol = 49.0%, call value = $0.4154/MMBtu costless put strike with skew = $3.179 (put vol 43.8%); with flat 45% = $3.033

Skew moves the floor up by about 15 cents. Before quoting, deduct hedging costs (Swindle uses a bid–offer that widens away from the money), which pushes the put strike back down. Swindle ch.8 (pp.163–165) Swindle also warns about the related knockout swap: a swap that disappears below a trigger price, giving the producer an above-market price that vanishes exactly in the price collapse it was meant to protect against. Swindle ch.8 (p.166)

Spread options and correlation

Most natural optionality in energy is a spread option, and infrastructure is where it comes from. A power plant is a call on power minus heat rate × fuel. A pipeline is a call on one location's price minus another's. Storage is a strip of calendar spread options. Swindle ch.9 (p.187) Tolling deals give daily exercise of spark spreads. Asset hedges are usually paid as monthly capacity payments to limit credit exposure to the (often unrated) asset owner. Swindle ch.9 (pp.189–190) Margrabe's formula values the zero-strike case under lognormal prices:

\[ V = e^{-rT}\left[F_1 N(d_1) - H F_2 N(d_2)\right],\quad d_{1,2} = \frac{\ln\!\big(F_1/(HF_2)\big) \pm \tfrac12\hat\sigma^2}{\hat\sigma},\quad \hat\sigma^2 = T\left(\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2-2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2\right) \]

Swindle ch.9 (pp.190–191) The spread volatility falls as correlation rises, so a spread option is short correlation. Correlation markets are thin, and implied correlation is mostly a quoting convention for comparing models against the Margrabe benchmark. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.6 (pp.236–237)

Worked example 3 · an at-the-money spark spread optionChecked by code

Six-month power is $30/MWh and gas $4/MMBtu, with a 7.5 heat rate, so the option is at the money. Volatilities are 50% for power and 40% for gas, and r = 4%.

ρ = 0.80: spread vol = 30.0%, value = $2.48/MWh ρ = 0.50: $3.78; ρ = 0.95: $1.44 a broker quote of $2.60 implies ρ = 0.778

Across the plausible range of correlations, value changes by a factor of more than two. For a 500 MW plant over 4,000 hours, each 10 cents/MWh is $200,000. Synthesis

Correlation has structure too

Eydeland and Wolyniec make three practical points. First, with non-lognormal prices, correlation measured on historical data needn't match the correlation that reprices spread options. Implied correlation varies with moneyness (the implied heat rate), and this correlation skew exists even when realised correlation shows none. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.6 (pp.244–245) Second, correlation has a term structure, partly induced by volatilities that rise toward expiry. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.6 (p.243) Third, historical correlation can be a poor guide to the future. Adding efficient generation without load growth tends to raise power–gas correlation and lower power volatility, which Module 6 reproduces. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.6 (pp.237, 244) Pooling months with different volatility ratios to estimate correlation also biases it downward when true correlation is high. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.6 (p.241)

ALM analogy

A non-standard-expiry option is a mid-curve swaption: you need the volatility of a forward over a window the market doesn't quote directly, and you get it by integrating a calibrated local volatility. Skew is the rates smile. A spread option is a curve-spread option (for example 2s10s): short correlation, priced with a spread volatility that collapses when the legs move together. As with rates, implied correlation is a convention more than a traded price. Synthesis

Dated: skew regimes and products

Swindle's skew examples are from 2008–2010. Since then gas skew has swung with LNG export growth and the 2021–22 crises, and European power and gas options show extreme call skew in stress. Weekly and daily options on major futures, together with OTC look-alikes, give more expiry points than older texts assume. Check current CME and ICE option expiries and exchange-published volatility surfaces. Outside the texts

Going deeper: what a Margrabe value leaves out

Margrabe assumes lognormal legs, a single exercise, zero strike and a fixed correlation. A real toll has daily exercise, operating constraints (start costs, minimum run, ramp limits), a variable O&M strike and power prices with spikes. The spikes produce the correlation skew that makes deep out-of-the-money heat-rate options worth more than Margrabe says. Desks often keep Margrabe as the quoting language, express everything else as an implied-correlation or implied-volatility adjustment, and run the full valuation in a simulation such as Module 6's hybrid model. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.6 (pp.236–237, 243) Synthesis Course 7 values the asset itself.

Key takeaways

  • Black-76 with integrated variance handles time-varying, seasonal and mean-reverting volatility.
  • Early-expiry options need a calibrated local-volatility curve. With volatility backwardation they are worth less than the listed volatility implies.
  • Skew decides costless-collar strikes. Knockout swaps embed tail risk the client may not see.
  • Spread options are short correlation. Implied correlation has a skew and a term structure, and stack changes can move it far from history.
