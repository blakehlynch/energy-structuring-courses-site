Curve building and seasonality

Core≈ 55 min8 quiz questions5 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish a forward curve from a price forecast.
  • Shape a monthly curve that reprices seasonal strip quotes exactly.
  • Derive off-peak from baseload and peak quotes.
  • Compare the arbitrage (heat-rate), econometric and spot-model approaches for power curves.

Prerequisites

  • Module 1.

A forward curve is not a forecast

A forward curve lists prices you can lock in today for delivery in each future period. A forecast lists what someone thinks spot will be. Clewlow and Strickland note that early power-market participants, and even some data vendors, confused the two. Forecasts differ between firms, while forwards are prices the market will actually trade. Clewlow & Strickland §4.1 Wherever a market quote exists, the curve should go through it. Modelling only fills the gaps between and beyond liquid quotes. Clewlow & Strickland §4.3

What is actually quoted

Liquidity is uneven along the curve. The front months of benchmark futures trade deeply. Further out, trading concentrates in strips (quarters, seasons, calendar years) and in benchmark months. Swindle ch.2 (p.30) A desk therefore builds its monthly curve from a mix of single months at the front and block quotes further out. A seasonal shape spreads each block across its months so that the block still reprices exactly. Synthesis

The rule that keeps the curve arbitrage-free: the volume-weighted average of the months in a block must equal the block's quote. In gas the weights are days, and in power they are hours in the relevant bucket. Otherwise you could buy the strip and sell the months, or the reverse, for a riskless profit. Outside the texts

\[ \frac{\sum_{m\in B} w_m F_m}{\sum_{m\in B} w_m} = F_B \]
Worked example 1 · shaping gas to season stripsChecked by code

Winter (Nov–Mar) trades at $4.00/MMBtu and summer (Apr–Oct) at $3.30. We assume a smooth winter-peaked shape, a cosine with ±12% amplitude centred on January, and scale it within each season so that the day-weighted average reprices the strip.

January $4.112, March $3.892, April $3.526, July $3.103 check: day-weighted winter average = $4.000 ✓; implied calendar strip = $3.590

Note the step from March ($3.89) to April ($3.53). The season boundary creates a jump that the shape alone doesn't produce. That is realistic for gas, since the injection season starts in April, but a desk should still check that the step matches traded month spreads. Synthesis

Seasonal curve builder

Season strip quotes plus a shape assumption → monthly curve

Set the amplitude to zero and the curve becomes two flat blocks. Any shape you choose still reprices both strips; it only redistributes value within each season.

Seasonality by commodity

Crude curves are usually smooth: mostly backwardated, occasionally in contango, sometimes switching quickly. Clewlow & Strickland §4.2.3 Gas curves carry a strong annual cycle with winter peaks. Clewlow and Strickland observe that the summer–winter spread looks like a storage arbitrage. They give reasons it persisted in the late 1990s: utilities could pass costs through, few players had both storage and trading capability, and relative-value trading was new. They expected it to shrink as markets matured. Clewlow & Strickland §4.2.3 Power is the most complex case. It has two seasonal peaks in many markets (heating and cooling) on top of weekly and intraday patterns, because the product can't be stored and the market remains regional. Clewlow & Strickland §4.3

Building power curves

Clewlow and Strickland describe three approaches for power, where quotes are sparse. Clewlow & Strickland §4.3

  • Arbitrage (heat-rate) approach. Electricity can't be stored, but its fuel can. Power is priced as heat rate × fuel price, plus adders for fixed costs, transmission and tolling. The power curve inherits the fuel curve's shape. The weak point is the heat rate itself, especially over long tenors. Clewlow & Strickland §4.3.1
  • Econometric or simulation approach. Production-cost and dispatch models produce expected spot prices from loads, fuel costs and transmission. The output is a forecast, not a tradeable forward. Clewlow & Strickland §4.3.2
  • Spot-price modelling approach. Specify stochastic factors (spot, long-term level, convenience yield) and derive forward prices analytically, as in fixed-income term-structure models. This is the route Modules 3–4 follow. Clewlow & Strickland §4.3.3
Worked example 2 · a heat-rate power curveChecked by code

Using the gas curve from Example 1, a 7.0 MMBtu/MWh market heat rate and a $3/MWh adder:

January power = 7.0 × 4.112 + 3 = $31.78/MWh; July power = 7.0 × 3.103 + 3 = $24.72/MWh

In reality July power in a summer-peaking market would be the highest month, because air conditioning drives demand even when gas is cheap. A constant heat rate misses that. The market heat rate itself is seasonal, and Module 6 builds it from the stack. Clewlow & Strickland §4.3.1 Synthesis

Peak, off-peak and shaping within the month

Power trades in delivery buckets. Typical US blocks are 5×16 peak (weekdays, 16 hours) and the complementary off-peak hours, and European markets quote base and peak. Swindle uses a 5×16 bucket in his tolling definitions. Swindle ch.9 (p.189) Because baseload is the hour-weighted mix of the two, quoting any two buckets fixes the third. Synthesis

Worked example 3 · off-peak from base and peakChecked by code

Calendar baseload trades at $50/MWh and calendar peak at $62. Use 4,160 peak hours and 4,600 off-peak hours.

off-peak = (50 × 8,760 − 62 × 4,160) / 4,600 = $39.15/MWh

When a client quote needs a bucket that doesn't trade, derive it from those that do. Never mark it independently, or the curve will be internally arbitrageable. Synthesis

Within a month, a daily curve can be held flat at the monthly price (a step function) or interpolated. Swindle notes that the choice matters most for storage valuation, where day-to-day spreads are the payoff. Swindle ch.11 (p.248)

ALM analogy

Building a curve from strips is like bootstrapping a swap curve from deposits, futures and swaps. You fit liquid instruments exactly and use an interpolation rule (here, a seasonal shape) for the rest. The shape is a modelling choice like the interpolation method on a discount curve. It doesn't change the price of quoted instruments, but it moves the value of anything in between, so it belongs in model-risk reserves. Synthesis

Dated: seasonality has changed

Clewlow and Strickland's gas curves date from 2000, and they expected seasonality to fade. Since then shale supply, LNG exports and a larger storage fleet have changed US seasonal spreads. European gas is now tied to global LNG, which made the 2022 curve extreme. In power, high solar penetration has inverted the intraday shape in many markets, with midday prices now often the lowest of the day. Outside the texts Check current exchange settlements (CME, ICE, EEX) for live shapes.

Going deeper: the shape is a position

Suppose you sell a client a fixed price for January only and hedge with the winter strip. You are then long February, March, November and December against short January, and your P&L depends on the January premium you assumed in the shape. If the market later trades January at a bigger premium, your mark moves even though the strip didn't. Shape risk is therefore real risk. It needs limits, and ideally hedges in the individual months once they become liquid. This is the curve version of the basis risk from Course 3. Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • Forwards are tradeable prices. Forecasts are opinions, and the curve should pass through every liquid quote.
  • Shapes distribute strip value across months, and weighted averages must reprice each strip.
  • Power curves come from fuel × heat rate, from simulation models, or from stochastic spot models. Only the first and third give tradeable relationships.
  • Any two of base, peak and off-peak fix the third. Shape assumptions are positions.
