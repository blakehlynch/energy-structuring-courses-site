Carry and convenience yield

Core≈ 55 min8 quiz questions7 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Price a forward by full cost of carry and back out the implied convenience yield.
  • Explain why consumption commodities can be backwardated without arbitrage.
  • Value a cash-and-carry or storage trade from forward prices.
  • Explain roll yield and why index returns differ from the nearby price.

Prerequisites

  • Course 1 Module 3 (contango and backwardation) and continuous compounding.

Cost of carry: where forward prices come from

For a financial asset, the forward price is pinned down by arbitrage rather than forecasts. You can replicate a forward sale by borrowing, buying the asset today and holding it to delivery. So the forward must equal spot grown at the funding rate, or someone earns a riskless profit. Clewlow & Strickland §4.2.1 Swindle ch.2 (p.27) Clewlow and Strickland stress the corollary: a forward curve is built from today's tradeable prices, and a schedule of what people expect spot to be later is a different object. Clewlow & Strickland §4.1

A physical commodity adds a storage cost \(w\) (as a yield) and, for consumption goods, a benefit of holding inventory that a forward holder doesn't get, the convenience yield \(\delta\). The full carry relationship is

\[ F(t,T) = S(t)\,e^{(r + w - \delta)(T-t)} \]

Depending on whether \(w\) or \(\delta\) dominates, the curve can slope up (contango) or down (backwardation). Clewlow & Strickland §4.2.2 Swindle makes the important practical point that markets only reveal the net of storage and convenience, \(q - \eta\) in his notation (where we write \(w\) for storage yield and \(\delta\) for convenience yield; in Courses 6–8 we also note both \(\alpha\) and \(\kappa\) for mean-reversion speed). You can observe \(r\) and \(F\), but not the two pieces separately. Swindle ch.2 (p.27)

Why backwardation is not an arbitrage

The cash-and-carry trade (buy spot, store, sell forward) is always available, so the forward can't sit far above full carry. The reverse trade requires shorting the physical commodity, which means borrowing it from someone who holds it. When inventory is scarce, holders won't lend it cheaply, so nothing stops the forward from sitting below spot. Clewlow & Strickland §4.2.2 Swindle draws the parallel with a bond "going special" in repo: lending the commodity against cash earns a negative implied yield when the curve is backwardated. Swindle ch.2 (pp.26–27) The carry formula is therefore an upper bound for consumption commodities. It becomes an equality only once you define \(\delta\) to make it one.

Worked example 1 · backing out the convenience yieldChecked by code

Crude is $70 spot. Funding is 5% and storage costs 3% a year (continuous). With no convenience yield the one-year forward would be:

F = 70 × e^{(0.05 + 0.03) × 1} = $75.83 the market one-year forward is $66 → implied δ = 0.05 + 0.03 − ln(66/70) = 13.88% observed forward yield ln(66/70) = −5.88% a year

Holders of physical crude are effectively valuing immediate availability at almost 14% a year. That is a large number, but entirely normal in a tight market. Numbers are illustrative. Clewlow & Strickland §4.2.2

Trading the carry: cash-and-carry and storage

If the forward ever exceeds full carry, the cash-and-carry trade locks in the difference. If it is below, someone with inventory sells spot and buys forward. Clewlow & Strickland §4.2.1 In gas, the same logic drives storage. A seasonal spread between summer and winter contracts is an arbitrage only for whoever controls storage capacity, and its owners will charge for that capacity. Clewlow & Strickland §4.2.3 Swindle goes further. Storage cost is not an independent input, because the price of leasing storage rises with the spread. The carry formula is therefore circular in practice. Swindle ch.2 (p.28)

Worked example 2 · a cash-and-carry checkChecked by code

Spot $70, one-year forward $77, funding 5%, tank storage $2.40/bbl a year.

financed purchase cost at year end: 70 × e^{0.05} = $73.59 profit = 77 − 73.59 − 2.40 = $1.01/bbl locked in

Before trading, check the real costs a desk would face: insurance, losses in tank, margin funding on the short futures, and whether the tank is truly available. Synthesis

Worked example 3 · a gas storage spreadChecked by code

Inject in summer at $3.20/MMBtu and sell the January contract at $4.10, six months apart. Injection, withdrawal, fuel and lease cost $0.45 together, and funding is 5%.

financing = 3.20 × (e^{0.05 × 0.5} − 1) = $0.081 margin = 4.10 − 3.20 − 0.081 − 0.45 = $0.369/MMBtu

This is the intrinsic value of storage: lock the spread once. Course 7 adds the extrinsic value of re-optimising as the spread moves. Synthesis

Inventory and the forward curve

The theory of storage links the slope of the curve to inventory. When stocks are low, short-dated prices rise relative to long-dated ones, and forward yields go negative. When stocks are high, the curve slopes up to pay for storing the surplus. Swindle ch.2 (pp.28–29) Swindle's example is late 2008. Crude went into very steep contango, and when onshore tanks filled, traders stored oil on chartered tankers. The cost of the marginal tanker then set the carry. Swindle ch.2 (p.30) Swindle also notes the relationship runs both ways: inventory responds to the curve and the curve to inventory. Swindle ch.2 (p.31)

ALM analogy

Carry is the commodity version of a repo curve. The funding rate is your cost of financing the asset, storage is a holding cost like custody, and convenience yield plays the part of a security trading special: owning the physical bond earns something that a forward on it doesn't. As in a repo book, only the net yield is observable, and specialness spikes exactly when the asset is scarce. Synthesis

Roll yield: why owning futures is not owning spot

A commodity index or ETF holds the nearby future and rolls into the next contract before expiry, keeping dollar value constant at each roll. Its value therefore tracks contract returns, not the nearby price series. Swindle ch.5 (pp.96–97) In contango, each roll sells the cheaper expiring contract and buys a dearer one, so the position bleeds even if the front price is flat. Swindle cites 2009, when the WTI front month rose sharply but a rolled long position lost money. Swindle ch.5 (p.97) Analysts make the matching error of computing returns directly from a nearby price series, which creates spurious returns on roll dates. Returns should be computed contract by contract and then joined. Swindle ch.5 (p.93)

Worked example 4 · roll decay in steady contangoChecked by code

Every month the front contract settles at $70 and the second month trades at $71.40, so the curve never moves.

forward yield between months 1 and 2 = ln(71.40/70) × 12 = 23.8% a year after 12 rolls the index is (70/71.40)^12 = 78.8% of its start → a loss of 21.2%

The front price never moved, yet the investor lost a fifth of their money. The roll paid storage owners for carrying the surplus. Swindle ch.5 (p.98)

Two meanings of backwardation

Swindle separates two ideas that share a name. The first is the observable slope of the curve (forward yields). The second is "normal backwardation", meaning forwards sit below the expected future spot price because producers hedge more than consumers and pay speculators a risk premium. Swindle ch.5 (pp.94–95) The second idea can't be observed directly, and the evidence on hedging pressure is mixed. Swindle reports, though, that WTI risk premia tended to be positive when the curve was backwardated. Swindle ch.5 (pp.95–98) For valuation, the risk-neutral forward is what you hedge against. A view on the risk premium is a separate, speculative position. Synthesis

Dated: funding benchmarks and storage episodes

Swindle uses LIBOR as the funding rate. USD LIBOR ceased in 2023, and desks now fund off SOFR or their own internal transfer-pricing curve. Outside the texts The 2008–09 floating-storage episode was repeated in 2020, when WTI May futures settled below zero because landlocked storage at Cushing ran out. That is a carry limit the older texts don't contemplate. Outside the texts Check current EIA and IEA inventory data and exchange notices on negative-price handling.

Going deeper: convenience yield as an option

Why should holding inventory be worth more when stocks are low? One way to see it: a barrel in tank is a call on tomorrow's spot price with no expiry. You can sell it into a squeeze or keep it. Its time value rises when spot volatility is high and inventory is scarce, which is when the curve backwardates. This reading connects Swindle's inventory–yield link with the stochastic convenience yield of Module 3's two-factor model, and with storage valuation in Course 7. Synthesis Swindle notes that modelling this feedback between curve dynamics and inventory is still largely unresolved. Swindle ch.2 (p.31)

Key takeaways

  • Full carry: \(F = S e^{(r+w-\delta)T}\). The forward is set by tradeable prices, not forecasts.
  • For consumption commodities carry is only an upper bound, because you can't borrow scarce inventory to short it. Convenience yield closes the gap.
  • Only the net of storage and convenience is observable. Storage costs themselves respond to the spread.
  • Rolled long positions earn the forward yield, not the spot move. Contango is a cost to longs and income to storage owners.
