Hybrid power price models

Core≈ 60 min8 quiz questions5 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Explain why reduced-form price processes struggle in power.
  • Build a stack transformation from load and fuel to price.
  • Show why expected price exceeds the price at expected drivers.
  • Explain how capacity additions change power–gas correlation and volatility.

Prerequisites

  • Modules 3–5; Course 5 Module 2 (merit order).

Why power breaks reduced-form models

Eydeland and Wolyniec list what a usable power price process needs. It must show spikes, mean reversion, fat tails and seasonality. It must also match the volatility surface, the correlations between forward contracts, and the cross-commodity correlation with gas. Above all, its parameters must be stable, so that one more month of data doesn't overturn them. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.7 (p.251) These demands conflict. Flexibility needs many parameters, but power markets have only a few seasons of reliable price history, so a heavily parameterised price process is estimated on noise. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.7 (pp.251–252) Clewlow and Strickland's mean-reverting jump diffusion (Module 3) can resemble pool prices, but it learns from prices alone. Clewlow & Strickland §2.8

Production-cost and equilibrium models

The utility industry has long used production-cost models. These optimise dispatch to meet load at least cost, subject to transmission, environmental and operating constraints, and require detailed data on plants, load and the network. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.7 (p.252) Their limits for trading are serious. The map from drivers to price is highly non-linear, so the expected price needs many simulated scenarios, each a full re-optimisation, and that is too slow beyond a few days. They also aren't designed to reproduce volatility or higher moments. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.7 (p.253) Equilibrium models add strategic behaviour (for example Nash bidding) or forward-market equilibrium. They are useful for qualitative analysis but weak at reproducing price dynamics quantitatively. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.7 (p.253)

The hybrid idea

A hybrid model keeps the market-consistency of financial models and borrows the information of fundamental ones. Model the drivers stochastically (load, fuel prices, outages, emissions, weather), and get power from a deterministic stack transformation:

\[ p_T = s\left(D_T,\, g_T,\, \ldots;\ \text{stack}_T\right) + \varepsilon_T \]

Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.7 (pp.254–255) The motivation has three parts. The dynamics come out naturally. Forward-looking non-price information, such as announced plant additions, enters explicitly. And the data set grows from a short history of power prices to long histories of load, temperature and fuel. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.7 (p.254) The approach follows a statistical tradition: model a complex variable as a non-linear transformation of simpler variables with well-behaved distributions. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.7 (p.255)

Two variants exist. Reduced-form hybrids calibrate the transformation to historical prices, for example by making jump intensity depend on reserve margin or load. Fundamental hybrids derive the transformation from the market's actual stack and only test it on history. Eydeland and Wolyniec favour the second as a more efficient use of scarce price data. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.7 (pp.258–259)

Worked example 1 · a stylised stackChecked by code

Capacity is 30,000 MW. The marginal heat rate rises from 6.5 to 10.5 MMBtu/MWh as utilisation goes from 0 to 100%, plus a $2 adder. Above 85% utilisation a scarcity adder rises steeply (cubic) toward a $1,000 cap. Gas is $3.50.

load 20,000 MW → $34.08; 24,000 → $35.95; 27,000 → $74.39; 28,500 → $334.35/MWh at 27,000 MW with gas at $5.00 → $89.54: gas moves only the fuel part of the price

The curve is a hockey stick: flat and gas-driven over most of the range, explosive near capacity. A real stack is a step function built from individual units (Course 5, Module 2). This smooth form is illustrative. Synthesis

Why the expected price is not the price at expected load

Because the stack is convex, the average price over uncertain load and gas is above the price at average load and gas. That is why Eydeland and Wolyniec insist on simulating the drivers and pushing every scenario through the transformation. Plugging expected inputs into a dispatch model understates the forward and misses the tails entirely. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.7 (p.253)

Worked example 2 · simulating the terminal distributionChecked by code

Using the Example 1 stack for one summer period, load is 24,000 MW ± 8% (normal). Gas is lognormal around $3.50 with 45% annual volatility over 3 months, and load–gas correlation is 0.3. We draw 4,000 scenarios.

price at expected drivers = $35.95; median $35.50; mean $47.80; 99th percentile $339 scenarios above 85% utilisation: 21.0%; excess kurtosis 97; power–gas correlation 0.25; implied heat rate (mean price / gas) 13.66

The mean is a third above the price at expected drivers, and nearly all the difference comes from a few tight scenarios. Spikes, fat tails and seasonality emerge from load and the stack without being imposed on the price process. Power–gas correlation is low because in tight hours price depends on load, not gas. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.7 (pp.251, 254) Synthesis

New capacity changes the statistics

A model fitted to past prices can't anticipate a change in the stack, but a hybrid can. Eydeland and Wolyniec point out that adding efficient generation without matching load growth should raise power–gas correlation and lower power volatility. Gas units set the price more often and scarcity hours become rare, so historical estimates understate future correlation. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.6 (pp.237, 244)

Worked example 3 · adding 3,000 MWChecked by code

Same drivers as Example 2, but capacity rises to 33,000 MW.

mean price $35.07 (was $47.80); 99th percentile $58.92 (was $339) power–gas correlation 0.87 (was 0.25)

For a tolling deal or a spark-spread option, this shift is first order. Lower volatility and higher correlation both reduce the value of the spread option (Module 5). A plant valued on historical statistics would be overvalued. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.6 (p.244) Synthesis

Matching market quotes

Some drivers can't be hedged (load, outages), and the stack map is imperfect, so a hybrid's raw distribution won't reprice traded power forwards and options. Eydeland and Wolyniec therefore adjust the simulated price, \(p^*_T = u(p_T, \ldots)\), so that it reproduces the balance-of-month and forward prices and the monthly and daily option prices exactly. Valuation and hedging then stay consistent with liquid tradables. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.7 (pp.256–257) The price of this parsimony is that you can't tune means, volatilities, correlations and kurtosis independently. The model is a model of the relationship between them. If it fails validation against history, you change the model rather than calibrate it. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.7 (pp.257–258)

Worked example 4 · the simplest quote matchChecked by code

Suppose the market forward for the period in Example 2 is $55, against the model's mean of $47.80. A multiplicative adjustment makes the simulated distribution reprice it:

k = 55 / 47.80 = 1.1506 → every scenario price × 1.1506

This matches only the forward. Matching an option strip as well needs a richer adjustment, for example one that reshapes the upper tail. A 15% gap is also diagnostic: the market is pricing more scarcity risk than the model sees, or risk premium. Investigate before you scale it away. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.7 (pp.256–257) Synthesis

ALM analogy

A hybrid power model is an economic scenario generator built on structural drivers: macro factors (load, gas) mapped through a deterministic transformation (the stack), as you might map rate and equity scenarios through a liability model. The quote-matching step is market-consistent calibration, like adjusting real-world ESG scenarios so that a risk-neutral version reprices swaptions. And just as a new product changes a lapse model, a change in the stack changes every statistic downstream. Synthesis

Dated: today's stacks

Eydeland and Wolyniec wrote about early-2000s US markets with gas on the margin and few renewables. Today's stacks have large zero-marginal-cost wind and solar, batteries, and carbon prices in many markets. Net load (load minus renewables) is now the key driver, weather drives supply as well as demand, and the stack has a near-zero or negative segment as well as a scarcity segment. Fundamental and hybrid models remain the industry standard (commercial vendors include Aurora, PLEXOS and others), and most desks combine them with reduced-form models for short-tenor risk. Outside the texts Check the ISO capacity and interconnection queues, and the market's current scarcity-pricing rules, when building the stack.

Going deeper: conditional structure

A subtle point in Eydeland and Wolyniec is that a hybrid fixes how forward prices, forward volatility and daily volatility evolve conditionally, because they inherit the conditional behaviour of the drivers. Temperature carries little information until about three months before delivery, and outages until shortly before. A hybrid therefore implies that much of the volatility of a power forward arrives late, in the cash period. This matches the high daily volatility observed relative to forward volatility, and it changes how you hedge: options on daily prices and options on the monthly forward hedge different risks. Eydeland & Wolyniec ch.7 (p.257) Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • Pure price processes need too many parameters for the little power price history that exists.
  • Hybrid models simulate drivers (load, fuel, outages) and map them through the stack. Spikes, fat tails and correlations emerge on their own.
  • Convexity means expected price exceeds the price at expected drivers, so simulate rather than plug in averages.
  • Adjust model outputs to reprice traded quotes. Use the stack to anticipate how capacity changes move volatility and correlation.
