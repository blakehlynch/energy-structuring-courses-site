Prerequisites

  • Module 3.

Entry and exit with sunk costs

Dixit and Pindyck extend the timing model to a firm that can both enter and leave. An idle firm can pay \(I\) to start a project earning \(P - C\) a year, where the output price \(P\) follows geometric Brownian motion. An active firm can pay \(E\) to exit and return to idle, keeping the right to re-enter. The values of the idle and active firm are linked: each holds an option on becoming the other, and they must be solved together. Dixit & Pindyck ch.7 §1 The solution is two thresholds. An idle firm enters when \(P\) rises to \(P_H\), and an active firm exits when \(P\) falls to \(P_L\). Between them, each stays as it is. Dixit & Pindyck ch.7 §1

The classical Marshallian rules would enter when price exceeds long-run average cost, \(W_H = C + rI\), and exit when it falls below average variable cost net of exit costs, \(W_L = C - rE\). Uncertainty widens the band on both sides: \(P_H > W_H\) and \(P_L < W_L\). Dixit & Pindyck ch.7 §1 This band of inaction is hysteresis. A temporary price spike that would have justified entry under certainty doesn't trigger it, and a firm keeps a loss-making operation open because closing would throw away the option to benefit from a recovery. Dixit & Pindyck ch.7 §1

A plant earns P − C per unit-year with C = $40, entry costs I = $300 and exit E = $20. r = δ = 5% and σ = 25%. We solve the four value-matching and smooth-pasting conditions numerically.

Marshallian: enter at W_H = 40 + 0.05 × 300 = $55.00, exit at W_L = 40 − 0.05 × 20 = $39.00 with uncertainty: enter at P_H = $102.01, exit at P_L = $22.72 at σ = 10%: P_H = $73.30, P_L = $29.62

At 25% volatility, an idle firm waits for nearly twice the full-cost price before entering, and an active firm tolerates prices 43% below its variable cost before leaving. That is the "irrational" persistence observers see in commodity industries. Dixit & Pindyck ch.7 §1

Entry and exit band

Hysteresis widens with volatility and sunk costs

Variable cost $40, r = δ = 5%. A negative exit cost means part of the investment is recovered on exit. The band between the two thresholds is where firms do nothing.

The copper example and energy parallels

Dixit and Pindyck use copper to illustrate. In the mid-1980s, with real copper prices at their lowest since the Depression, producers kept unprofitable mines and smelters running. When prices were high, they didn't build new mines or reopen closed ones. That reluctance to close also pushed prices lower than they would otherwise have gone. Dixit & Pindyck ch.7 §1 Energy industries show the same pattern. Refineries run at negative margins for long stretches rather than close, old gas plants stay in the market through years of thin spark spreads, and new capacity is not built until prices look high. Synthesis

Mothballing, reactivation and scrapping

Between operating and abandoning there is often a middle option. Lay up (mothball) the asset: pay maintenance to keep it ready, and reactivate later for much less than a new build. Ships are laid up, and mines are kept from flooding. Dixit and Pindyck model three states (operating, mothballed, scrapped) with optimal switches between them. Dixit & Pindyck ch.7 §2 They note that capital "rusts" when idle, so restarting is costly but cheaper than building new, and that maintenance is the price of avoiding that rust. Dixit & Pindyck ch.7 Copeland and Antikarov value such switching options on lattices, with a state variable for the operating mode. Copeland & Antikarov ch.6

Same plant, now active with price at $38 (below the $40 cost), over a 40-year lattice. Mothballing costs $5, upkeep $4 a year and reactivation $15. Scrapping costs $20 from either state.

value without a mothball option: $135.25; with it: $151.09 → the lay-up option is worth $15.84

The plant is worth a lot despite operating at a loss today: that is the option on recovery. The ability to suspend cheaply adds roughly 12% more by limiting losses in deep downturns without giving up the upside. Dixit & Pindyck ch.7 §2 Synthesis

Using this on the desk

  • Asset valuation. An asset's value includes its options to suspend, mothball and restart. A buyer who values only the expected cash flow of continuous operation undervalues flexible assets and overvalues rigid ones. Synthesis
  • Tolling and capacity contracts. A toll buyer who controls dispatch effectively holds the owner's operating options. The capacity payment should reflect that value (Course 7). Synthesis
  • Market forecasts. Hysteresis means supply responds slowly and asymmetrically to prices. Forecasts that assume entry at full cost and exit at variable cost will misjudge how long low prices persist and how high prices must go to draw in new capacity. Dixit & Pindyck ch.7 §1 Synthesis
ALM analogy

Hysteresis is familiar from insurance: carriers keep writing an unprofitable line for years because exit costs (staff, distribution, reputation) are sunk and re-entry is expensive, and they wait for a hard market before entering a new one. Mothballing a product (stop selling but keep the infrastructure) versus closing it is the same three-state decision. Synthesis

Dated: today's examples

Dixit and Pindyck's copper and shipping examples are from the 1980s and early 1990s. Recent energy cases include refinery closures and conversions to renewable diesel after 2020, coal and gas plant retirements versus mothballing under capacity markets, and LNG trains held idle when spreads collapse. Market rules (for example ISO deactivation notices and reliability-must-run contracts) now constrain and pay for these options. Outside the texts

Going deeper: why the band, and why it is so wide

Entry and exit are linked options. Entering buys a project plus an option to exit, and exiting buys back the option to enter. Each threshold therefore depends on the other's costs. A cheaper exit (lower E) makes entry more attractive, lowering P_H, because the downside is easier to escape. Higher volatility raises the value of both waiting options, pushing P_H up and P_L down. The band can be wide even when sunk costs are small relative to project value, because what matters is the option value of keeping the other choice open, not just the size of the costs. Dixit & Pindyck ch.7 §1 Try it in the widget: set E to −$100 (part of I recovered on exit) and watch P_H fall. Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • With sunk entry and exit costs, firms enter above full cost and exit below variable cost. The gap grows with volatility.
  • Hysteresis explains persistent operation at a loss and slow capacity response to high prices.
  • Mothballing and reactivation are valuable middle options. Value assets with all their operating modes.
  • Entry and exit options are linked and must be solved together.
