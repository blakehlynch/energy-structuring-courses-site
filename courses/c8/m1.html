NPV, decision trees and real options

Secondary≈ 55 min8 quiz questions7 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Show how irreversibility plus the ability to wait creates an opportunity cost that NPV ignores.
  • Value a deferral option by replication with a twin security.
  • Explain why decision-tree analysis with a constant discount rate violates the law of one price.
  • Use risk-neutral probabilities and the market price of risk.

Prerequisites

  • Corporate finance NPV and CAPM; Course 6 Module 5 (option basics).

What the NPV rule misses

The textbook rule says invest when the present value of expected cash flows exceeds the cost. Dixit and Pindyck argue this is often wrong, and sometimes very wrong, because it ignores two features of most investments. Capital is irreversible, since a plant can rarely be un-built for its cost. And the firm can usually wait for more information. Together they make an investment opportunity like a call option. Investing kills the option, so the option's value is part of the true cost of investing. Dixit & Pindyck ch.1 If you could disinvest and recover the outlay, or if the choice really is now or never, the opportunity cost disappears and NPV is fine. Dixit & Pindyck ch.2 §1

Worked example 1 · invest now or wait a year?Checked by code

For a $520 outlay, a small plant earns a margin of one unit a year forever, starting immediately. Today's margin is $50. Next year it moves permanently to $80 or $25 with equal probability, and the risk is diversifiable, so we discount at 8%.

invest now: value = 50 + 52.50 / 0.08 = $706.25 → NPV = $186.25 wait a year, then invest only if the margin rose: at year 1 the payoff is 80 × 1.08/0.08 − 520 = $560 (up) or −182.5 (down, so don't invest) NPV of waiting = 0.5 × 560 / 1.08 = $259.26 → the right to wait is worth $73.01

NPV is positive, yet investing now is a mistake. If the outcomes widen to $95 or $5 with the same probabilities, NPV of investing now falls slightly to $155, but waiting is worth $353.01 and the option $198.01. Higher uncertainty raises the value of waiting. Dixit & Pindyck ch.2 §1 Copeland & Antikarov ch.4 (pp.85–87) Hedging doesn't change the answer either: selling the output forward fixes the NPV of investing now but leaves it below the value of waiting, a Modigliani–Miller result. Dixit & Pindyck ch.2 §1

Why one discount rate across a decision tree is wrong

A natural fix is decision-tree analysis: put the decisions in the tree and discount the expected payoffs at the project's cost of capital. Copeland and Antikarov show this violates the law of one price. A deferral option changes the pattern of payoffs, cutting off the bad states, so it has a different risk from the project and needs a different discount rate. The only way to know that rate is to value the option another way first. Copeland & Antikarov ch.4 (pp.89–92) The fix is replication: find a traded "twin" whose payoffs move in step with the project, build a portfolio of the twin and riskless bonds that matches the option's payoffs in every state, and price the option as that portfolio. Copeland & Antikarov ch.4 (pp.87–89) Equivalently, use risk-neutral probabilities \(p = ((1+r_f)-d)/(u-d)\) and discount at the risk-free rate. These probabilities don't change from node to node, which makes them convenient on a lattice. Copeland & Antikarov ch.4 (pp.96–102)

Worked example 2 · replication versus decision treeChecked by code

A project pays $150 or $60 next year with equal probability. A twin security trading at $23 pays $37.50 or $15, exactly a quarter of the project. The risk-free rate is 6%, and committing now means paying $105 next year for sure.

twin's expected return = (0.5 × 37.5 + 0.5 × 15)/23 − 1 = 14.13% → project PV = 4 × 23 = $92.00; commit-now NPV = 92 − 105/1.06 = $−7.06 deferral pays 45 or 0: replicate with m = 2.00 twins and B = $−28.30 of bonds → value $17.70 decision tree at 14.13%: 22.5 / 1.1413 = $19.71, too high; the rate that actually fits the deferral payoff is 27.13%

The flexibility to defer is worth $24.75 (from −$7.06 to +$17.70). The decision tree gets the sign right but the amount wrong, because the option is riskier than the project and deserves a higher rate. Copeland & Antikarov ch.4 (pp.89–92)

When no twin trades: the market price of risk

Hull generalises the method. Value a real asset by lowering the expected growth rate of each risky variable by its market price of risk times its volatility, then discount at the risk-free rate. For commodities, futures prices give the risk-neutral process directly, which is a big advantage for energy projects. Hull ch.36 (pp.781–784) Where no futures exist, the market price of risk can be estimated from the variable's correlation with the market (a CAPM argument), or set to zero if the variable is genuinely unrelated to the market. Hull ch.36 (pp.783–784) Hull also points out that comparable-company betas include those companies' own embedded options, so even the "base project" discount rate is suspect. Hull ch.36 (p.781)

ALM analogy

This is the move ALM made from discounting liability cash flows at a single rate to market-consistent valuation of embedded guarantees. A guarantee changes the payoff pattern, so it can't share the base contract's discount rate. You replicate it with traded instruments or use risk-neutral scenarios. The commodity forward curve plays the part the swap curve plays for you. Synthesis

Dated: practice and tools

Copeland and Antikarov (2001) and Dixit and Pindyck (1994) set the frame, and Hull's 2021 edition is current. In practice, energy companies now use real-options thinking mainly for staging, abandonment and optionality in development decisions and asset deals, while board-level hurdles often remain NPV-based. Climate-transition uncertainty (carbon prices, policy) is a major source of volatility today that the older texts don't address. Outside the texts

Going deeper: the full cost of investing

Dixit and Pindyck put the decision another way: invest only when the project's value covers the direct cost plus the value of the option you give up. In Example 1, investing now yields $706 but costs $520 plus a waiting option worth $259, so it doesn't pay. For a low enough investment cost, waiting forfeits a year's margin that is worth more than the option, and investing now wins. That is the crossover point in the widget. Dixit & Pindyck ch.2 §1 Hysteresis, triggers and mothballing (Modules 3–4) all follow from this idea of an opportunity cost. Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • Irreversibility plus the ability to wait makes investing a call option. The option's value is part of the cost of investing.
  • Uncertainty raises the value of waiting even when expected cash flows are unchanged.
  • Decision trees with one discount rate misprice flexibility. Replicate, or use risk-neutral probabilities.
  • Commodity futures give energy projects a risk-neutral process directly.
