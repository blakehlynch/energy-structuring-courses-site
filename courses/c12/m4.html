Counterparty exposure: EPE, PFE, netting and collateral

Core≈ 65 min8 quiz questions8 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Define EFV, EPE, ENE, PFE and average EPE, and compute them for simple cases.
  • Build the exposure profile of a monthly commodity swap and explain its shape.
  • Show how netting, thresholds and the margin period of risk change exposure.
  • Describe energy credit support: ISDA, EEI and NAESB agreements, delivered-not-paid risk, ring fencing.

Prerequisites

  • Modules 1–2; Course 7 Module 1 (swaps).

Why counterparty risk is asymmetric

If a counterparty defaults, the surviving party closes out the contracts, nets what is owed and accounts for any collateral. If the net value is negative, the survivor still owes the money. If it is positive, the survivor has a claim as an unsecured creditor and recovers only part of it. Losses come only from positive values, so exposure is defined as max(value, 0). Gregory (xVA) ch.11 (pp.283–284) From the survivor's side this looks like a short option: the defaulting counterparty can choose not to pay. Volatility therefore drives exposure, and even a simple swap's exposure is an option-pricing problem. Gregory (xVA) ch.11 (p.286) Counterparty risk is bilateral: the negative part, min(value, 0), is the counterparty's exposure to you, and Module 5 prices it as DVA. Gregory (xVA) ch.11 (p.284)

Exposure differs from market risk in three ways. It runs over the life of the trade, not ten days, so cash flows, ageing and the drift of the underlying matter. Netting and collateral terms change it. And it is needed for both risk management, using real-world parameters, and pricing, using risk-neutral ones. Gregory (xVA) ch.11 (pp.287–288) Gregory's earlier book stresses that over long horizons the drift can dominate the volatility, because volatility grows with the square root of time but drift grows linearly. Commodity forwards in contango or backwardation imply strong drifts that may not be good forecasts. Gregory (CCR) ch.8 (pp.150–151)

Exposure metrics

At each future date the distribution of portfolio value yields several metrics. Gregory (xVA) ch.11 (pp.288–291)

  • Expected future value (EFV): the mean of the future value, which can differ from today's value because of cash-flow timing and forward drift.
  • Potential future exposure (PFE): a high quantile of the exposure, the counterparty-risk analogue of VaR, used for credit limits.
  • Expected positive exposure (EPE): the mean of max(value, 0), counting negative scenarios as zero. It drives CVA.
  • Expected negative exposure (ENE): the mean of min(value, 0). It drives DVA.
  • Average EPE: the time-average of the EPE profile, a "loan-equivalent" amount effectively lent to the counterparty.

When the future value is normal with mean μ and standard deviation σ, the metrics have closed forms: Gregory (xVA) ch.11 (p.291)

\[ \text{EPE} = \mu\,\Phi\!\left(\tfrac{\mu}{\sigma}\right) + \sigma\,\varphi\!\left(\tfrac{\mu}{\sigma}\right), \qquad \text{PFE}_c = \mu + \alpha_c\,\sigma, \qquad \text{ENE} = \mu - \text{EPE} \]
Worked example 1 · Gregory's metrics by hand and in closed formChecked by code

Five equally likely scenarios give future values of 70, 50, 30, −10 and −30. Then take a normal distribution with mean 2 and standard deviation 2. Gregory (xVA) ch.11 (pp.290–291)

scenarios: EFV = 22, EPE = (70 + 50 + 30)/5 = 30, ENE = (−10 − 30)/5 = −8, PFE = 70 normal(2, 2): PFE 99% = 2 + 2.326 × 2 = 6.65, EPE = 2.17, ENE = −0.17

EPE is not the average of the positive values but the average across all scenarios with negatives set to zero. Like an out-of-the-money option, ENE in the normal case is very sensitive to volatility. Gregory (xVA) ch.11 (pp.289–291)

Exposure profiles of forwards and swaps

A forward with one payment at maturity has exposure that grows with the square root of time until it settles. A swap is different: uncertainty grows with time, but each settled period removes cash flows, so the exposure rises and then falls. Gregory's approximation for an interest rate swap is proportional to (T − t)√t, which peaks at a third of the maturity. Gregory (xVA) ch.11 (pp.292–294) A monthly fixed-for-floating commodity swap behaves the same way. With a flat curve, a par fixed price and one factor driving all delivery months, the value's standard deviation at time t is the remaining volume times the forward times its volatility times √t. Synthesis

\[ \sigma_V(t) = Q\,F\,\sigma \sum_{m>t} e^{-\kappa (T_m - t)}\ \sqrt{\frac{1 - e^{-2\kappa t}}{2\kappa}} \;\;\xrightarrow{\ \kappa\to 0\ }\;\; Q\,F\,\sigma\,N_{\text{rem}}(t)\sqrt{t} \]

The mean-reversion term κ reflects Gregory's point that commodities revert towards their marginal cost of production, which damps long-dated exposure. Gregory (CCR) ch.9 (p.168) Course 6 Module 3 introduced this one-factor model.

Worked example 2 · a five-year gas swapChecked by code

A dealer sells a utility a five-year monthly swap on 100,000 MMBtu a month at a par fixed price of $3.00. Forward volatility is 35% and there is no mean reversion. The dealer's exposure arises when gas rises.

scale: 100,000 × $3.00 × 35% = $105,000 per month remaining per √year month 20: σ_V = 105,000 × 40 × √(20/12) = $5.422m peak EPE (at month 20) = $2.163m peak 95% PFE = $8.919m average EPE (loan equivalent) = $1.496m with mean reversion κ = 0.5: peak 95% PFE = $2.965m

The peak at month 20 is a third of the way through, as Gregory's rule predicts. A swap with a notional value of $18 million carries a peak PFE of about $9 million and a loan equivalent of about $1.5 million. Mean reversion cuts the peak by two-thirds, so the choice of exposure model can move a credit line by a factor of three. Gregory (xVA) ch.11 (pp.291–294) Gregory (CCR) ch.9 (p.168) Synthesis

Netting and aggregation

Close-out netting lets the values of all trades under one master agreement offset at default. Exposure is computed on the netting set, and EPE across trades is not additive: the netted EPE can never exceed the sum of the individual EPEs. The benefit depends on correlation. Positively correlated trades offset little; negatively correlated trades, such as a client's hedges in both directions, offset a lot. Gregory (xVA) ch.11 (pp.302–304) Even a one-directional portfolio can reduce the exposure of a new trade if it is currently out of the money. Gregory (xVA) ch.11 (pp.304–305) Energy adds legal complications. Lehman's energy subsidiary, Eagle Energy Partners, filed days after its parent with no netting across the two entities. Offsetting positions became separate claims, terminated on different days. Swindle ch.16 (p.423) Edwards notes that many energy groups ring-fence a regulated utility from its unregulated parent, and structural subordination puts parent-level creditors behind the subsidiaries' own creditors. Knowing which legal entity you face matters as much as the trade. Edwards ch.6.3

Collateral, thresholds and the margin period of risk

Collateral held reduces exposure, so exposure becomes max(value − margin, 0). Posted collateral can create exposure if it is not returned. Gregory (xVA) ch.11 (pp.305–306) Collateral is imperfect for three reasons: granularity (thresholds and minimum transfer amounts), delay (the margin period of risk, MPoR, the time from the last margin received to closing out after a default), and the volatility of non-cash collateral. A threshold acts roughly as a cap on exposure. Gregory (xVA) ch.11 (pp.306–307) Swindle sets out the energy version. ISDA, EEI (power) and NAESB (gas) master agreements with credit support annexes define thresholds, netting, events of default, cross-default and close-out. The collateral requirement is min(max(V − L, 0), Cmax), and thresholds often tighten on downgrade. Swindle ch.16 (pp.423–424) Constellation Energy had to tell investors in 2008 that its potential downgrade collateral calls were roughly double what it had previously disclosed. Swindle ch.16 (p.423)

Worked example 3 · the same swap under a $2 million thresholdChecked by code

The utility signs a CSA with a $2 million threshold. Margin is called on the value at the start of a ten-day margin period of risk, so the dealer is exposed to the threshold plus any move during those ten days. Synthesis

ten-day value σ at month 20 = 105,000 × 40 × √(10/252) = $0.837m peak 95% PFE: uncollateralised $8.92m → with threshold = $3.995m peak EPE with threshold = $0.884m (average $0.753m) peak EPE with a zero threshold (MPoR risk only) = $0.501m

The threshold caps PFE near $2 million plus a ten-day move, and cuts the loan equivalent by half. Even with a zero threshold, exposure remains during the margin period of risk. That residual, and the liquidity the utility needs to meet margin calls, are what Module 6 prices. Gregory (xVA) ch.11 (pp.306–307) Synthesis

Settlement exposure in physical energy

Swindle points out that spot and cleared trades are not free of credit risk. Physical energy is delivered first and paid for weeks later, so the seller carries a delivered-not-paid (DNP) receivable. Swindle ch.16 (pp.422–423)

Worked example 4 · delivered-not-paid on a month of powerChecked by code
100 MW × 16 on-peak hours × 22 weekdays = 35,200 MWh receivable at $50/MWh = $1.76m, paid about two weeks into the next month

Two power contracts create a $1.8 million unsecured exposure that lasts six weeks and rolls every month. Swindle calls this short-dated but high-notional credit risk. It matters most to physical traders, and it is the receivable that Course 4's credit insurance and receivables finance protect. Swindle ch.16 (p.423) Synthesis

ALM analogy

EPE is the reinsurance recoverable of a derivatives book: the amount the counterparty would owe you if it failed, averaged over scenarios. PFE is the stressed recoverable used for a counterparty limit. A collateral threshold is an uncollateralised retention, and the margin period of risk is the lag before a funds-withheld account is topped up. Synthesis

Dated: margin rules and energy clearing

Since 2016 the bilateral margin rules have phased in initial margin for uncleared derivatives between large financial firms, and many energy end users are exempt. The SA-CCR method replaced older exposure add-ons for bank capital, with a supervisory factor for commodities. During the 2022 European crisis, exchange margin on power and gas futures rose so far that utilities sought state liquidity lines and some trading moved back to bilateral OTC. Check EMIR and CFTC margin rules, your clearing house's margin methodology, and the BCBS SA-CCR standard. Outside the texts

Going deeper: where the swap exposure peaks

For a swap with N equal monthly settlements, the value's standard deviation after k months is proportional to (N − k)√k. Differentiating, (N − k)/(2√k) − √k = 0 gives k = N/3, so the peak falls a third of the way through, as Gregory shows for interest rate swaps. Gregory (xVA) ch.11 (p.294) Two refinements change the picture for energy. Mean reversion caps the growth of uncertainty, so long-dated commodity swaps have flatter, lower profiles than a random-walk model suggests. Seasonal volumes, such as a winter-only supply deal, put the exposure in the months with the most volatile prices. Using the right model of the forward curve (Course 6) matters more for exposure than for valuation, because exposure depends on the whole distribution of future values, not just the mean. Gregory (CCR) ch.9 (p.168) Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • Exposure is max(value, 0): an option-like payoff driven by volatility, drift and the life of the trade.
  • EPE drives CVA, ENE drives DVA, PFE sets limits, and average EPE is the loan equivalent.
  • A monthly swap's exposure peaks about a third of the way through; mean reversion flattens it.
  • Netting, thresholds and the margin period of risk shape exposure; in energy, legal entity, ring fencing and delivered-not-paid matter as much as the model.
