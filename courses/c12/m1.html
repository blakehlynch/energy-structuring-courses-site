Value at risk and expected shortfall

Core≈ 55 min8 quiz questions7 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Compute parametric VaR and expected shortfall for a commodity position and convert between horizons and confidence levels.
  • Explain why VaR is not coherent and when expected shortfall is a better measure.
  • Choose VaR parameters for benchmarking, capital and backtesting.
  • Show how fat tails change VaR at high confidence levels.

Prerequisites

  • Course 6 Module 5 (volatility); basic statistics. ALM readers will know most of the capital logic.

What VaR measures

Value at risk (VaR) summarises the downside of a portfolio in one number: the loss over a chosen horizon that will be exceeded only with a small, chosen probability. Its appeal is that one figure can be compared across desks and reported to boards and shareholders. J.P. Morgan was among the first to disclose it, reporting an average daily trading VaR of $15 million at 95% in its 1994 annual report. Jorion ch.5 (p.105) Formally, with confidence level c and the loss L counted as a positive number, VaR is the smallest loss such that the chance of a bigger one is at most 1 − c. Jorion ch.5 (p.106)

\[ \Pr(L > \text{VaR}) \le 1 - c \]

VaR assumes the portfolio is frozen over the horizon: it combines today's positions with the distribution of risk-factor moves. Jorion ch.5 (p.105) Swindle stresses what that leaves out for energy. It ignores market depth and transaction costs, so it is at best a guide to losses while unwinding liquid positions in normal markets. Swindle ch.16 (p.411) Jorion lists the steps: mark the portfolio to market, measure the volatility of the risk factors, set the horizon, set the confidence level, and read the loss off the resulting distribution. Jorion ch.5 (p.107)

Parametric and historical VaR

VaR can be read directly from an empirical distribution of P&L as a sample quantile, or computed from a parametric distribution. Under normality it is a multiple of the standard deviation. Jorion ch.5 (pp.108–111) For a position W with daily volatility σ, a horizon of Δt trading days and the normal deviate α for the confidence level:

\[ \text{VaR} = \alpha\,\sigma\,\sqrt{\Delta t}\;W, \qquad \alpha_{95\%} = 1.645,\ \ \alpha_{99\%} = 2.326 \]

The square root of time assumes independent, identically distributed returns, and time is counted in trading days, conventionally 252 a year. Jorion ch.5 (p.108) Jorion distinguishes VaR relative to the expected value from absolute VaR relative to zero. Over a day they barely differ. Jorion ch.5 (p.108)

Worked example 1 · VaR and ES on a Henry Hub positionChecked by code

A desk is long 1,000,000 MMBtu of prompt Henry Hub at $3.00, a $3.0 million position. Daily volatility is 3%, about 47.6% a year, which is typical of prompt gas.

95% one-day VaR = 1.645 × 3% × $3.0m = $148.0k 99% one-day VaR = 2.326 × 3% × $3.0m = $209.4k 99% ten-day VaR = 2.326 × 3% × √10 × $3.0m = $662.1k 95% one-day ES = 3% × $3.0m × φ(1.645) / 0.05 = $185.6k 99% one-day ES = $239.9k

The ten-day figure is √10 ≈ 3.16 times the one-day figure. That holds only if the position is unchanged for ten days and daily moves are independent. Gas prices mean-revert and spike, so the scaling is a convention, not a fact. Jorion ch.5 (p.122) Synthesis

Expected shortfall and coherence

VaR says nothing about how bad losses are once the threshold is crossed. Artzner and co-authors set out four properties a risk measure should have for setting capital: monotonicity, translation invariance, homogeneity and subadditivity (merging portfolios cannot increase risk). A measure with all four is called coherent. Quantile VaR can fail subadditivity, for example with short options that carry a small chance of a large loss. The expected tail loss, also called expected shortfall (ES), is the average loss beyond VaR and is coherent. Jorion ch.5 (pp.113–114) Under normality ES is simply a bigger multiple of σ:

\[ \text{ES}_c = \sigma\,W\,\frac{\varphi(\alpha_c)}{1-c} \]

Jorion notes that, at the level of a whole bank, P&L tends towards normality and different measures rank risks similarly, which is why VaR survived as the benchmark. ES matters most for option books and concentrated credit risk. Jorion ch.5 (pp.114–115) Energy books are rarely diversified at that scale, which makes ES more relevant for them. Synthesis

Fat tails

A Student t with four to six degrees of freedom describes the tails of most financial data reasonably well. Jorion ch.5 (p.134) For typical confidence levels of 90–95% the normal distribution is usually adequate. At higher levels it underestimates losses, and extreme-value theory can smooth the far tail. Jorion ch.5 (pp.113, 129–134)

Worked example 2 · the same position with fat tailsChecked by code

Keep the 3% daily volatility but let returns follow a Student t with 4 degrees of freedom, scaled to the same standard deviation.

95% VaR: normal $148.0k t(4) $135.7k 99% VaR: normal $209.4k t(4) $238.5k (ratio 1.139) 99.9% VaR: normal $278.1k t(4) $456.5k 99% ES: normal $239.9k t(4) $332.2k

At 95% the fat-tailed distribution gives a lower VaR, because more of its variance sits in the extremes. At 99% the two are within 15%, but at 99.9% the t distribution's VaR is over 60% higher, and its 99% ES is almost 40% above the normal figure. Choosing a confidence level is partly choosing how much the distributional assumption matters. Jorion ch.5 (pp.132–134) Synthesis

Choosing the horizon and confidence level

Jorion argues that the parameters should follow the use. Jorion ch.5 (pp.115–120)

  • Benchmarking. If VaR is only a common yardstick, the choice is arbitrary as long as it is consistent; banks settled on one day at 99% to match Basel.
  • Potential loss. The horizon should be the time needed to liquidate or hedge the portfolio. Banks use a day because their positions turn over quickly, and because a daily figure can be compared with daily P&L.
  • Capital. If VaR sets equity capital, the confidence level should reflect the target credit rating. A Baa rating implied a one-year default rate of about 0.31%, and so a confidence level of 99.69%. The horizon should match the time needed to cut risk or raise capital.
  • Backtesting. Short horizons and moderate confidence give more observations in the tail, so the model can be tested. Module 3 shows why 95% is better than 99% for this.

The Basel internal-models approach used 99% over ten business days, multiplied by at least 3. Jorion shows how a factor of about 3 can be justified from Chebyshev's inequality, as protection against a misspecified distribution. Jorion ch.5 (pp.120, 136) Clearing houses use the same logic to set margin: a one-day 99% VaR on a futures contract. Jorion ch.5 (p.121) Course 7 Module 6 noted that margin funding is now a first-order hedging cost.

Worked example 3 · converting parameters and a ratings-based capital levelChecked by code
Jorion's steps: $100m × 15% × √(10/252) × 2.326 = $6.95m 95% one-day → 99% ten-day: (2.326 / 1.645) × √10 = × 4.472 Baa target: one-year default 0.31% → confidence 99.69%, deviate 2.737 capital for a book with $10m annual P&L volatility = 2.737 × $10m = $27.4m

Converting between conventions only works under normality and constant positions, and Jorion's $7 million is this calculation rounded. Jorion ch.5 (pp.107, 122) Using one-year normal VaR as capital for a single gas position fails badly: at 47.6% annual volatility a 2.737-sigma loss would exceed the position itself. For long horizons and concentrated commodity books, capital needs simulation and stress tests, not scaled daily VaR. Jorion ch.5 (p.117) Synthesis

How precise is a VaR number?

VaR is an estimate, and its sampling error can be large. Under normality the standard error of an estimated volatility is σ/√(2T), so a year of daily data pins volatility down to within a few percent. Sample quantiles are far noisier. With 250 observations the 95% band around the true 95% quantile of 1.645 runs from about 1.38 to 1.91, and around the 99% quantile of 2.33 from about 1.85 to 2.80. Jorion ch.5 (pp.123–128) Parametric VaR is more precise because the standard deviation uses every observation, but only if the distribution is right. ES is noisier still, because it averages a handful of extreme points. Jorion ch.5 (pp.127–129) Reporting VaR to many significant figures gives a false impression of accuracy. Jorion ch.5 (p.123)

VaR for an energy company: cash flow at risk

A trading desk marks to market daily; a producer, utility or airline cares about cash flow over its planning year. Jorion adapts VaR into cash flow at risk (CFaR): map each budgeted cash flow to the price variables that drive it, simulate those variables over the planning horizon, and read off the worst shortfall at a chosen confidence level. Earnings at risk extends the same idea to the income statement. Quantities, competitors' responses and operational hedges make CFaR harder to estimate than trading VaR. Jorion ch.15 (pp.384–387) For an origination desk, the client's CFaR is often the real objective of a hedge. Module 6 returns to the liquidity a hedge consumes through margin calls. Synthesis

ALM analogy

An actuary already uses this toolkit: the Solvency II 99.5% one-year VaR is exactly the "VaR as equity capital" case, with the confidence level set by a target default probability. Expected shortfall is the tail value at risk (TVaR) used in the Swiss Solvency Test. What changes on an energy desk is the horizon: daily rather than annual, with positions that can be closed in days rather than liabilities that run for decades. Synthesis Outside the texts

Dated: regulatory parameters

Jorion describes the Basel II market-risk rules. Under the Fundamental Review of the Trading Book, bank capital uses a 97.5% expected shortfall with liquidity horizons of 10 to 120 days by risk factor, plus backtesting and P&L attribution tests by desk. Commodity trading firms outside banking are not subject to these rules but often mirror them. Check the Basel Committee's MAR standard and your regulator's implementation. Outside the texts

Going deeper: the confidence level is a credit rating

Jorion's table of default rates makes the link between VaR and solvency explicit: Aaa firms had essentially zero one-year defaults and Ba firms about 1.4%. Over ten years the Baa default rate rises to about 7.6%. A firm can reach the same rating with a longer horizon at a lower confidence level, or a shorter horizon at a higher one. Jorion ch.5 (pp.117–118) A one-day 99% VaR, as used for limits, therefore says nothing on its own about solvency. It becomes a capital statement only once it is scaled to the horizon over which losses could build before management can act, and that scaling is exactly where the i.i.d. assumption breaks down for energy. Jorion's maxVAR makes a related point: if a portfolio can breach the threshold during the horizon and be liquidated before it recovers, the relevant loss is the worst along the path, which at 99% is about 11% larger. Jorion ch.5 (pp.117–119) Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • VaR is a quantile of the loss distribution for a frozen portfolio; parametric VaR is α × σ × √Δt × position.
  • Expected shortfall averages losses beyond VaR, is coherent, and matters for option and concentrated books.
  • Fat tails lower VaR at moderate confidence and raise it sharply at high confidence.
  • Parameters should follow the use: benchmarking, potential loss, capital or backtesting. For energy companies, cash flow at risk is often the measure that matters.
