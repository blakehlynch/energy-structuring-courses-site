CVA, DVA and wrong-way risk

Core≈ 65 min8 quiz questions7 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Derive default probabilities from a credit curve.
  • Compute the CVA of a commodity swap and express it per MMBtu.
  • Explain DVA and why CVA is a market price, not an actuarial reserve.
  • Assess wrong-way and right-way risk for producer and consumer hedges.

Prerequisites

  • Module 4.

Default probabilities: real-world and risk-neutral

CVA needs the probability that the counterparty defaults in each future period. Real-world probabilities come from historical default rates by rating; risk-neutral ones are implied from credit default swaps or bond spreads. Risk-neutral probabilities are higher, because investors demand a premium for bearing credit risk. Giesecke and co-authors found that, over almost 150 years, credit spreads averaged about twice realised default losses. Gregory (xVA) ch.12 (p.315) The gap is largest for good credits. Hull and co-authors estimated a Baa one-year default probability of 47 basis points from history against 238 implied from spreads. Gregory (xVA) ch.12 (p.316)

Accounting has settled the choice for most firms. IFRS 13 requires observable market inputs for fair value, so CVA is now generally a market price of counterparty risk computed from spreads, not an actuarial reserve. Most counterparties have no traded CDS, so banks map them to proxy curves by rating, industry and region. Gregory (xVA) ch.12 (pp.317–318) Edwards notes that CDS-implied probabilities are available only for very large names such as oil majors and banks, and that the rest of an energy book relies on ratings. Edwards ch.6.3 A common approximation turns a credit spread s and loss given default (LGD) into the unconditional probability of default between two dates: Gregory (xVA) ch.12 (p.319)

\[ \text{PD}(t_{i-1}, t_i) \approx \exp\!\left(-\frac{s_{i-1}\,t_{i-1}}{\text{LGD}}\right) - \exp\!\left(-\frac{s_i\,t_i}{\text{LGD}}\right) \]
Worked example 1 · Gregory's credit curveChecked by code

Spreads of 100, 125, 150, 175 and 200 basis points at one to five years, with a 60% LGD. Gregory (xVA) ch.12 (p.319)

year 1: 1 − e^(−0.0100 × 1 / 0.6) = 1.65% year 2: e^(−0.0100 × 1 / 0.6) − e^(−0.0125 × 2 / 0.6) = 2.43% year 3 = 3.14% year 4 = 3.79% year 5 = 4.34%

These match Gregory's table. Recovery assumptions matter less than they appear, because the same LGD is used to imply the PD and then to apply it, and the two partly cancel. Recoveries vary enormously in practice: Lehman's CDS auction settled at 8.6%, while actual recoveries on its derivatives claims later reached 30–40%. Gregory (xVA) ch.12 (pp.321–322)

The CVA formula

CVA combines three questions: does the counterparty default, what is the exposure if it does, and how much of it is lost? Gregory (xVA) ch.17 (p.487) Assuming the three are independent, there is no wrong-way risk, and CVA becomes a weighted sum of the discounted EPE profile from Module 4: Gregory (xVA) ch.17 (pp.488–489)

\[ \text{CVA} \approx \text{LGD}\sum_{i=1}^{m} \text{EPE}(t_i)\;\text{PD}(t_{i-1}, t_i) \]

Edwards gives the same idea in its simplest form, expected loss = PD × LGD × EAD, applied at each payment date. Edwards ch.6.3 A useful approximation expresses CVA as a running spread: CVA per year is roughly the average EPE times the counterparty's credit spread. This separates the credit component from the market-risk component. Gregory (xVA) ch.17 (p.492)

Worked example 2 · CVA on the five-year gas swapChecked by code

Take the uncollateralised five-year swap of Module 4: 100,000 MMBtu a month, $3.00, 35% volatility. The utility's credit spread is a flat 300 basis points, the LGD is 60% and discounting is at 4%.

cumulative five-year default probability = 1 − e^(−0.03 × 5 / 0.6) = 22.1% CVA = 0.6 × Σ EPE(t_i) × PD(t_{i−1}, t_i) × DF(t_i) = $0.186m per MMBtu on 6 million MMBtu = 3.11 cents approximation: average EPE $1.496m × 3% × 5 years = $0.224m

The credit charge is about 3 cents per MMBtu, roughly 1% of the price, on a swap whose bid-offer might be 1–2 cents. The approximation overstates it here because it ignores discounting and the falling survival probability. It is still useful for intuition: double the spread or the average exposure and the CVA roughly doubles. Gregory (xVA) ch.17 (pp.492–493) Synthesis

Two further features matter for pricing. The shape of the credit curve changes CVA even at the same five-year spread: for Gregory's ten-year swap, CVA is 24.6 basis points with an upward-sloping curve, 20.0 with a flat one and 15.7 with an inverted one. Gregory (xVA) ch.17 (pp.493–494) And CVA is non-linear in the spread: as a counterparty approaches default, CVA converges to the current exposure, which creates large credit gamma and jump-to-default risk. Gregory (xVA) ch.17 (p.494)

DVA: your own default

By symmetry, the counterparty is exposed to you. Debit value adjustment (DVA) is the value of your own option to default on what you owe, driven by ENE and your own spread. Gregory (xVA) ch.11 (p.284) Edwards notes that US fair-value accounting (ASC 820) requires credit adjustments to both assets and liabilities: CVA lowers the value of what counterparties owe you, and DVA lowers the value of what you owe them. Edwards ch.6.3 DVA produces the uncomfortable result that a firm reports gains when its own credit worsens. It also cannot be monetised without defaulting or buying back the liability, which is why it is contentious. Gregory (xVA) ch.11 (p.284) Synthesis

Wrong-way and right-way risk

The CVA formula above assumes exposure and default are independent. Wrong-way risk (WWR) is an adverse dependence: exposure tends to be largest just as default becomes more likely. Right-way risk is the reverse and lowers CVA. Gregory (xVA) ch.17 (p.514) Gregory's commodity example is a producer hedging its output. The producer owes money when prices are high, which is when it is most profitable, so the trade is right-way. That holds because the producer is hedging rather than speculating. Gregory (xVA) ch.17 (p.515) Gregory (CCR) ch.15 (pp.309–310)

Consumers are subtler. An airline that buys an oil swap owes money when oil falls, which is good for its costs, so small price falls look right-way. A very large fall usually means a recession, which is bad for airlines, so in the tail the trade becomes wrong-way. Gregory's earlier book draws this as right-way risk turning into wrong-way risk, as seen in the 2008 crisis. Gregory (CCR) ch.15 (p.328) Swindle tested these arguments with credit spreads. Producers' CDS spreads did tend to fall as prices rose (Apache −0.25 and Chesapeake −0.32 against WTI). But consumers such as Alcoa (−0.40) and Delta (−0.08) showed the same sign, because macroeconomic shocks drive both energy prices and credit. Swindle ch.16 (pp.426–427) He also warns of a structural source of WWR in energy: credit support by liens on a producer's reserves is worth least exactly when low prices make default likely. Swindle ch.16 (pp.428–429)

Gregory distinguishes general WWR, driven by macroeconomic relationships that may show in data, from specific WWR, driven by structural links that history may not reveal. The first can be priced; the second should be avoided or stress-tested. Gregory (xVA) ch.17 (p.516) A simple way to price general WWR uses a single correlation between the exposure driver and default, as Gregory does: replace the EPE in each period by the EPE conditional on default then. Gregory (xVA) ch.17 (p.516) In a one-factor Gaussian model, conditional on default at t, the exposure's mean shifts up by ρ times the default threshold: Synthesis

\[ \text{EPE}(t \mid \tau = t) = \text{EPE}_{\text{normal}}\!\left(\mu = -\rho\,\Phi^{-1}\!\big(F(t)\big)\,\sigma_V(t),\ \ \sigma = \sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,\sigma_V(t)\right) \]
Worked example 3 · wrong-way and right-way CVA on the swapChecked by code

Same swap and credit curve. A correlation of +0.5 represents a counterparty that weakens as gas rises; −0.3 represents a producer whose credit improves as gas rises.

CVA, no WWR = $0.186m CVA, ρ = +0.5 (wrong-way) = $0.377m (× 2.02) CVA, ρ = −0.3 (right-way) = $0.098m (× 0.53) EPE uplift at ρ = 0.5: year 1 × 2.27, year 5 × 1.43 same ρ for a 100 bp counterparty: CVA × 2.54

A 0.5 correlation roughly doubles CVA, in line with Gregory's illustration. The uplift is largest early in the trade and for better credits: when default is unlikely, a default is a bigger surprise and carries a bigger shift in the exposure driver. That is Gregory's counter-intuitive conclusion that WWR increases as credit quality improves. Gregory (xVA) ch.17 (pp.516–517) Synthesis

Managing energy CVA

CVA has market risk (to the commodity) and credit risk (to the spread) at once, and energy makes both hard to hedge. Commodity volatility is often twice that of other asset classes, so the exposure, and the CDS notional needed to hedge it, change constantly. Single-name CDS on energy names were wide, about 20 basis points bid-offer at five years and 120 at one year. Index CDS were cheaper to trade but correlated only about 0.45 with names like Apache. Swindle ch.16 (pp.429–430) Banks therefore run CVA desks that charge CVA to the trading desks and manage it centrally. Module 6 covers how. Swindle ch.16 (p.423)

ALM analogy

CVA is the credit spread an insurer charges in a matching-adjustment or illiquidity-premium calculation, but applied to a contingent asset whose size moves with the market. Real-world versus risk-neutral PDs is the same debate as best-estimate defaults versus spread-implied defaults when valuing a credit portfolio. Wrong-way risk is the reinsurer that fails in the same catastrophe it was meant to cover. Synthesis

Dated: CVA capital and the CDS market

Basel III introduced a CVA capital charge, revised in the final Basel III package into the BA-CVA and SA-CVA methods. Single-name CDS liquidity has fallen since 2012, so most energy counterparties are proxied from indices. The 2020–22 period added new wrong-way cases, including counterparties hit by both price spikes and margin calls. Check the BCBS MAR50 standard and your accounting policy for the CVA method actually applied. Outside the texts

Going deeper: why wrong-way risk grows as credit quality improves

In the one-factor model, default happens when a latent variable falls below Φ−1(PD). Conditional on default, the latent variable sits at that threshold, so the exposure driver's mean shifts by −ρΦ−1(PD) standard deviations. For a high-yield name with a 20% cumulative PD the threshold is about −0.84; for an investment-grade name with a 2% PD it is about −2.05. The same correlation therefore shifts the conditional exposure more than twice as far for the better credit. Gregory's figure shows exactly this ordering. Gregory (xVA) ch.17 (p.517) Synthesis The practical consequence: a strong-looking counterparty with a business tied to the trade, such as a utility whose rate base moves with gas, or an airline in a recession, can carry a larger proportional WWR adjustment than a weaker but unrelated name. That is why Gregory recommends treating specific WWR by limits and stress tests rather than a correlation parameter. Gregory (xVA) ch.17 (pp.516–518)

Key takeaways

  • CVA uses risk-neutral default probabilities from spreads (or proxies), making it a market price of counterparty risk.
  • CVA ≈ LGD × Σ EPE × PD, or roughly average EPE × spread per year; for a five-year gas swap it is a few cents per MMBtu.
  • DVA is the mirror image, driven by ENE and your own spread.
  • Hedging producers are usually right-way; consumers can turn wrong-way in a crash, and lien-based collateral is a structural wrong-way risk.
