Backtesting by counting exceptions

A VaR model makes a testable claim: losses larger than the 99% VaR should happen on about 1% of days. Backtesting compares each day's VaR with the next day's P&L and counts the exceptions. Jorion recommends testing against both actual P&L and a hypothetical P&L on the frozen portfolio. If the model passes on hypothetical but fails on actual P&L, intraday trading is the cause; if it fails on hypothetical P&L, the model itself is wrong. Jorion ch.6 (p.142)

If the model is right, the number of exceptions N in T days is binomial with probability p = 1 − c. For large samples the standardised count is approximately normal, and Kupiec's likelihood ratio gives a test that is asymptotically chi-square with one degree of freedom, rejected above 3.841. Jorion ch.6 (pp.143–147)

\[ z = \frac{N - pT}{\sqrt{p(1-p)T}}, \qquad \text{LR}_{uc} = -2\ln\!\left[(1-p)^{T-N}p^{N}\right] + 2\ln\!\left[\left(1-\tfrac{N}{T}\right)^{T-N}\left(\tfrac{N}{T}\right)^{N}\right] \]
Worked example 1 · J.P. Morgan in 1998Checked by code

J.P. Morgan reported that daily revenue fell short of its 95% VaR on 20 days in 1998, nine of them between August and October. Take 252 trading days. Jorion ch.6 (p.144)

expected exceptions = 5% × 252 = 12.6 z = (20 − 12.6) / √(0.05 × 0.95 × 252) = 2.14 (> 1.96) Kupiec LR = 3.91 (> 3.841)

Both tests reject the model at 95% test confidence, and these are the figures Jorion reports. The bank switched to historical simulation in the fourth quarter of 1998, which handles fat tails better. Jorion ch.6 (pp.144, 147)

Type 1 and type 2 errors and the Basel traffic light

Any backtest trades off two errors: rejecting a correct model (type 1) and accepting a wrong one (type 2). Rare events make the trade-off harsh. With one year of data at 99%, Kupiec's non-rejection region is any count below 7, with no lower bound. Jorion ch.6 (pp.146–147) Our own calculation differs slightly from that table: the likelihood ratio does reject a year with no exceptions at all (LR = 5.07 for the table's 252 days, 5.03 for 250). That is its only signal against a model that overstates risk, so it catches only gross overstatement. A model reported as 99% whose true coverage is 99.9% has no exceptions in 78% of years and is then rejected; at a true 99.5% that happens in only 29% of years. Synthesis The Basel rules count exceptions of the 99% VaR over 250 days. Up to four is green; five to nine is yellow, with the capital multiplier raised from 3 by 0.40 to 0.85; ten or more is red and adds 1. In the yellow zone the supervisor decides, depending on whether the cause is model integrity, accuracy, intraday trading or bad luck. Jorion ch.6 (pp.148–149)

Worked example 2 · six exceptions at 99%Checked by code
expected exceptions = 1% × 250 = 2.5 P(5 or more | model correct) = 10.8% (type 1 error of the yellow line) P(6 or more | model correct) = 4.1% P(4 or fewer | true coverage only 97%) = 12.8% (type 2 error) Kupiec LR with N = 6 = 3.56 (< 3.841: not rejected) Basel zone: yellow → multiplier = 3.50

Six exceptions in a year would happen only 4.1% of the time with a correct model, yet Kupiec's test does not reject it, while Basel's yellow zone already raises capital by a sixth. A model with only 97% coverage, whose reported 99% VaR is about 19% below the true one, still passes as green 12.8% of the time. Jorion's point is that the test's weakness comes from the 99% level: testing at 95% catches more bad models for a similar type 1 error. Jorion ch.6 (pp.149–150)

Liquidity-adjusted VaR

VaR values positions at mid prices and assumes they can be closed at no cost. If the bid-offer spread S is fixed, liquidation costs half the spread on the whole position, which can simply be added. Bangia and co-authors add a worst-case widening of the spread as well. Jorion ch.13 (p.341) For large positions the price impact matters more: selling everything at once costs the most, while selling evenly over n days cuts impact but leaves the position exposed to price risk for longer. Jorion ch.13 (pp.342–344)

\[ \text{LVaR} = \alpha\sigma W + \tfrac12\,W S, \qquad \text{risk of uniform liquidation over } n \text{ days} = \frac{(n-1)(2n-1)}{6n}\ \text{days of variance} \]
Worked example 3 · an illiquid basis positionChecked by code

A desk holds $20 million of gas at an illiquid hub with 3% daily volatility. The broker market is $0.10 wide on a $3.00 price, a 3.33% spread.

95% one-day VaR = 1.645 × 3% × $20m = $986.9k half-spread cost = ½ × $20m × 3.33% = $333.3k liquidity-adjusted VaR = $1,320.2k uniform sale over 5 days: variance of 1.20 days → VaR $1,081.1k (before costs) uniform sale over 31 days ≈ 9.84 days of variance

For a liquid stock Jorion's spread adjustment is about 7% of VaR; here it is a third, because illiquid energy hubs have wide markets. Spreading the sale over five days exposes the book to only 1.2 days of variance, because the position shrinks as it is sold. The Basel ten-day horizon is roughly equivalent to an even sale over a month. Jorion ch.13 (pp.341, 344) Synthesis Swindle adds that a full unwind of a complex energy book can take months or years, so for illiquid books VaR should be read as the risk of the benchmark exposures that could be liquidated, not of the whole book. Swindle ch.16 (p.413)

Stress testing

VaR describes normal markets at a chosen confidence level; stress tests ask what happens in abnormal ones. Jorion calls stress testing non-statistical, since it attaches no probability to the result. It combines scenario analysis, stressed models and parameters, and policy responses, and it was one of the Basel conditions for using internal models. Jorion ch.14 (p.357) The October 1987 crash was a 20-standard-deviation day for US equities. Bookstaber's rule of thumb is that every market sees at least one 4-sigma day a year, and some market sees a 10-sigma day. Jorion ch.14 (pp.358–359) Stress tests matter most where history is not a guide: shocks that have never occurred, or structural breaks such as the 1992 collapse of the European exchange-rate mechanism. Jorion ch.14 (pp.360–361)

  • Sensitivity tests move one factor at a time by a set amount. They are simple and comparable, but ignore correlation. Jorion ch.14 (pp.362–363)
  • SPAN, the exchange margin system, scans a grid of price and volatility moves and sets margin at the worst portfolio loss, with extreme moves counted at 35%. Jorion ch.14 (pp.363–365)
  • Prospective scenarios posit an event and infer market moves. The factor-push method pushes every factor to an extreme in its worst direction, which is conservative but ignores correlation. Jorion ch.14 (pp.365–366)
  • Conditional scenarios stress the key variables and predict the others from their covariance with the key variables. Kupiec found that this predicted loss tracked actual losses far better than stressing the key variable alone. Jorion ch.14 (pp.366–367)
  • Historical scenarios replay real episodes, which builds in the correlations of the time. Jorion ch.14 (p.368)
Worked example 4 · a cold snap on the spark bookChecked by code

Use the spark book of Module 2: long $2.5m of power, short $1.125m of gas, daily volatilities 4% and 3%, correlation 0.70. The scenario is a cold snap that lifts gas 60%.

narrow stress loss (gas only) = −1.125m × 60% = $−675k regression beta of power on gas = 0.7 × 4% / 3% = 0.933 predicted power move = 0.933 × 60% = 56.0% conditional (predicted) result = 2.5m × 56% − 1.125m × 60% = +$725k if power rises only 20% = $−175k

Stressing gas alone shows a $675k loss; the conditional scenario says the book makes $725k, because power usually follows gas up. Whether it does depends on why gas spiked. If the cold snap also raises power demand, the book wins. If gas spikes on a pipeline constraint while power is set by other plants, the book loses. Correlations estimated in normal times are exactly what fails in stress, so Jorion recommends checking stress results with correlations from hectic periods. Jorion ch.14 (pp.366–368) Synthesis

Lessons from energy losses

Jorion's list of derivatives losses includes Metallgesellschaft's $1.34 billion on oil futures in 1994 and China Aviation Oil's $550 million in 2004. Jorion ch.2 (p.33) MG's US subsidiary had sold 180 million barrels of fixed-price product over ten years and hedged with rolling short-dated futures. When spot prices fell from $20 to $15 in 1993, it faced about a billion dollars of margin calls, and the parent liquidated at a loss of $1.3 billion. Jorion classes it as market and liquidity risk. Jorion ch.2 (pp.38–39) Barings was a failure of controls: one trader ran both the front and back office. Jorion ch.2 (pp.36–38) Swindle adds the energy-specific pattern: some blow-ups come from big outright bets, but probably more from large basis positions at illiquid locations, which control groups missed because nobody verified the marks. Swindle ch.16 (p.405) His first line of defence is price integrity: checking illiquid marks against exchange settlements, consensus surveys, recent trades and statistical relationships to liquid hubs. Swindle ch.16 (pp.406–409)

ALM analogy

Backtesting is experience analysis. An actuary compares actual against expected deaths or lapses and uses a binomial or Poisson test, facing the same problem: rare events give low power. Conditional stress testing is the same discipline as a realistic disaster scenario that moves all assumptions together rather than one at a time. Synthesis

Going deeper: why a 99% test is weak

At 99% over 250 days a correct model expects 2.5 exceptions, and the counts from correct and badly wrong models overlap heavily. Jorion's table shows that, using the Basel yellow line, a correct model is wrongly penalised 10.8% of the time, while a model with only 97% coverage passes 12.8% of the time. Moving the test to 95% gives 12.5 expected exceptions a year. Rejecting the model at 17 or more exceptions gives a similar type 1 error of 12.5%, but the type 2 error, against a model whose true VaR is 23.7% above the reported one (the reported VaR is 19.2% too low), falls to 7.4%. Jorion ch.6 (pp.149–150) The practical lesson for an energy desk: backtest at 95%, scale to 99% for limits, and treat a cluster of exceptions in a stress period as a model problem, not bad luck. Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • Backtest VaR against hypothetical and actual P&L by counting exceptions; use the binomial distribution and Kupiec's likelihood ratio.
  • At 99% with one year of data, tests have little power; Basel's traffic light raises capital progressively from five exceptions.
  • Add half the bid-offer to VaR for illiquid positions, and remember that liquidation horizons change both cost and risk.
  • Stress tests should move correlated factors together, using conditional or historical scenarios, and price integrity checks catch the basis losses VaR misses.
