Ancillary services, renewables and storage

Foundation≈ 60 min9 quiz questions8 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Classify balancing and reserve services by timescale.
  • Price reserve at opportunity cost.
  • Explain renewables' effects on prices and capture rates.
  • Value storage arbitrage and its limits.

Prerequisites

  • Modules 3 and 5.

Keeping the system secure

The operator must keep the system able to survive whatever happens next, not just balanced now. Kirschen and Strbac's opening example: two 100 MW units can securely serve only 100 MW, because one could trip. Kirschen & Strbac §6.2 Imbalances arrive with different "time signatures", and each needs a different resource. Kirschen & Strbac §6.2.1.1

  • Regulation: fast, continuous correction of small random fluctuations under automatic generation control.
  • Load following: tracking slower movements within trading periods that the energy market doesn't schedule.
  • Contingency reserves: large, sudden losses such as a unit trip. Spinning reserve responds immediately. Supplemental reserve can come from units not yet synchronised, or from interruptible load.

Kirschen and Strbac describe a 1995 GB incident in which over 1 GW tripped. Primary response arrested the frequency fall within seconds, secondary response restored it within minutes, and gas turbines then started to replace the lost output. Kirschen & Strbac §6.2.1.1 Wind and solar connect through power electronics and add no inertia, so frequency falls faster after a trip as their share grows. Forecast uncertainty also widens with lead time, which raises reserve needs. Kirschen & Strbac §6.2.1, §6.2.1.2 Network security adds thermal, voltage and stability limits on transfers. Voltage is supported by reactive power, which is best supplied by generators near where it is needed. Kirschen & Strbac §6.2.2

Ancillary services and how they're priced

Stoft lists the services an operator needs: real-power balancing and frequency stability, voltage support, transmission security, economic dispatch, and enforcing trades. Stoft ch.3-4 Operators obtain them through mandatory provision, long-term contracts, or markets, often co-optimised with energy. Kirschen & Strbac §6.3–6.5 The key pricing idea is opportunity cost. A unit held back to provide reserve gives up the energy margin it would otherwise earn, so the reserve price must at least cover it. Scoring reserve offers on capacity price alone invites gaming. Stoft ch.3-10

Worked example 1 · opportunity cost of reserveChecked by code

A unit with a $38/MWh marginal cost is backed down by 1 MW to hold reserve when energy pays $55.

forgone margin = 55 − 38 = $17 per MW-hour, the minimum reserve price that leaves it indifferent

In co-optimised markets the reserve price and the energy price move together for exactly this reason. Stoft ch.3-10 Synthesis

Wind and solar in the market

Kirschen and Strbac summarise the issues. Wind and solar have free fuel but are intermittent (they produce when the weather allows) and stochastic (forecasts err), so they face imbalance costs (Module 3). Policy support takes several forms: renewable portfolio standards, investment and production tax credits, feed-in tariffs, government CfDs, and tradable certificates. Kirschen & Strbac §4.5.3 Their market effects:

  • They lower average prices by displacing higher-cost plant.
  • Prices are high when the wind drops or the sun sets.
  • They can drive prices negative when a production subsidy makes it worth generating even at a negative price.
  • Rooftop solar creates a midday dip in net demand.

Kirschen & Strbac §4.5.3.3 As their share rises, their own production hours become the cheap hours. The price they capture falls relative to the average, an effect usually called cannibalisation. Outside the texts

Storage and flexible demand

Storage earns by temporal arbitrage: buy low, sell high, losing a fraction to round-trip inefficiency. Kirschen and Strbac show both self-scheduling against forecast prices and central optimisation. A large battery also changes prices: it can become the price-setter and flatten the profile until its own arbitrage margin disappears. Kirschen & Strbac §4.6 Flexible demand works like storage without new hardware, but subject to process or comfort constraints, and small loads need aggregation. Kirschen & Strbac §4.7

Worked example 3 · a 4-hour battery on the duck dayChecked by code

1 MW, 4 MWh, 85% round-trip efficiency: charge in the 4 cheapest hours and discharge in the 4 dearest.

charging cost $171; discharge revenue (× 0.85) $587; margin $416 per MW per day if every day looked like this: ≈ $152k per MW-year, before degradation, other services and price impact

Note that sorting the 4 cheapest and 4 dearest hours is exact here because the midday solar trough chronologically precedes the evening ramp, and applying \(\eta=0.85\) to the 4 discharge hours models a 4 MWh tank discharging 3.4 MWh of net energy (or charging \(4/0.85=4.71\text{ MWh}\) over 4.71 hours at a 1 MW inverter rating); on multi-peak days a chronological dynamic program enforcing \(0 \le SoC_t \le E_{\max}\) at every hour is required. Real spreads also vary day to day, and arbitrage revenue shrinks as more storage competes for the same spread. Kirschen & Strbac §4.6 Synthesis

The price shape is stylised: net demand (demand minus solar) mapped to price by a convex supply curve. The battery is a price-taker charging once and discharging once a day.

ALM analogy

A battery is a calendar-spread trader in electricity: long the cheap hours, short the dear ones, paying a "carry cost" in efficiency losses. Its revenue resembles a strip of daily spread options, and its capacity value is like a liquidity facility the operator can draw. Solar cannibalisation is a crowded-trade effect: when everyone owns the same exposure, its market price falls. Synthesis

Dated: renewables, storage and services

Battery costs and deployment have changed beyond anything in the texts. California and Texas now run multi-GW battery fleets, GB has new fast frequency services (Dynamic Containment and successors), and markets value inertia and fast response explicitly. Negative prices are frequent in many markets, and FERC Order 841 (2018) opened US wholesale markets to storage. Outside the texts Check ISO ancillary-service manuals, the EIA, BloombergNEF and Lazard LCOS summaries, and NESO (GB) for current products and prices.

Going deeper: stacking revenues

Storage and flexible plant rarely live on one revenue stream. They stack energy arbitrage, reserves and regulation (opportunity-cost priced), capacity payments (Module 5) and sometimes local network value. The streams compete for the same MW. Holding reserve caps how much arbitrage you can do, and capacity obligations require charge to be available at peak. Kirschen & Strbac §4.6, §6.5 Stoft ch.3-10 Synthesis Valuing the stack is an optimisation with exclusivity constraints: the storage problem of Course 7, applied to electrons.

Key takeaways

  • Regulation, load following and contingency reserves address imbalances on different timescales. Lower inertia and forecast error raise needs.
  • Ancillary services are best priced at opportunity cost and co-optimised with energy.
  • Wind and solar lower average prices, create negative-price hours and depress their own capture prices as they grow.
  • Storage arbitrages daily spreads less efficiency losses. Its margins shrink as storage capacity grows, so value comes from stacking services.
