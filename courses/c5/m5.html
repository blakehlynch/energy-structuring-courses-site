Scarcity, VOLL and capacity markets

Foundation≈ 60 min9 quiz questions9 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Explain how scarcity rents recover peaker costs.
  • Compute optimal shortage hours under VOLL pricing.
  • Quantify missing money and an implied capacity price.
  • Compare capacity mechanisms and their risk properties.

Prerequisites

  • Modules 2–3.

Price spikes pay for peakers

Stoft's central argument in Part 2: in a competitive market where generators bid marginal cost, plants still recover their fixed costs, through scarcity rents earned when price exceeds their variable cost. For peakers those rents come almost entirely from short, high price spikes when capacity is exhausted. Stoft ch.2-2 Summarise the year's prices as a price-duration curve. The area above a plant's variable cost is its annual contribution to fixed costs, which Stoft calls the aggregate price spike for a peaker. Long-run entry and exit push that area to equal the peaker's fixed cost. Stoft ch.2-2

Worked example 1 · fixed-cost recovery from a spikeChecked by code

Suppose prices exceed a plant's $50 variable cost 3% of the year, rising linearly to a $2,050 peak.

average fixed-cost contribution = (2,050 − 50) × 0.03 / 2 = $30/MWh of capacity, about $263k per MW-year

A few dozen hours can matter more than the rest of the year combined. Stoft ch.2-2 Synthesis

Value of lost load

When demand can't respond, the operator rations by shedding load, so somebody must set the price in those hours. Stoft defines VOLL as the marginal loss of consumer value per MWh shed: well defined but very hard to estimate. He cites an Australian study finding customer values from $1,000 to $90,000/MWh. Stoft ch.2-5 Setting the shortage price to VOLL is optimal in his simple model. Peakers are then built until the expected hours of shedding, D, satisfy D × (VOLL − VCpeaker) = FCpeaker, which is the same condition a planner would choose. Stoft ch.2-5 Errors in estimating VOLL are costly but not catastrophic, and no better method exists until demand can respond. Stoft ch.2-5

\[ D^{*} = \frac{FC_{peaker}}{VOLL - VC_{peaker}} \]
Worked example 2 · how many shortage hours?Checked by code

Using Module 2's peaker (fixed cost $76.6k/MW-yr, variable cost $92.50/MWh):

VOLL $10,000/MWh: D* = 76,621 / (10,000 − 92.5) = 7.7 hours of shortage a year price cap of $1,000/MWh: the peaker needs 84.4 hours at the cap to break even

A low cap doesn't remove the need for revenue. It requires many more scarcity hours, meaning a tighter, less reliable system, or some other payment. Stoft ch.2-4, ch.2-5 Synthesis

Missing money and its remedies

Kirschen and Strbac explain why pure energy-only markets struggle in practice. Price spikes are politically unpopular and are capped. They are very risky for investors, because a mild summer or a wet year means few spikes. Long build times can create boom-and-bust investment cycles. They conclude that consumers buy energy with reliability, and markets usually add a mechanism for capacity. Kirschen & Strbac §7.3.1 The shortfall a cap creates is commonly called "missing money" Outside the texts. Remedies:

  • Capacity payments: a regular $/MW payment, simple but hard to set. The England and Wales Pool added a capacity element of LOLP × (VOLL − SMP) to the energy price, roughly LOLP × VOLL when the system marginal price is small (for example 0.2% × $5,000 ≈ $10/MWh in that half-hour), which proved easy to manipulate. Kirschen & Strbac §7.3.2
  • Capacity requirements and markets: retailers must hold capacity (Stoft cites requirements around 118% of peak) or buy it in an organised market, with deficiency penalties and derating for forced outages. Stoft shows that a capacity requirement can fully replace energy price spikes as the investment signal, with lower risk. But regulators usually forget to make capacity demand elastic, inviting market power. Stoft ch.2-8 Kirschen & Strbac §7.3.3
  • Reliability options: central purchase of long-dated call options. Generators receive a premium, pay back (spot − strike) when spot exceeds a high strike, and face penalties if unavailable. Consumers get a hedge, and generators get steady revenue. Kirschen & Strbac §7.3.4
  • Operating-reserve pricing: high prices when reserves run short, rather than only when load is shed. Stoft argues this gives the same investment with less risk and market power. Stoft ch.2-6

The Operating Reserve Demand Curve (ORDC)

In energy-only markets (most famously ERCOT), missing money is resolved without a capacity market by implementing an Operating Reserve Demand Curve (ORDC), formulated by William Hogan. Instead of pricing scarcity only when rolling blackouts occur, the system operator automatically calculates an administrative price adder in real time based on the probability that operating reserves will fall below the minimum contingency level (\(X_{\min}\)): \[ P_{adder} = (VOLL - \lambda) \cdot LOLP(R), \qquad LOLP(R) = \begin{cases} \Phi\left(\dfrac{X_{\min} - R}{\sigma_R}\right) & R > X_{\min} \\ 1 & R \le X_{\min} \end{cases} \] where \(R\) is available operating reserve, \(\lambda\) is the system lambda (marginal energy price), \(\sigma_R\) is net load and generator trip forecast uncertainty over the dispatch horizon, and \(\Phi(\cdot)\) is the standard normal cumulative distribution. As reserves dwindle the adder escalates smoothly, and once reserves fall to \(X_{\min}\) the price reaches the full Value of Lost Load (\(VOLL = \$5{,}000\)/MWh). Stoft ch.2-6 Outside the texts

Dated: ERCOT's ORDC

ERCOT ran the ORDC as a separate real-time price adder from 2014. With real-time co-optimisation (RTC+B), launched in December 2025, energy and ancillary services clear together against ancillary-service demand curves, and the standalone ORDC adder was retired. ERCOT's VOLL and minimum contingency level have also changed over time, so the inputs below are illustrative. Outside the texts Check ERCOT's current protocols and PUCT rules.

Worked example 3b · Hogan ORDC scarcity adder in real-time dispatchChecked by code

Illustrative ERCOT-style conditions: system lambda \(\lambda = \$60\)/MWh, \(VOLL = \$5{,}000\)/MWh, minimum contingency level \(X_{\min} = 2{,}000\) MW, and 1-hour net load uncertainty \(\sigma_R = 500\) MW. Available operating reserves tighten to \(R = 2{,}500\) MW:

reserve normalized z-score = (2,000 − 2,500) / 500 = −1.00 loss-of-load probability LOLP = Φ(−1.00) = 15.87% ORDC real-time scarcity adder = (5,000 − 60) × 15.87% = $783.76/MWh total all-in real-time clearing price = 60 + 783.76 = $843.76/MWh

The ORDC automatically injects a $783.76/MWh scarcity premium into every node's price, compensating generators and demand-response providers for grid reliability before blackouts occur. Stoft ch.2-6 Outside the texts

Worked example 3 · missing money and a capacity priceChecked by code

With a $1,000 cap, expect 20 scarcity hours a year, plus $8k/MW-yr of other energy and ancillary margins.

scarcity revenue = (1,000 − 92.5) × 20 = $18.15k/MW-yr missing money = 76.6 − 18.15 − 8.0 = $50.5k/MW-yr ≈ $138/MW-day: the capacity price needed to keep the peaker

This is essentially "net cost of new entry": fixed cost less expected energy and ancillary margins. It is the anchor for administratively set capacity demand curves. Stoft ch.2-8 Outside the texts

ALM analogy

A peaker is a deep out-of-the-money call on scarcity. Its value comes from rare, extreme events, so its revenue distribution is highly skewed. Capacity payments convert that skewed payoff into a steady annuity, a premium paid for tail protection. Reliability options make the insurance explicit: consumers buy a call and generators sell it. Synthesis

Dated: scarcity and capacity designs

The texts predate most current designs: PJM's RPM and its later reforms (capacity performance, then sloped demand curves and accreditation by effective load-carrying capability), the GB Capacity Market (from 2014), ERCOT's operating-reserve demand curve (2014) and its post-2021 changes, the EU's acceptance of capacity mechanisms under state-aid rules, and Ireland's and Italy's reliability options. Price caps and VOLL estimates have also changed. Outside the texts Check ISO capacity-market manuals, regulator VOLL studies and ACER's security-of-supply reports.

Going deeper: risk changes the answer

Stoft's equivalence of price spikes and capacity requirements is deterministic. With randomness, the shape of the profit function matters. A spike-based regime makes peaker profits steep and volatile in installed capacity. Risk-averse investors then demand a premium or build less, and market power is rewarded. A capacity requirement with a penalty gives a step-shaped profit function, lower risk, and makes reliability easier to control. Stoft ch.2-7, ch.2-8 For a structurer this is the key point: capacity revenue is a regulated, auction-set, often multi-year stream that lenders will finance (Course 9), while scarcity rent is an option most lenders will not. Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • Fixed costs are recovered from inframarginal and scarcity rents. Peakers depend on a few high-price hours.
  • With VOLL pricing, optimal shortage hours equal the peaker's fixed cost divided by (VOLL − VC).
  • Caps create missing money. Capacity payments, markets and reliability options replace volatile spikes with steadier revenue.
  • Net cost of new entry (fixed cost minus energy and ancillary margins) anchors capacity prices.
