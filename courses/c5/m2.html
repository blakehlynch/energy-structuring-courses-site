Prerequisites

Fixed and variable costs

Every generator has a fixed cost: capital recovered over its life plus fixed O&M, expressed in $ per MW per year. It also has a variable cost per MWh: heat rate × fuel price + variable O&M + carbon. Stoft converts overnight cost to an annual fixed cost with the amortisation (capital recovery) formula and stresses that the units ($/kW-yr) are really dollars per unit of energy-time. Stoft ch.1-3 Murray's cost-chain chapters build the same components up for UK plant, including how utilisation drives average cost. Murray ch.3

\[ FC = OC\cdot\frac{r}{1-(1+r)^{-T}} + FOM, \qquad VC = HR\times P_{fuel} + VOM + e_{CO_2}\times P_{CO_2} \]
Worked example 1 · three technologiesChecked by code

Illustrative inputs at an 8% cost of capital: a 30-year capital recovery factor of 0.0888; gas at $6/MMBtu; carbon at $50/t.

combustion turbine (peaker): FC $76.6k/MW-yr, VC $92.50/MWh combined-cycle gas (CCGT): FC $112.7k/MW-yr, VC $59.50/MWh (without carbon: $42.00) nuclear (baseload): FC $514.0k/MW-yr, VC $9.28/MWh

The pattern is what matters: as you move from peaker to baseload, fixed cost rises and variable cost falls. Stoft's own table shows the same ordering from 2001 US data. Stoft ch.1-3 Synthesis

Screening curves and the optimal mix

A screening curve plots the annual cost of one MW of a technology against its capacity factor: a straight line starting at FC with slope VC × 8,760. Where two lines cross is the capacity factor above which the higher-fixed-cost technology is cheaper. Map those crossing points onto the load-duration curve and you can read off the least-cost amount of each technology. Stoft ch.1-4, ch.2-2 Stoft adds two cautions. In a market, price shapes the load-duration curve, so screening curves can confirm an equilibrium but not predict one. And the optimal amount of peaking capacity depends on what consumers would pay to avoid curtailment (Module 5). Stoft ch.1-4, ch.2-2

Worked example 2 · breakeven capacity factorsChecked by code
CCGT vs peaker: CF* = (112.7 − 76.6)k / ((92.50 − 59.50) × 8,760) = 12.5% ≈ 1,094 hours nuclear vs CCGT: CF* = 91.2%; without the carbon price it would be 140%, i.e. never

Loads lasting fewer than about 1,100 hours a year are cheapest to serve with peakers. Nuclear earns a place only for near-constant load, and only because carbon is priced. Policy and fuel prices move these boundaries, which is why technology mixes shift with gas and carbon prices. Synthesis Stoft ch.1-3

Reading the breakeven durations on the load-duration curve from Module 1 (peak 13,976 MW):

This is the regulated planner's answer. Stoft shows that a competitive market with scarcity pricing reaches the same mix of baseload and mid-merit plant, but less peaking capacity, because demand would be curtailed in the very few hours when serving it costs more than it is worth. Stoft ch.2-2

The merit order and uniform pricing

In the short run, fixed costs are sunk. Plants are dispatched in order of variable cost: the merit order or generation stack. The marginal plant sets the price for everyone. Edwards ch.4.2 Kirschen & Strbac §3.3.2 Kirschen and Strbac explain why markets pay this uniform clearing price rather than each plant's bid. Under pay-as-bid, every generator would bid its guess of the clearing price instead of its cost. Some low-cost units would guess too high and be displaced by dearer ones, and risk premia would be added, so average prices would not fall and dispatch would be less efficient. Kirschen & Strbac §3.3.2 Because short-run supply is flat for most of the stack and steep at the top, small changes in demand or availability near the peak move prices a great deal. Edwards ch.4.2 Kirschen & Strbac §7.3

Worked example 4 · clearing a stackChecked by code

Offers on a cheap-gas day (illustrative): nuclear 3,000 MW at $9, CCGT 4,000 at $26, coal 2,500 at $28, CT 1,500 at $42 and oil 500 at $95. Demand is 10,500 MW.

cumulative: 3,000 → 7,000 → 9,500 → 11,000 ≥ 10,500 → CT is marginal → price $42/MWh uniform-price payments $441k/h vs $243k/h if (hypothetically) everyone bid cost and was paid as bid

The difference is the inframarginal rent that pays fixed costs. Stoft's "fixed-cost fallacy" is the mistaken belief that marginal-cost pricing cannot cover fixed costs. Rents earned below the clearing price, together with scarcity hours, do cover them in equilibrium. Stoft ch.2-2

ALM analogy

Screening curves are a fixed-versus-variable cost trade-off, like choosing between a large fixed fee and a high usage rate. The breakeven is the utilisation at which fixed pays off. Capital-intensive technologies are long-duration assets whose economics swing with the discount rate, so the mix is partly an interest-rate bet. Synthesis

Dated: costs and fuel prices

Stoft's 2001 cost table and Murray's 2009 UK costs are far out of date. Since then, solar, wind and battery costs have fallen dramatically, gas prices have swung (the 2022 European spike), nuclear projects have run over budget, and carbon prices have risen in Europe. Outside the texts Use current sources: the EIA's Annual Energy Outlook capital cost assumptions, Lazard's LCOE and LCOS reports, IEA/NEA Projected Costs of Generating Electricity, and ISO marginal-unit reports.

Going deeper: screening curves and renewables

Screening curves assume a dispatchable plant chooses its capacity factor. Wind and solar can't: their output follows the weather. They have near-zero variable cost and their own "capacity factor" fixed by resource. The right comparison is therefore the value of the energy they produce, at the prices in the hours they produce it, against their fixed cost. Kirschen & Strbac §4.5.3, §7.4 Synthesis As their share grows, they lower prices in their own production hours (Module 6). Combined with demand, they create a net-load duration curve that the dispatchable fleet must serve, which is steeper and with more peaking and flexibility needs. The screening logic still works, applied to net load. Synthesis Outside the texts

Key takeaways

  • Annualise capital with the capital recovery factor, and build variable cost from heat rate, fuel, O&M and carbon.
  • Screening curves give breakeven capacity factors. Mapped onto the load-duration curve, they give the least-cost mix.
  • Uniform-price merit-order dispatch pays inframarginal rents that fund fixed costs.
  • Gas, carbon and capital costs move the technology boundaries, so the mix is a policy and rate exposure.
