Prerequisites

  • Module 3.

How a transmission limit splits a market

Kirschen and Strbac's Borduria–Syldavia example shows the core idea. Two markets with different supply costs, joined by an unconstrained line, become one market with one price: the cheap region exports until marginal costs equalise. If the line's capacity is below that flow, the cheap region's price stays lower and the dear region's higher. The single market splits into two, and each extra MW must be served locally. This is locational marginal pricing; when every bus has its own price it is nodal pricing. Kirschen & Strbac §5.3.1 Stoft argues that congestion pricing is simply competitive pricing with transmission scarcity, not a tax. Stoft ch.5-3

Worked example 1 · a two-zone marketChecked by code

Zone A (exporter): supply π = 12 + 0.004·P, demand 3,000 MW. Zone B: π = 20 + 0.006·P, demand 5,000 MW. All figures are illustrative.

unconstrained: one price $34.40, with 2,600 MW flowing A → B line limited to 1,000 MW: πA = $28.00, πB = $44.00 consumers pay $304,000/h; generators receive $288,000/h; congestion rent = (44 − 28) × 1,000 = $16,000/h

Congestion helps zone A's consumers (cheaper) and zone B's generators (shielded from competition), and hurts the other two groups. The difference between what consumers pay and generators receive is the merchandising (congestion) surplus, collected by the market operator. Kirschen & Strbac §5.3.1.3

Nodal prices in a meshed network

In a real meshed network, power follows physics (Kirchhoff's laws), not contract paths. Kirschen and Strbac's three-bus example shows the consequences. Kirschen & Strbac §5.3.2

  • With m binding constraints there are m + 1 marginal generators. Each sets the price at its own bus, and other buses' prices are weighted combinations of those, fixed by the network's sensitivities.
  • A bus with no marginal generator can have a price above any generator's cost. In their example, bus 2's price is 1.5 × one generator's cost minus 0.5 × another's.
  • Power can flow from a high-price node to a low-price node on some lines, which looks irrational but is forced by physics.
  • Raising a line's limit can raise some prices and generator profits, because the marginal units change.

Prices are computed by the operator's optimisation as Lagrange multipliers, in practice usually on a DC (linearised) network model. Kirschen & Strbac §5.3.2–5.3.4 Stoft ch.5-4 Stoft also covers loss pricing and the case that competitive locational prices aren't "too high". Stoft ch.5-5, ch.5-7

Power Transfer Distribution Factors (PTDF) & LMP Decomposition

In meshed networks, power flows according to Kirchhoff's Laws rather than commercial contracts. A Power Transfer Distribution Factor (PTDF) \(PTDF_{l,i}\) defines the fraction of power injected at bus \(i\) (with reference withdrawal at the slack bus) that flows over line \(l\). Under DC optimal power flow (DC-OPF), every nodal price decomposes into three distinct economic components: \[ LMP_i = \lambda_{ref} + LMP_i^{loss} + LMP_i^{cong} = \lambda_{ref} + LMP_i^{loss} - \sum_{l} PTDF_{l, i} \cdot \mu_l \] where \(\lambda_{ref}\) is the energy reference price at the slack bus, and \(\mu_l \ge 0\) is the shadow price (Lagrange multiplier) on transmission line \(l\)'s thermal rating. Kirschen & Strbac §5.2.2 Outside the texts

Worked example 1b · 3-bus PTDF transmission congestion and LMP divergenceChecked by code

Consider a 3-bus triangle (Buses A, B, C with equal reactance \(X\) across all three lines; Bus C is the reference slack bus at $50/MWh). When power is injected at A and withdrawn at C, \(2/3\) flows directly on line A-C and \(1/3\) loops through line A-B and B-C (\(PTDF_{AB,A} = +1/3\)). For an injection at B, reverse loop flow yields \(PTDF_{AB,B} = -1/3\). If line A-B binds with a shadow price \(\mu_{AB} = \$60\)/MWh (losses ignored):

LMP at Bus A (exporting, depressed) = 50 − (+1/3) × 60 = $30.0/MWh LMP at Bus B (importing, elevated) = 50 − (−1/3) × 60 = $70.0/MWh LMP at Bus C (slack bus reference) = 50 − 0 × 60 = $50.0/MWh locational price spread (LMP_B − LMP_A) = $40.0/MWh

Congestion creates a $40/MWh locational wedge ($70 − $30) between buses B and A, exactly equal to \((PTDF_{AB,A} - PTDF_{AB,B}) \times \mu_{AB} = (1/3 - (-1/3)) \times 60 = 40\). Generators at Bus A are curtailed and face price depression, while Bus B customers pay a severe congestion premium. Kirschen & Strbac §5.2.2 Outside the texts

Why contracts break and how FTRs fix them

A CfD between a generator in A and a load in B fails under congestion. The generator is paid πA, the load pays πB, and each expects the other to cover the gap against the strike. Kirschen and Strbac show that the total shortfall equals F × (πB − πA), exactly the congestion surplus on that flow. A financial transmission right (FTR) from A to B pays its holder F × (πB − πA) out of the congestion surplus, and so perfectly hedges the locational spread. Kirschen & Strbac §5.3.5

Worked example 2 · hedging a cross-zone contractChecked by code

A load in B has a 400 MW CfD at $30, referenced to zone B's price, with a generator in A. It also holds 400 MW of A→B FTRs. Prices are as in example 1.

the load pays the pool 400 × 44 = $17,600; the CfD pays the load (44 − 30) × 400 = $5,600 (shown as $−5,600 from the generator's side) the generator receives the pool 400 × 28 = $11,200 and pays $5,600 under the CfD → nets $5,600 = $14/MWh: the generator holds the A–B spread an A→B FTR of 400 MW pays (44 − 28) × 400 = $6,400, restoring the generator to $12,000 = $30/MWh the load's net cost: 17,600 − 5,600 = $12,000 = $30/MWh

Whoever bears the spread needs the FTR. With it, both sides effectively trade at $30 wherever they are. Kirschen's point holds: contract for energy and for the network's ability to deliver it. Kirschen & Strbac §5.3.5 Synthesis

  • Point-to-point FTRs are defined between any two nodes, independent of the path. The operator should sell only a simultaneously feasible set so that the congestion surplus covers payouts (revenue adequacy). If the network is degraded, FTRs can be underfunded. Kirschen & Strbac §5.3.5.3
  • Obligations versus options. An FTR obligation pays negative amounts if the spread reverses. FTR options pay only when positive. Flowgate rights are tied to a line's shadow price, but markets have adopted point-to-point rights. Kirschen & Strbac §5.3.5.3–5.3.5.4
  • FTRs are usually auctioned, and a bidder should pay roughly the expected spread. Kirschen & Strbac §5.3.5.2
ALM analogy

Locational basis is like a cross-currency or cross-index basis: a hedge in one location (or currency) against exposure in another leaves a spread. An FTR is the basis swap, funded here from a pool (the congestion surplus) rather than by a counterparty. Revenue adequacy is the pool's solvency. Synthesis

Dated: zonal versus nodal

US ISOs use nodal pricing with FTRs (called CRRs in some markets). Europe uses large bidding zones with flow-based market coupling and long-term transmission rights, and has debated splitting zones. GB has debated locational pricing in its market reform review. FTR auction designs and underfunding rules have also changed since the texts. Outside the texts Check ISO FTR/CRR manuals, ACER bidding-zone reviews and Ofgem/DESNZ REMA documents.

Going deeper: the spread as an option on network scarcity

Congestion rent is zero most of the time and large when the line binds. An FTR's value is therefore the expected value of a spread that is truncated at zero for the binding direction. It is driven by load and renewable scenarios, outages and fuel prices in each zone. In the widget, the spread appears only once A's demand is low or B's high enough to bind the line. Synthesis Kirschen and Strbac's chapter on investing in transmission values a line by the arbitrage it enables, the congestion rent it removes over its life, which links FTR prices to transmission planning. Kirschen & Strbac §8.4 For a structurer, a generator hedged at a hub but located at a node is short this option. Its value at risk is the tail of the nodal-to-hub spread, not its average. Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • Transmission limits split markets: importers see higher prices and exporters lower ones. The difference times the flow is the congestion surplus.
  • In meshed networks, nodal prices come from physics, and counterintuitive flows and prices are normal.
  • CfDs across congested locations don't settle cleanly. FTRs pay the locational spread from the congestion surplus.
  • FTR obligations can pay negative amounts. Revenue adequacy requires selling only feasible rights.
