Day-ahead, real-time and two-settlement

Foundation≈ 60 min9 quiz questions8 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Describe the sequence from bilateral contracts to real-time balancing.
  • Explain complex bids, unit commitment and uplift.
  • Settle positions under a two-settlement system.
  • Use CfDs and explain renewables' imbalance costs.

Prerequisites

  • Modules 1–2.

A sequence of markets

Electricity trading runs through forward markets whose trading periods get shorter and shorter, ending in a spot market run by the system operator. Long-term bilateral contracts cover large volumes for months or years. Standard OTC products and exchanges refine positions. The spot (real-time, balancing) market covers residual imbalances in each trading period, from an hour down to five minutes. Kirschen & Strbac §3.2–3.4 Murray describes the UK version: bilateral contracts, exchanges for day-ahead adjustment, and a balancing mechanism through which the system operator buys and sells to balance in real time. Murray ch.1–2

  • Decentralised (bilateral) trading. Each deal sets its own price and there is no official price, though reporting services publish OTC summaries. Kirschen & Strbac §3.3.1
  • Centralised (pool) day-ahead markets. Generators submit complex bids (incremental cost, start-up cost, minimum output, ramp rates, minimum up and down times). The operator runs a unit-commitment optimisation for all of tomorrow's periods at once, and the prices are the marginal costs of the marginal units. Kirschen & Strbac §3.3.2 Stoft compares four day-ahead designs and the long controversy over whether the day-ahead market should be a power exchange or an ISO-run optimisation. Stoft ch.3-3, ch.3-7

When marginal prices don't cover start-up costs

Because prices are set by marginal cost and exclude no-load and start-up costs, a unit can be scheduled and still lose money. Kirschen and Strbac describe the fixes: uplift (make-whole) payments charged to consumers, compensation for "profit sub-optimality" relative to self-scheduling, and convex-hull pricing, which raises prices to minimise uplift. Uplift is controversial because it is an out-of-market payment that hides costs from the price. Kirschen & Strbac §3.3.2.5

Worked example 1 · a make-whole paymentChecked by code

A unit with a $600/h no-load cost and a $35/MWh marginal cost is committed to run at 100 MW in an hour when it is the marginal unit and the price is $35.

revenue 100 × 35 = $3,500; cost 600 + 100 × 35 = $4,100 → uplift needed = $600

The schedule is efficient (the unit was needed), but the energy price alone doesn't pay for its commitment. Kirschen & Strbac §3.3.2.5

Mixed-integer non-convexities & Convex Hull Pricing (ELMP)

In power systems, thermal generators have binary commitment variables (\(u_{i,t} \in \{0,1\}\)), minimum run capacities (\(P_{\min}\)), and lumpy start-up costs. In linear programming, the shadow price on demand (\(\lambda\)) supports a competitive equilibrium without uplift. But in mixed-integer linear programming (MILP), non-convexities create a duality gap: uniform marginal prices cannot simultaneously clear the market and guarantee zero financial loss for every committed unit. Under Convex Hull Pricing, the ISO prices against the convex envelope of the generators' cost functions, which yields the uniform prices that minimise total uplift (make-whole plus lost-opportunity payments). MISO's Extended LMP (ELMP, 2015) is a practical approximation; PJM and other ISOs adopted narrower fast-start pricing reforms rather than full convex hull pricing. Kirschen & Strbac §3.3.2.5 Outside the texts

Worked example 1b · multi-hour commitment and start-up amortizationChecked by code

A 100 MW peaker is committed for a 4-hour evening peak: start-up cost $2,000, no-load cost $500/h, and marginal cost $35/MWh. The system clearing price averages $35/MWh over the 4 hours:

total commitment cost = 2,000 + (500 + 35 × 100) × 4 = $18,000 market energy revenue = 35 × 100 × 4 = $14,000 make-whole uplift payment (bid-production cost shortfall) = $4,000 effective uplift shortfall per MWh delivered = $10.00/MWh

Without an uplift payment of $4,000 ($10/MWh), the peaker would refuse to start. Convex hull pricing incorporates start-up and no-load costs into the energy price clearing engine, allowing prices to rise toward $45/MWh to internalize commitment costs into market signals. Kirschen & Strbac §3.3.2.5 Outside the texts

The managed spot market and gate closure

The last-resort market is not a true spot market. The system operator is counterparty to every balancing trade, chooses which bids and offers to use, and settles imbalances at the spot price. A generator that delivers 97 MW against a 100 MW contract is deemed to have bought 3 MW at spot. Kirschen & Strbac §3.4 Balancing resources include generators raising or lowering output, consumers reducing load, and longer-term option-like reserve contracts (Module 6). Kirschen & Strbac §3.4.1 Gate closure trades off the operator's need for time against participants' wish to trade as close to real time as possible, because forecasts, especially for wind and solar, improve sharply in the last hours. Kirschen & Strbac §3.4.2

The two-settlement system

In centralised markets, quantities scheduled day-ahead are settled at the day-ahead price, and deviations from the schedule are settled at the real-time price for each interval. Kirschen & Strbac §3.5 Stoft ch.3-2 The day-ahead position is therefore a financially binding forward. Real-time only prices the difference.

\[ \text{Revenue} = Q_{DA}\,P_{DA} + (Q_{actual}-Q_{DA})\,P_{RT} \]
Worked example 2 · a wind farm's settlementChecked by code

A wind farm sells its 80 MWh forecast day-ahead at $40. The wind drops and it produces 65 MWh; the real-time price is $70, because the system is short too. Numbers are illustrative.

day-ahead: 80 × 40 = $3,200; real-time: (65 − 80) × 70 = $−1,050 → total $2,150 = $33.08/MWh realised opposite case: produces 95 MWh when real-time is $15 (system long) → total $3,425 = $36.05/MWh

Either way the realised price falls below the $40 day-ahead price, because wind's forecast errors tend to line up with the system's (short when everyone is short). Kirschen and Strbac list the remedies: better forecasts, intraday trading as forecasts improve, and pairing with flexible plant or storage. Kirschen & Strbac §4.5.3 Synthesis

How day-ahead and real-time prices relate

The real-time market disciplines forward prices. If real-time prices tend to be low, buyers under-buy forward and push forward prices down. If real-time is prone to spikes, they over-buy, pushing forward prices up. Virtual bids (financial buy-day-ahead, sell-real-time positions by parties without assets) help the two converge and may reduce market power. Kirschen & Strbac §3.4.4 Kirschen and Strbac add that real-time spikes partly reflect the cost of the small, flexible slice of energy delivered at short notice. Fear of those spikes can inflate forward prices. Kirschen & Strbac §3.4.4 Stoft records traders' complaint about ex-post pricing, where real-time prices are recomputed after the fact. Stoft ch.3-2

Hedging with contracts for difference

Participants in a centralised market must buy and sell physically through the pool, but they hedge with bilateral financial contracts. A contract for difference pays (strike − spot) × quantity, so a generator and a load each settle at the pool and then exchange the difference, locking in the strike. Kirschen & Strbac §3.3.3, §5.3.5

Worked example 3 · settling a CfDChecked by code

A generator has a 100 MWh CfD at a $45 strike with a retailer. The pool price is $62.

CfD payment to the generator = (45 − 62) × 100 = $−1,700, i.e. the generator pays the retailer $1,700 generator: pool revenue 6,200 − 1,700 = $4,500 = $45/MWh; retailer: pool cost 6,200 − 1,700 = $4,500

Both lock $45 regardless of the pool, provided both settle at the same price. Module 4 shows what goes wrong when they settle at different nodes. Kirschen & Strbac §5.3.5

ALM analogy

Day-ahead is the term trade and real-time the overnight rate on the residual. A two-settlement book is a forward position plus a funding gap marked at the spot rate. Shape and volume risk, from not knowing exactly what you'll produce or consume, is the analogue of prepayment or behavioural risk on a hedged book. Synthesis

Dated: market rules

Details differ by market and change often: settlement interval (15-minute in Europe since 2025, 5-minute in Australia and most US ISOs), intraday continuous trading and auctions in Europe, US scarcity pricing and fast-start pricing reforms, and uplift allocation rules. Outside the texts Check each market's current manuals (PJM, ERCOT, CAISO, EPEX/Nord Pool, Elexon for GB) before relying on specifics.

Going deeper: why intermittent sellers capture less than the average price

Write realised price = PDA + (Qact − QDA)(PRT − PDA)/Qact. Its expected shortfall against PDA is driven by the covariance between the producer's forecast error and the real-time spread. When a whole fleet of wind farms is short together, the system is short, so PRT > PDA exactly when Qact < QDA. That systematic correlation is a cost no amount of averaging removes. It is the same algebra as Course 1's convenience yield and Course 7's volume-price correlation in swing contracts. Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • Trading runs from long-term contracts through day-ahead and intraday markets to a managed real-time balancing market.
  • Centralised day-ahead markets clear complex bids by unit commitment. Uplift covers costs the marginal price misses.
  • Two-settlement: the day-ahead quantity is paid at the day-ahead price, and deviations at the real-time price.
  • CfDs lock prices only when both sides settle at the same location. Forecast errors correlated with the system cost renewables money.
