Prerequisites

  • Modules 2 and 4.

What market power is, and why power markets are prone to it

Stoft defines market power as the ability to alter the market price profitably away from the competitive level. A supplier exercises it by withholding: physically, by not offering capacity, or economically, by offering it above marginal cost. Stoft ch.4-1, ch.4-2 Electricity is unusually vulnerable for three reasons. Short-run demand barely responds to price. Output can't be stored to discipline prices over time. And transmission constraints create local markets in which a few plants are essential (Module 4). Stoft ch.4-3 Kirschen & Strbac §2.6, §5.3.1 Stoft notes that a monopolist facing a demand elasticity below 1 would raise prices without limit. Power demand is far below 1 in the short run, so only regulation, demand response or rivals constrain it. Stoft ch.4-3

Modelling markups

  • Monopoly. The Lerner index L = (P − MC)/P = 1/e, where e is the demand elasticity. Stoft ch.4-3
  • Cournot oligopoly. Each supplier's markup is Li = si/e, its market share over elasticity. The share-weighted average markup equals HHI / e, where HHI is the sum of squared shares. Stoft ch.4-3
  • Stoft warns that HHI and Lerner estimates are unreliable predictors on their own. Elasticity, the style of competition, transmission and contracts matter as much as concentration. Stoft ch.4-5
Worked example 1 · concentration and markupsChecked by code

Five suppliers with shares 35/25/20/10/10%; effective demand elasticity 0.5 (illustrative).

HHI = 0.35² + 0.25² + 0.2² + 0.1² + 0.1² = 2,450 (on the 10,000 scale) average Cournot markup = 0.245 / 0.5 = 0.49; the largest firm's L = 0.35/0.5 = 0.70 → price = MC/(1 − L) = 30/0.3 = $100 on a $30 cost

With demand this inelastic, even a moderately concentrated market yields huge markups. That is why mitigation, contracts and demand response matter so much. Stoft ch.4-3

Withholding on a supply stack

Withholding pays when the price rise on the firm's remaining output outweighs the margin lost on the withheld MW. A firm is pivotal when demand can't be met without it, and then it can set almost any price. Stoft's distinction between marginal and non-marginal generators shows that a large owner of cheap baseload gains most from a price rise it engineers with a few MW of dearer plant. Stoft ch.4-2

\[ \Delta\Pi = (P_1-P_0)\,(K-w-F) \;-\; w\,(P_0-MC) \]
Worked example 2 · is withholding profitable?Checked by code

Using Module 2's stack (demand 10,500 MW, price $42 set by CTs), a firm owns 3,000 MW of CCGT (MC $26). If it withholds 600 MW, CT capacity runs out and oil sets the price at $95.

no contracts: ΔΠ = (95 − 42) × 2,400 − 600 × (42 − 26) = +$117.6k per hour 2,500 MW sold forward: ΔΠ = (95 − 42) × (3,000 − 600 − 2,500) − 9,600 = $−14.9k: withholding no longer pays

Forward contracts are among the most powerful mitigants. A firm that has sold its output forward gains little from a higher spot price. Stoft ch.4-4 Synthesis

Withholding and forward contracts

Does withholding pay?

Stack: nuclear 3,000 at $9, CCGT 4,000 at $26 (the firm's capacity is part of this block), coal 2,500 at $28, CT 1,500 at $42, oil 500 at $95. Demand is perfectly inelastic. The firm is paid the clearing price only on uncontracted output.

Monitoring and mitigation

Stoft's design chapter lists what keeps prices down: demand elasticity, the number and size of suppliers, forward contracts, and bidding rules. Stoft ch.4-4 He then describes the US practice of market monitoring and the ambiguity of FERC's standards. Stoft ch.4-6 Kirschen and Strbac note that congestion can shield local generators and that a larger interconnected market is more competitive. Kirschen & Strbac §5.3.1 Common tools today, not detailed in the texts, include pivotal-supplier and residual-supply tests, offer caps for local market power, must-offer rules, and independent market monitors. Outside the texts

Worked example 3 · a residual supply indexChecked by code

Total offered supply 11,500 MW; the firm controls 3,000 MW; demand 10,500 MW.

RSI = (11,500 − 3,000) / 10,500 = 0.81 < 1 → the firm is pivotal: demand cannot be met without it

The RSI is a widely used screen (for example in California and PJM), not a measure from the course texts. Outside the texts Note how it complements the HHI: a market with a modest HHI can still have pivotal suppliers in peak hours.

ALM analogy

Withholding is like a dominant holder squeezing a thinly traded instrument: control enough of the deliverable supply when demand is inelastic and the price is yours. Forward sales are the natural defence: once you've sold your position forward, squeezing the spot market hurts you. Regulators monitor concentration in peak hours the way exchanges monitor positions near expiry. Synthesis

Dated: market power cases and rules

Stoft's analysis drew heavily on the California crisis of 2000–01. Since then: FERC market-based-rate tests, local market-power mitigation in all US ISOs, REMIT in the EU (2011) with ACER surveillance, GB's Transmission Constraint Licence Condition, and enforcement cases on manipulation in several markets. Outside the texts Check FERC, ACER/REMIT, Ofgem and independent market monitor reports.

Going deeper: forward contracts and competition (Allaz & Vila)

In a Cournot-style model, a firm's incentive to raise price depends on its uncontracted output, not its total output. Long-term contracts therefore make spot markets more competitive. A firm that has sold most of its output forward behaves almost like a price-taker. Stoft ch.4-4, ch.4-5 This was formalized by Allaz & Vila (1993): in a two-stage game where producers can trade forward contracts before competing in the spot market, forward trading creates a prisoner's dilemma. Each producer commits forward to steal spot market share from its rival. In subgame-perfect equilibrium, both firms contract forward, spot output rises, price falls, and industry profit is eroded toward competitive levels as trading opportunities multiply. Outside the texts Synthesis

Worked example 4 · the Allaz–Vila forward contracting equilibriumChecked by code

Two symmetric power producers face linear market demand \(P(Q) = 100 - 0.01\,Q\) with constant marginal cost \(c = \$20\)/MWh (\(a - c = 80\)):

pure Cournot (no forward market): output per firm = (100 − 20) / (3 × 0.01) = 2,666.7 MW (total = 5,333.3 MW) market spot clearing price = $46.67/MWh operating profit per firm = $71.11k/h Allaz–Vila duopoly (1 stage of forward trading before spot): forward sales per firm f = (100 − 20) / (5 × 0.01) = 1,600.0 MW total output per firm q = 2 × 80 / (5 × 0.01) = 3,200.0 MW (total = 6,400.0 MW) clearing price P = 100 − 0.01 × 6,400 = $36.00/MWh operating profit per firm (P − c) × q = $51.20k/h

In the spot stage each firm's best response is \(q_i = [(a-c)/b + 2f_i - f_j]/3\): every MW sold forward makes it more aggressive in the spot market. Each firm therefore sells forward to win share, and in equilibrium \(f = (a-c)/5b\) and \(q = 2(a-c)/5b\). Forward contracting expands output by 20% and cuts the market price from $46.67 to $36.00/MWh, reducing each firm's profit by 28%. Liquid forward power markets are thus an organic market mitigation mechanism that disciplines spot market power without regulatory intervention. Outside the texts Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • Inelastic demand, no storage and transmission limits make power markets prone to market power.
  • Lerner = 1/e for a monopoly and s/e for a Cournot supplier. Average markup = HHI/e.
  • Withholding pays when the price gain on remaining uncontracted output exceeds the margin lost. Forward contracts blunt it.
  • Monitors use pivotal-supplier and residual-supply tests, offer caps and must-offer rules, not concentration alone.
