Why a MWh is not a barrel

Kirschen and Strbac open their chapter on energy markets with this question. Power is a commodity, but one tied to a physical system that must balance supply and demand second by second. Flows from all generators are pooled, so one plant's output cannot be steered to one customer. A system collapse stops everyone, not just the parties to a failed trade. Kirschen & Strbac §3.1 Three consequences shape every market design:

  • No meaningful storage at scale (historically). Production must equal consumption at every instant, so prices vary sharply through the day as cheaper plants run out and dearer ones start. Kirschen & Strbac §3.1 Stoft ch.1-4
  • Balance shows up in frequency, not in visible inventories. If generation falls short, frequency drops (motors slow) and voltage sags. Within an AC interconnection frequency is the same everywhere, so operators locate imbalances by tracking scheduled versus actual flows between control areas. Stoft ch.1-4
  • Demand barely responds to price in the short run, because most customers don't see real-time prices. A single accountable operator must therefore be responsible for real-time balance "at practically any cost". Kirschen & Strbac §3.1 Stoft ch.1-4
ALM analogy

Electricity behaves like an intraday liquidity position that must net to zero continuously, with the system operator as lender and borrower of last resort. Any shortfall is cleared by the operator at the real-time (penalty) rate. Forward contracts are the term funding; the balancing market is the overnight facility. Synthesis

Power, energy and capacity

Stoft insists on careful units. Power (MW) is a rate. Energy (MWh) is power times time. Capacity is the maximum power a generator can deliver. Its cost is a fixed cost per MW per year, which can also be expressed per MWh of capacity-time. Stoft ch.1-3 A plant's capacity factor is its energy output divided by what it would produce running flat out all year. Stoft ch.2-2

a 500 MW plant producing 1.2 TWh in a year: CF = 1,200,000 / (500 × 8,760) = 27.4% a 300 MW wind farm at a 35% capacity factor: 300 × 8,760 × 0.35 = 919.8 GWh a year

Capacity factor drives the economics of every technology choice in Module 2: high-fixed-cost plants need high capacity factors. Stoft ch.1-3

The load-duration curve

Sort a year's 8,760 hourly loads from highest to lowest and you have the load-duration curve. It shows how many hours load is at or above any level, which can also be read as the probability that load exceeds that level in a random hour. It loses the time sequence but answers the key planning question: how much capacity is needed, and how often each slice of it runs. Stoft ch.1-4 Peakers serve the thin top slice and may run only 1% of hours. Baseload plants serve the thick bottom. If customers faced real-time prices, the peak would be shaved flat and less capacity would be needed. Stoft ch.1-4 Murray ch.3

The top ~9% of capacity is needed in only 10% of hours. Paying for it is the "peaker problem" behind scarcity pricing and capacity markets (Module 5). Stoft ch.1-4, ch.2-2 Synthesis

Industry structures

Kirschen and Strbac trace a progression. Kirschen & Strbac §1.2–1.3

  • Vertically integrated monopoly under a "regulatory compact".
  • Independent power producers selling to the incumbent.
  • Wholesale competition among generators. Retailers buy for their customers, and an independent system operator (ISO) runs the market and the grid. Alternatively, a transmission system operator (TSO) balances while separate exchanges host trading.
  • Retail competition over regulated monopoly wires.
  • Distributed resources joined through aggregators.

Murray compares the models used around the world: a gross pool; a "multi-market" of bilateral trading plus exchanges and a balancing market (the common European model); a single buyer contracting through PPAs (often suited to developing systems that need investment); zonal power boards; open IPP access; and the state utility. Each scores differently on competition, coordination, investment and security of supply. Murray ch.1, Table 1.1

  • Gencos sell energy and services.
  • Retailers buy wholesale and sell to consumers.
  • Wires businesses remain regulated monopolies.
  • The system operator keeps the lights on and often runs the market.
  • Regulators oversee prices, entry and market power.

Kirschen & Strbac §1.3

Dated: market designs keep moving

Stoft (2002) describes US markets as they were being built; Murray (2009) the UK's NETA/BETTA arrangements; Willis (2005) a utility perspective; Kirschen and Strbac (2019) are the most current. Since then: European market coupling under the EU target model, five-minute settlement in Australia (2021), FERC Order 2222 opening US markets to aggregated distributed resources, rapid growth in batteries, the ERCOT failure in Winter Storm Uri (2021), the 2021–22 European price crisis and its emergency measures, and the UK's market reform review (REMA). All are outside the texts. Outside the texts Check ISO and RTO market manuals, ACER and Ofgem publications, FERC orders and the EIA.

Going deeper: what "inelastic demand" does to everything

Stoft calls the lack of real-time price response a demand-side flaw, and it explains most of power-market design. Stoft ch.1-1, ch.2-1 Synthesis

  • With a vertical demand curve, the market can't clear when capacity runs out, so the operator sheds load. Someone must therefore choose a price for shortage hours (VOLL or a cap, Module 5).
  • A supplier facing demand that doesn't respond has huge potential market power (Module 7).
  • Reserves must be held administratively (Module 6).
  • Even modest demand response flattens the top of the load-duration curve and cuts both the capacity needed and price spikes, which is why demand response is valued so highly in modern designs.

Key takeaways

  • Electricity must balance continuously over a shared network, and short-run demand is almost price-inelastic.
  • Keep MW (power, capacity) and MWh (energy) apart. Capacity factor links them.
  • The load-duration curve sizes the system and shows how rarely the top slices of capacity run.
  • Structures range from integrated monopoly through single buyer to full wholesale and retail competition with an independent system operator.
