Risk allocation

Learning objectives

  • Explain lenders' asymmetric risk position and focus on low-probability, high-impact risks.
  • Apply the commercial-viability test.
  • Size contingency and liquidated damages against construction risk.
  • Build a risk matrix.

Prerequisites

  • Modules 1–2.

How lenders look at risk

Investors have upside and downside. Lenders have only downside, because their return is fixed. Risk minimisation is therefore their organising principle, and in a successful project the equity return is typically at least twice the debt return. Yescombe ch.9 (p.199) Lenders judge a risk by its financial impact as much as by its likelihood. A low-probability, high-impact risk worries them even when sponsors consider it commercially unimportant. For the same reason, Yescombe argues that Monte Carlo averages are of limited use to lenders. Yescombe ch.9 (p.199) For a public offtaker, the question is whether a risk transfer is value for money. Nearly every risk has a transfer price, and pushing a risk onto a party that can't manage it buys an expensive safety margin. Yescombe ch.9 (p.199)

Gatti describes risk management in project finance as four linked steps: identify the risks, analyse their effect on cash flows, allocate each to the party best able to bear or control it, and manage what remains. Allocation is also an incentive tool, because a party that bears a risk works to prevent it. Gatti ch.1 (p.24)

First test: commercial viability

Before reading any contract, ask whether the deal makes long-term commercial sense for every party. A contract that is too good for one side will be attacked. Yescombe's failure cases include:

  • a contractor who bid too low and then fights every specification;
  • an offtake price that becomes uncompetitive, so the offtaker looks for a way out;
  • a plant the grid can't connect, leaving a white elephant;
  • a fixed-price input contract overtaken by market prices, leading to supplier default;
  • an unaffordable public contract that becomes a political problem. Yescombe ch.9 (pp.201–202)

The risk matrix

Due diligence runs through standard headings: commercial viability, construction, revenue, operating, input supply, uninsured, environmental, residual value, contract mismatch, sponsor support, offtaker risks, reasons for failure, and loss on default. The usual output is a risk matrix showing, for each risk, whether a contract covers it, what other mitigation exists (guarantees, insurance), and what impact any residual has on the project company and so on the lenders. Construction and completion

Completion is the riskiest phase. Lenders want a single-point, fixed-price, date-certain EPC contract with an experienced, creditworthy contractor, with LDs for delay and performance that mirror the penalties in the offtake contract. Without that, they expect contingent sponsor support. Clews ch.18 (p.340) Yescombe ch.6 (pp.113–114) LDs are capped, and any overrun beyond the caps and the contingency facilities falls first on equity and then on lenders. Yescombe ch.12 (p.328)

ALM analogy

A risk matrix is a project's risk register mapped to reinsurance. Each risk is ceded to a counterparty (contractor, offtaker, insurer) up to a limit (the LD cap, a policy limit), and the retention sits with equity and then debt. Lenders read it the way a regulator reads an insurer's reinsurance programme: what retained tail is left, and who pays when the reinsurer fails. Synthesis

Dated: construction markets

Since 2021, EPC contractors have pushed back on fixed-price, lump-sum risk after inflation and supply-chain shocks. Many energy projects now use split EPC, cost-plus or multi-contract structures, and lenders accept more sponsor support in exchange. Offshore wind and LNG cost overruns have been large. Outside the texts Check recent lender technical-adviser reports and rating-agency criteria.

Going deeper: contract mismatch

A project can have every risk "allocated" yet still fail, because the contracts don't line up. The EPC delay LDs may be smaller than the PPA's late-completion penalty. The fuel contract may not let you cut volumes when the offtaker stops dispatching. Force-majeure definitions may differ between contracts, leaving the project paying under one while excused under another. Yescombe flags contract mismatch as a heading in its own right. Work through each risk event and trace its effect through every contract at once. Yescombe ch.9 (p.200) Yescombe ch.8 (p.178) Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • Lenders have only downside, so they focus on low-probability, high-impact risks.
  • Test commercial viability before contract terms. Unfair contracts get broken.
  • Allocate each risk to the party best able to manage it, and record coverage and residual in a risk matrix.
  • Construction risk is the biggest. EPC LDs, contingency and sponsor support share it, and the tail remains.
