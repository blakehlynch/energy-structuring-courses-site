SPV structure and why project finance

Core≈ 50 min8 quiz questions5 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Define project finance and the role of the project company.
  • Show how leverage raises equity return and lowers the price an offtaker must pay.
  • Explain contamination risk and why large, correlated projects are financed off balance sheet.
  • List the costs and limits of project finance.

Prerequisites

  • Corporate finance basics; Course 4 Module 1 helpful.

What project finance is

In project finance, lenders look mainly to one project's cash flow for repayment, not to the sponsors' balance sheets. Yescombe summarises the logic as a chain. Yescombe ch.2 (pp.7–8)

  • Major, long-lived assets need long-term debt (typically 15–25 years).
  • Lenders rely on projected cash flow, so the project must be ring-fenced in a special-purpose project company.
  • Debt covers roughly 70–90% of cost.
  • Physical assets are worth far less than the debt in a default, so the real security is the project's contracts, licences and rights. Lenders therefore analyse in detail how risks are allocated through those contracts.
  • The project has a finite life, so the debt must be fully repaid within it.
  • Investors give no guarantees, so the debt is non-recourse, or limited-recourse if some support is given.

Corporate loans, by contrast, rest on the whole company's cash flow and assets and can be rolled over indefinitely. Yescombe ch.2 (p.8) Gatti makes the same distinction: approval depends on the project's ability to generate cash, not on the sponsors' creditworthiness or the collateral they can offer. Gatti ch.1 (p.2) The technique began in natural resources. Yescombe dates it to bank lending against Baku oil output in the 1880s and Texas oil-field loans in the 1930s, followed by the North Sea from the 1970s. Independent power took it up after PURPA in 1978, and infrastructure and PPPs followed. Yescombe ch.2 (pp.9–10)

Why sponsors use it

Project finance is more expensive than corporate finance. Yescombe notes that the cost of funds can be two to three times that of corporate finance, before adding due diligence and advisers. It also can't make an unfinanceable project financeable. Yescombe ch.2 (p.21) Sponsors still use it because of: Yescombe ch.2 (pp.21–25)

  • High leverage: cheaper debt replaces expensive equity, which lifts equity returns on long-life, modest-return assets.
  • Borrowing capacity and risk limitation: the loss is capped at the equity. Yescombe describes this as an option, where the equity buys the right to walk away.
  • Risk sharing: joint ventures and unequal partners can invest side by side.
  • Long tenor, credit enhancement and tax: interest deductibility reinforces the case for leverage.

Offtakers benefit too. Higher leverage lowers the price the project must charge for its product. Yescombe ch.2 (pp.25–26)

Worked example 1 · leverage and the equity returnChecked by code

A $1,000 project earns $110 a year before interest. With 30% debt at 6%, or 80% debt at 7.5% (a higher rate, because there is more debt):

30% debt: (110 − 18) / 700 = 13.14% return on equity; 80% debt: (110 − 60) / 200 = 25.00% after 25% tax: 9.86% versus 18.75%

In corporate-finance theory (Modigliani–Miller) higher leverage means higher equity risk, so the higher return isn't free. Yescombe's point is that in project finance high leverage is only allowed where contracts have already removed much of the risk. Yescombe ch.2 (p.22)

Worked example 2 · what leverage does for the offtakerChecked by code

Reverse the calculation. If investors need 14% on equity, the annual revenue the project must earn is:

30% debt: 18 + 0.14 × 700 = $116; 80% debt: 60 + 0.14 × 200 = $88

The tariff an offtaker pays falls by almost a quarter. That is why utilities and contracting authorities sign the kind of contracts lenders need. Yescombe ch.2 (pp.25–26)

Why keep it off the balance sheet: contamination risk

Gatti gives a portfolio-theory reason. Suppose a project is (1) large relative to the sponsor, (2) riskier than the sponsor's existing assets, and (3) closely linked to its core business. Financed on balance sheet, it raises the risk of the whole company. Because it is correlated with existing business, there is little diversification, and because it is large, its failure could sink the rest. Gatti calls this contamination risk. Ring-fencing in a project company stops it. Gatti ch.1 (pp.25–28)

Worked example 3 · a big correlated project on the balance sheetChecked by code

A company's existing business has 6% return volatility. It adds a project three times as large (75% of combined assets) with 18% volatility.

correlation 0.9: combined volatility = 14.9%; correlation −0.5: 12.8%

Even with negative correlation, the combined company is about twice as risky as before, because size dominates. Lenders and shareholders reprice the whole company, which is the cost project finance avoids. Gatti ch.1 (pp.27–28)

ALM analogy

A project company is a ring-fenced fund, like a with-profits sub-fund or a securitisation vehicle. Liabilities are matched to the assets inside it, and creditors have no claim outside. The sponsor's equity works like a first-loss tranche with an embedded option to walk away. Contamination risk is the concentration risk an insurer's ALM function watches when one large, correlated block threatens the whole balance sheet. Synthesis

Dated: market context

Yescombe's market figures are pre-2014, and Gatti's 2023 edition covers the post-crisis shift. Since then private credit, infrastructure debt funds and project bonds have taken a large share of lending. Margins and base rates rose sharply after 2022, and LIBOR has been replaced by SOFR, SONIA and €STR. Renewables now dominate energy project-finance volumes. Outside the texts Check IJGlobal or Refinitiv league tables and recent deal terms.

Going deeper: equity as an option to walk away

Because lenders have no recourse, sponsors' equity is a call option on project value, struck at the debt. If things go well, equity keeps the upside. If they go badly, sponsors walk away and lenders take the asset. This is why lenders insist on enough equity at risk, especially during construction, why they control cash through the waterfall (Module 6), and why sponsors' incentives can diverge from lenders' as the project nears distress. Gatti's discussion of conflicts of interest and wealth expropriation between sponsors and lenders addresses exactly this. Gatti ch.1 (p.29) Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • Project finance is non-recourse lending to a ring-fenced company. The security is contracts and cash flow.
  • Leverage raises equity returns and lowers offtake prices, but is only allowed where contracts remove risk.
  • It is expensive and slow. Use it for large, long-lived, contractable projects.
  • Contamination risk explains why large, correlated projects are kept off the sponsor's balance sheet.
