Offtake, PPAs, tolling and take-or-pay

Offtake contracts by the risk they remove

Lenders can only provide high leverage if the project company's sales risk is limited, and the offtake contract is the main tool for that. Yescombe distinguishes the forms below. Yescombe ch.6 (pp.106–108)

  • Take-or-pay: the offtaker takes the product or pays anyway, at an agreed tariff. It is rarely "hell or high water": the project is paid only if it can deliver. This is the standard form for power purchase agreements.
  • Take-and-pay: the offtaker pays only for what it takes. This gives lenders little certainty.
  • Long-term sales contract: volume is committed but the price follows a market index. This is common in mining, oil and gas and petrochemicals. A floor price makes it act like take-or-pay at the floor.
  • Hedging contract: a forward sale, a floor, or a floor-and-ceiling collar with a trader. For example, oil sold at no less than one price and no more than another.
  • Contract for differences: the project sells into the market, and the counterparty pays or receives the difference from an agreed price. This is purely financial, and widely used where all power must go through a pool.
  • Throughput contract: for pipelines. A shipper commits to minimum volumes and a minimum price.

Yescombe warns that market usage of "take-or-pay" and "take-and-pay" is confused, so read the clause, not the label. Yescombe ch.6 (p.108)

Inside a PPA

A PPA has the project build a plant with agreed capacity, heat rate and emissions, reach commercial operation by an agreed date after performance tests, and then make it available. The offtaker takes the dispatch risk: it decides when the plant runs, and pays whether or not it does. Yescombe ch.6 (pp.109–110) The tariff has two parts. Yescombe ch.6 (pp.111–112)

  • Capacity (availability) charge: paid whether or not the plant is dispatched. It covers fixed operating costs, debt service and the equity return, on pre-agreed assumptions.
  • Energy charge: covers variable costs, mainly fuel, using pre-agreed heat-rate assumptions and actual or indexed fuel costs.

The project company keeps the risks of cost overruns, low availability and poor operating performance, because the capacity charge is fixed at signing. Yescombe ch.6 (pp.112–113) Penalties are liquidated damages for late completion, low output, a high initial heat rate and low availability, and they should be mirrored in the EPC and O&M contracts. Yescombe ch.6 (pp.113–114)

Worked example 1 · building a capacity chargeChecked by code

An 800 MW CCGT costs $800m, financed 75% with 18-year debt at 6.5%. Equity requires 12% over a 25-year life, and fixed O&M is $16m a year.

debt service (annuity) $57.51m + equity return $25.50m + fixed O&M $16m = $99.01m a year → $10.31/kW-month if availability is 86% against a 90% requirement, a pro-rata deduction leaves $94.61m

Course 7's toll had the same structure from the buyer's side. Here the capacity charge is a cost-based annuity, not an option value. Splitting it into debt ($57.51m for years 1–18), equity ($25.50m for years 1–25) and O&M ($16m) components lets the tariff step down once the 18-year debt is retired (charging the full $99.01m flat through years 19–25 would lift the equity IRR from 12.0% to 14.33%) and lets each component be indexed differently. Yescombe ch.6 (p.111) Synthesis

US Renewable Tax Equity (Partnership Flips & IRA §6418 Transferability)

In US utility-scale renewables, project-level debt is awkward alongside tax equity because a foreclosure could trigger tax-credit recapture. Projects therefore use a Partnership Flip: a sponsor with little taxable income partners with a tax equity investor (typically a large bank). The tax equity investor contributes roughly 35–50% of capex and receives 99% of the tax credits (30% Investment Tax Credit [ITC] or Production Tax Credit [PTC]) and 99% of the 5-year MACRS depreciation tax shield, plus a minority of cash, until it reaches its target flip yield (typically 6–9% after-tax IRR). After the flip (often around year 6, after the 5-year ITC recapture period), its interest drops to 5% and the sponsor takes 95% of residual cash. Sponsor debt is structured as back-levered debt at the sponsor holding-company level, sized strictly against post-flip and Class B cash distributions without recourse to the project assets. Outside the texts

Dated: US clean-energy tax credits

The Inflation Reduction Act (2022) created the technology-neutral credits (§45Y PTC, §48E ITC) and credit transferability (§6418). The 2025 budget law (the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, July 2025) ends the wind and solar credits unless a project begins construction by 4 July 2026 or is placed in service by the end of 2027, adds foreign-entity-of-concern restrictions, and restored 100% bonus depreciation. Tax equity sizing, credit eligibility and the ITC/PTC choice now depend on these dates. Outside the texts Check current IRS guidance before using any of these figures.

Worked example 1b · sizing a partnership flip & sponsor back-leverageChecked by code

A 100 MW solar project has $120m capex eligible for a 30% ITC ($36m credit). Tax basis after the 50% ITC basis reduction is $102m. Tax equity takes 99% of the ITC and MACRS tax benefits plus 20% of operating cash ($8m a year in total) and must reach an 8.0% after-tax IRR at a year-6 flip (21% tax rate). How much can it pay in?

ITC tax credit (year 1) = 30% × $120m = $36.0m tax basis for MACRS (120 − 0.5×36) = $102.0m year 1 TE cash flow (99% ITC + MACRS shield + 20% cash) = $41.48m total 6-year TE benefits and cash received = $66.45m TE contribution = PV of those flows at 8.0% = $57.60m (48.0% of capex) check: TE IRR on that contribution = 8.00% sponsor operating cash before the flip (80%) = $6.40m/yr

The front-loaded ITC and depreciation let tax equity fund almost half the capex while the sponsor keeps 80% of operating cash. A higher target yield or a later flip shrinks the contribution, and the sponsor fills the gap with equity or back-leverage. The model is simplified: it assumes the investor can use every tax benefit in the year it arises and ignores post-flip flows. Since §6418 transferability, sponsors can also sell credits for cash (typically 90–95¢ on the dollar); a sale cannot pass on depreciation, so flips and hybrid "T-flip" structures remain common where the depreciation is worth monetising. Input supply and tolling

The fuel contract is the mirror image of an offtake contract. For the supplier it is the offtake. Forms include put-or-pay, take-and-pay, fixed or variable supply, output or reserve dedication, and interruptible supply at a discount. Yescombe ch.8 (pp.178–180) In a tolling arrangement, inputs are supplied free and the project is paid a capacity charge to process them. In pull tolling the offtaker supplies the fuel. In push tolling the input supplier (an oil company with a refinery, say) pays the toll and takes the product price risk. Yescombe ch.8 (p.179) Prices should be linked so that the project isn't caught between a fixed-price input and a floating-price output, or the reverse. Yescombe ch.8 (pp.180–181)

When there's no offtaker

Lenders will take price risk where the product trades in deep markets, as in natural resources and merchant power. They underwrite using the low end of historic prices, expect producers to be in the lowest-cost quartile, and require much higher cover. Yescombe ch.9 (pp.223–224) For merchant power they focus on the plant's cost competitiveness and break-even price, and usually want mitigants: hedges or CfDs for part of the output, an anchor PPA, fuel priced off power, subordinated fuel payments, a toll, or a portfolio of plants. A full CfD or toll gives about the same debt capacity as a PPA. Yescombe ch.9 (pp.224–225)

Worked example 2 · the same plant under three revenue structuresChecked by code

4.2 TWh a year, $120m operating costs and $70m debt service. The market price averages $55/MWh with 30% volatility. We compare a $52 PPA, a $47–$70 collar and full merchant exposure over 5,000 simulated years.

PPA: DSCR fixed at 1.41 collar: mean 1.62, 5th percentile 1.11 merchant: mean 1.59, 5th percentile 0.22, and DSCR below 1 in 30.3% of years

The merchant plant earns more on average but fails to cover debt service in almost a third of years, so lenders would size much less debt against it. The collar keeps most of the upside while protecting the downside, which is why partial hedging is the usual compromise. Yescombe ch.9 (pp.224–225) Synthesis

ALM analogy

The offtake contract plays the part of the liability side in a matched book. Take-or-pay is a fixed annuity receivable, a long-term sales contract is a floating-rate receivable, and a collar is a capped-and-floored note. Lenders size debt against the guaranteed part of the receivables, much as a regulator credits only the guaranteed element of a with-profits policy. Synthesis

Dated: today's offtake market

Yescombe's PPA model is the utility PPA of IPP programmes. Today corporate PPAs (physical and virtual) from technology and industrial buyers, government CfD auctions (UK, EU), merchant-tail renewables and hybrid storage tolls dominate new energy offtake. Shape, basis and volume risks have moved from offtaker to project. Outside the texts Check LevelTen or Pexapark PPA indices and national auction results.

Going deeper: who holds dispatch risk

In a capacity-plus-energy PPA, the offtaker holds dispatch risk and in return controls when the plant runs. Under a CfD, the project must sell into the market and keeps dispatch and bidding risk. That is why a CfD counterparty may want to control the bidding strategy, so it doesn't end up paying for an under-priced bid. Yescombe ch.6 (p.110), ch.9 (p.223) When you structure an offtake, map each risk (price, volume, dispatch, shape, basis, availability) to a party, and check that the debt is sized only on the parts the project doesn't hold. Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • Offtake contracts range from take-or-pay through sales contracts, hedges and CfDs to merchant sales. Leverage follows the risk removed.
  • A PPA has a capacity charge (fixed costs, debt, equity) and an energy charge (fuel pass-through). The project keeps cost and availability risk.
  • Tolling separates processing from commodity risk, with pull or push structures.
  • Merchant exposure means a much weaker downside and less debt. Collars and partial hedges are the usual middle ground.
