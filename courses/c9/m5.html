Upstream: lending against reserves

Upstream lenders face geology they can't fully see, volatile prices, development risk and often difficult jurisdictions. Clews describes the traditional stance: lend only against proved, developed, producing reserves. Lenders have relaxed this with portfolio borrowing bases and with sponsor completion support for undeveloped reserves. Clews ch.18 (pp.338–339) Prices come from the banks' own conservative price decks: discounted forward prices in the early years, then a flat real medium-term price, inflated. Differentials for quality and location must be verified independently. Clews ch.18 (p.339) Lenders also test the break-even price, the lowest price in a period at which cash flow still services the debt. Clews ch.18 (p.340) Base cases use proved reserves for revenue, which may mismatch with costs based on proved-plus-probable development plans. Ongoing capex, decommissioning provisions and complex fiscal terms (production sharing, royalties, special taxes) all have to be modelled. Clews ch.18 (pp.341–342)

Debt capacity rises with the price deck and falls with volatility. Hedging can raise it, but a production outage then leaves the project exposed on the hedge. Clews ch.18 (p.342) Yescombe adds the reserve tail: proven reserves should remain after the loan is repaid. His rule of thumb is a reserve cover ratio of 2.0 at the bank case price and at least 1.0 in a downside. Yescombe ch.12 (p.325) Course 4 introduced the borrowing-base mechanics. Here we size one.

Worked example 1 · sizing a borrowing baseChecked by code

Proved production declines from 12 to 2.5 mmbbl a year over 12 years. The price deck is $70, $68, then $65 flat. Opex is $18/bbl, government take 40% of net cash, discount rate 8% and cover ratio 1.5.

NPV of proved cash flow (field life) = $1,713m → field-life (PLCR) base = 1,713 / 1.5 = $1,142m the last 25% of reserves is produced after year 7; NPV to tail = $1,468m → loan-life (LLCR) base at 1.3× = $1,129m binding borrowing base = min(1,142, 1,129) = $1,129m

In practice banks test both a field-life cover (PLCR, here 1.5×) and a loan-life cover up to the reserve tail (LLCR, usually a lower threshold, here 1.3×), and the smaller base binds. Lending the full $1,142m field-life base would leave an LLCR of only 1.29×, just under the 1.3× test. Banks also weight reserve categories before dividing by the cover, typically counting PDP in full and PDNP and PUD only partly (Course 4 Module 3 uses 95%, 50% and 30%; banks vary and often cap PUD's share of the base). The base is redetermined twice a year as prices, reserves and production change. Clews ch.17 (p.321) Outside the texts

Worked example 2 · debt break-even priceChecked by code

Same field, with debt service of $180m in years 2–3 stepping down to $60m in year 8, opex $18 and 40% take.

break-even = max over years of [opex + debt service / (production × (1 − take))] = $52.31/bbl

The binding year is late, when production has declined faster than debt service. Lenders compare the break-even with historical prices and volatility. Here it sits uncomfortably close to a $65 deck. Clews ch.18 (p.340) Synthesis

Midstream: selling capacity

Pipelines, storage, ships and FPSOs earn revenue by selling capacity forward under ship-or-pay, storage or charter contracts. With strong contracts and creditworthy users, Clews reports gearing of 80% or more and DSCRs as low as about 1.2. That compares with 1.5–2.0 upstream and downstream, and some of these assets reach the bond market. Clews ch.18 (pp.350–352) Risks shift to counterparty credit, regulation (anti-competition rules, third-party access, regulated tariffs), residual value beyond the contracts, and "project-on-project" completion risk, where a pipeline depends on an upstream field and downstream infrastructure built by others. Clews ch.18 (pp.350–354) Regulated tariffs are often set on a cost-of-service basis. Clews ch.18 (p.351)

Worked example 3 · a cost-of-service pipeline tariffChecked by code

Rate base $900m, allowed return 9%, depreciation $36m, opex $28m, a 25% tax rate grossed up on the equity half of the return (40.5 × 0.25/0.75 = $13.5m), and 146m bbl a year of throughput (400 kb/d).

revenue requirement = 28 + 36 + 81 + 13.5 = $158.5m → tariff = $1.086/bbl

A ship-or-pay contract at this tariff for most of the capacity is what makes the 80% gearing possible. Throughput below commitment is still paid for, while throughput above it is upside. The method is illustrative, since regulators' formulas differ. Clews ch.18 (pp.351–352) Outside the texts

LNG Project Finance & SPA Structuring

Liquefied Natural Gas (LNG) liquefaction trains represent the largest single-asset project financings globally ($10bn–$30bn). Debt is underwritten against long-term (15–20 year) Sale & Purchase Agreements (SPAs). Commercial structuring has evolved into two competing paradigms: Outside the texts

  1. US Gulf Coast Henry Hub-linked FOB SPA: Buyers pay a fixed liquefaction fee (roughly $2.25–$2.75/MMBtu in many contracts) plus 115% of NYMEX Henry Hub for feed gas and fuel (\(P_{\text{FOB}} = 1.15 \times P_{\text{HH}} + L_{\text{fee}}\)), and arrange their own shipping. Feed gas is passed through, so the project carries little commodity price risk, and debt is sized mainly on the fixed fees, which buyers owe even if they cancel cargoes.
  2. Traditional Asian Oil-Linked SPA: LNG is sold Delivered Ex-Ship (DES) on an oil index slope (\(P_{\text{LNG}} = \alpha \times P_{\text{Brent}} + \beta\), where \(\alpha\) is typically 12.0–14.5%), often with an "S-curve" flattening the slope at high and low oil prices to protect buyer and seller. Destination-restriction clauses (banning resale) were historically standard but have been challenged by competition authorities (the European Commission, and Japan's Fair Trade Commission in 2017), and newer contracts often share the profit on diverted cargoes instead.
Worked example 4 · LNG SPA pricing: US Gulf tolling vs Asian Brent parityChecked by code

Compare delivered LNG prices in Asia: US Henry Hub is $3.00/MMBtu with a $2.50 liquefaction fee and $2.00 shipping to Tokyo. Brent is $80.00/bbl, and a long-term Asian SPA uses a 13.5% slope plus $0.50 constant:

US FOB export price = 1.15 × 3.00 + 2.50 = $5.95/MMBtu → delivered Asia (+$2.00 freight) = $7.95/MMBtu oil-linked contract price = 0.135 × 80.00 + 0.50 = $11.30/MMBtu arbitrage spread (oil-linked parity premium) = 11.30 − 7.95 = $3.35/MMBtu S-curve, Brent $110 with a $90 ceiling kink (half slope above it): 0.135 × (90 + 0.5 × 20) + 0.50 = $14.00/MMBtu vs $15.35 on the straight line

When Brent is $80, US tolling delivers gas to Asian buyers $3.35/MMBtu cheaper than traditional oil-indexed contracts. That spread drove the expansion of US Gulf project-financed liquefaction terminals (Sabine Pass, Corpus Christi, Plaquemines). The S-curve line shows how the buyer's protection works at high oil prices: above the kink, each extra dollar of Brent adds only half as much to the LNG price. Synthesis

Downstream: margin risk

Refineries earn thin margins (a few dollars a barrel) on huge volumes, while petrochemicals earn higher margins on smaller volumes. Both are cyclical. Lenders rely on independent market consultants for margin forecasts, sensitivity and break-even analysis. They want secure feedstock with backup supply, proven technology wrapped by the contractor, and a cost position in the top quartile. Clews ch.18 (pp.354–355) Course 2 covered the margin economics. The financing answer is usually lower gearing, sponsor support, and offtake or tolling arrangements with sponsors (Module 2's push toll). Synthesis

ALM analogy

A borrowing base is a collateralised loan with a haircut and periodic remargining. The "collateral value" is the PV of proved reserves at a stressed price, the cover ratio is the haircut, and redeterminations are margin calls. The pro-cyclicality is the same as in repo: when prices fall, the base and the borrower's liquidity shrink together. Synthesis

Dated: today's petroleum finance

Clews (2016) predates the energy transition's effect on bank appetite. Many banks have restricted upstream and coal lending under net-zero commitments, and ESG covenants, decommissioning security and transition-risk scenarios now feature in term sheets. Private credit and commodity traders' prepayments (Course 4) fill some of the gap. Outside the texts Check current bank energy-policy statements and Equator Principles requirements.

Going deeper: redeterminations as a written option

A borrowing base gives lenders the right to resize the facility at each redetermination. In effect, the borrower has written lenders a series of options on the price deck. When prices fall, available debt falls exactly when the borrower most needs liquidity. A sponsor can partly buy this back with hedges that raise the deck price used for the hedged volumes, and lenders often credit hedged volumes at the hedge price. As Clews notes, though, hedges create their own exposure if production falls short. Clews ch.18 (p.342) Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • Upstream debt is sized on proved reserves at a conservative price deck, with a cover ratio and a reserve tail.
  • Break-even price analysis shows how much price headroom the debt has.
  • Midstream capacity contracts support high gearing and low DSCRs. The risks are credit, regulation and project-on-project dependence.
  • Downstream margin volatility means lower gearing, top-quartile cost positions and sponsor-linked offtake.
