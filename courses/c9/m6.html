Lenders and documentation

Core≈ 55 min8 quiz questions4 flashcards

Learning objectives

  • Lay out a cash-flow cascade and reserve accounts.
  • Model DSRA draws, lock-ups and sweeps under stress.
  • Explain distribution-stop ratios and events of default.
  • Understand loan pricing, margin ratchets and fees.

Prerequisites

  • Modules 1–5.

Controlling the cash

Once a project operates, lenders control its cash through accounts held under their security. Revenues may go into a single account, or be split into an operating account the company controls and a revenue account under joint control. Yescombe ch.14 (p.372) Reserve accounts sit in the company's name, but withdrawals need the agent's consent. Yescombe ch.14 (pp.372–374)

  • Debt service reserve account (DSRA): usually six months of debt service, drawn if operating cash can't pay. It can be funded in the construction budget (better for investors, because lenders fund their share) or from early operating cash.
  • Maintenance reserve account: smooths major overhaul cycles.
  • Tax, change-in-law and other smoothing reserves, plus an insurance proceeds account.

Sponsors can replace cash reserves with letters of credit to release cash and lift their IRR. Yescombe ch.14 (p.374) In ratio tests, DSRA movements are ignored in the ADSCR, and the DSRA balance is deducted from debt in the LLCR. Yescombe ch.14 (p.374)

The cash-flow cascade

A typical order of priority is: Yescombe ch.14 (p.375)

  1. operating costs, fuel and taxes;
  2. agent and trustee fees;
  3. interest and hedge payments;
  4. scheduled principal;
  5. top-ups of the DSRA and other reserves;
  6. distributions to investors.

Distributions face further hurdles. A distribution-stop (lock-up) ratio blocks payouts if, say, the trailing ADSCR is below 1.20 against a 1.35 base case. Trapped cash then prepays debt or sits in a reserve until the ratios recover. Yescombe ch.14 (p.376) Clews adds cash sweeps (a share of excess cash used to prepay debt) and limited principal deferrals as further variations. Clews ch.17 (p.320)

Base-case CFADS runs from $150m to $176m over 10 years. Debt is sized as an annuity at 1.35 minimum DSCR and 6.5%: $799m, with debt service of $111.11m a year. The DSRA holds six months, the lock-up is below 1.20, and 50% of locked-up cash is swept.

no stress: total distributions $570m 20% CFADS fall in years 4–5: DSCR ≈ 1.15 → lock-up both years, $16.5m swept, distributions $490m 45% fall: DSCR ≈ 0.8 → DSRA draws of $24.2m and $22.6m keep the debt current; distributions $427m 60% fall: the DSRA runs out and the loan defaults in year 5

The waterfall turns a cash shock into graduated consequences: first equity loses distributions, then reserves absorb shortfalls, and only then is there a payment default. That ordering is why lenders accept high leverage. Yescombe ch.14 (pp.375–376) Synthesis

Pricing and fees

Bank loans pay a base rate plus a margin. Yescombe gives international ranges of about 2–3.5% (after margins roughly doubled or tripled following 2008), higher before completion, then stepping up over time (a margin ratchet). Fees include arranging, underwriting, commitment (on undrawn amounts), agency and lenders' adviser fees. Yescombe ch.12 (pp.337–338) Clews reports oil and gas margins between about 50 and 400 bp depending on market, sponsor and project, and notes that ratchets encourage refinancing once the project has a track record. Clews ch.17 (pp.323–324) Prepayments may carry breakage costs, mandatory prepayment applies on illegality, insurance proceeds or ratio failures, and replacement debt must respect the existing intercreditor terms. Clews ch.17 (pp.321–322)

ALM analogy

The cascade is a priority-of-payments waterfall like a CLO's, and the lock-up and sweep work like over-collateralisation tests that divert cash from equity to senior notes. The DSRA is a liquidity facility. For an actuary, think of a closed fund's surplus distribution rules: surplus is released only after the solvency buffer (the cover ratio) is met. Synthesis

Dated: documentation and rates

Both books quote LIBOR-based pricing. Since 2023, loans reference SOFR, SONIA or €STR, with credit-adjustment spreads on legacy deals. LMA and LSTA templates, green-loan principles and sustainability-linked margin ratchets are now standard features of energy project loans. Outside the texts Check current LMA documentation and recent deal announcements for market terms.

Going deeper: cash trapped versus cash swept

Trapping cash during a lock-up protects lenders without shortening the loan. The cash is released once the ratios recover. A sweep permanently prepays debt, which cuts lenders' risk and also lowers future interest and scheduled debt service, making a cure easier, while equity loses the swept cash during the lock-up. In Worked Example 1's simplified fixed-schedule waterfall (where swept cash is deducted without re-amortising the remaining annuity), a 20% shock in years 4–5 sweeps $16.5m and leaves $490m of distributions. When swept cash instead prepays loan principal and re-sculpts the remaining 6.5% annuity, the prepayment lowers subsequent annual debt service from $111.11m to $107.12m, lifting total 10-year equity distributions to $510.5m. Many loan agreements instead apply prepayments in inverse order of maturity, which shortens the loan but leaves near-term debt service unchanged. Lenders prefer sweeps in volatile sectors, and sponsors negotiate for trapping. Clews notes that a sweep can significantly reduce sponsor returns. Clews ch.17 (p.320) Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • Lenders control cash through accounts and reserves. The DSRA covers about six months of debt service.
  • The cascade puts opex, fees, interest, principal and reserves ahead of distributions.
  • Lock-ups and sweeps divert cash from equity when cover weakens, and the DSRA absorbs short shocks.
  • Pricing is base rate plus a ratcheting margin plus fees. Prepayment, replacement debt and refinancing terms matter for sponsors.
