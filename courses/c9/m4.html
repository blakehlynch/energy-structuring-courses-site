Cover ratios

Debt capacity in project finance is set by cash flow, not by asset value. Lenders measure the margin of safety with cover ratios, all calculated on cash, not accounting profit. Yescombe ch.12 (pp.322–323)

  • ADSCR = cash flow available for debt service (CFADS) ÷ debt service for the period. It is usually tested on a rolling 12-month basis every six months.
  • LLCR = present value of CFADS over the remaining loan life, at the debt rate ÷ debt outstanding (less the DSRA balance).
  • PLCR = the same over the whole project life. Because it includes the tail after the loan, it is higher than the LLCR. In resource projects the equivalent is the reserve cover ratio.

Yescombe's rough minimum ADSCRs are 1.20 for accommodation PPPs, 1.25 for process plants with offtake contracts, 1.50 for natural resources without offtake, and 2.00 for merchant power. Minimum LLCRs run about 10% higher, PLCRs 15–20% higher, and the reserve cover rule of thumb is 2.0. Yescombe ch.12 (pp.323–325) The corresponding gearing is roughly 90:10, 85:15, 70:30 and 50:50. Yescombe ch.12 (p.327) Corporate measures such as interest cover or current ratios matter little, because the debt must be repaid rather than rolled over. Yescombe ch.12 (p.326)

Worked example 1 · cover ratios set the debtChecked by code

CFADS of $500m a year for 20 years, an annuity loan at 7%:

ADSCR 1.50 → maximum debt service 333.3 → debt $3,531m; ADSCR 1.30 → debt $4,075m a one-period check: CFADS 120, interest 50, principal 35 → ADSCR = 1.41

Lenders work backward from the cover ratio to the debt amount, and the gearing falls out. A riskier offtake means a higher required cover, less debt and more equity. Yescombe ch.12 (pp.326–327)

Interest During Construction (IDC) & financing fee circularity

In project finance models, total project cost (TPC) is not known in advance: it equals base EPC capex plus Interest During Construction (IDC) and upfront bank financing fees. But IDC and fees depend on the size of the debt facility, which in turn depends on TPC via the target gearing ratio \(g = D / \text{TPC}\). Structurers solve this circularity algebraically: Yescombe ch.10 (p.266) Yescombe ch.13 (p.353) Outside the texts

\[ \text{TPC} = C_{\text{base}} + D\cdot (r_d \cdot t_{\text{avg}} + f_{\text{fee}}) \implies D = \frac{g \cdot C_{\text{base}}}{1 - g\cdot (r_d \cdot t_{\text{avg}} + f_{\text{fee}})}. \]
Worked example 1b · solving IDC & fee circularityChecked by code

A power project has $500m base EPC capex. Target gearing is 70% of TPC. Construction runs 3 years with an S-curve spending profile (average debt draw duration \(t_{\text{avg}} = 1.5\) years). Construction debt rate is 6.0% p.a., and the upfront bank arrangement fee is 1.5% of the facility:

IDC + fee factor = 6.0% × 1.5 yrs + 1.5% fee = 0.105 (10.5%) debt sized with IDC circularity = (0.70 × 500) / (1 − 0.70 × 0.105) = $377.77m IDC during construction = $377.77m × 9.0% = $34.00m upfront financing fee (1.5%) = $377.77m × 1.5% = $5.67m total project cost (TPC) = 500 + 34.00 + 5.67 = $539.67m equity contribution required = 539.67 − 377.77 = $161.90m (gearing check: 70.0%)

Ignoring IDC circularity would undersize debt by nearly $28m (0.70 × $500m = $350m vs $377.8m), forcing sponsors to inject unexpected late equity. The closed form treats IDC as simple interest on the average drawn balance; full models compound it monthly and iterate, but land close to this. Synthesis

The repayment profile

Tenor depends on the certainty of long-term cash flow and the need for a debt tail, a margin of contracted cash flow after the last repayment. A 20-year contract might support 17–19 years of debt. Yescombe ch.12 (p.330) The shape of repayment matters as much as the tenor. Yescombe ch.12 (pp.333–336) Clews ch.17 (pp.319–321)

  • Level principal: debt service is front-loaded, giving a low ADSCR in the early years (when start-up problems hit) and back-loaded dividends. Rare in project finance.
  • Annuity: level debt service. The standard profile when cash flow is flat.
  • Sculpted: debt service set each period as CFADS ÷ target DSCR, so the ratio is constant even when cash flow is irregular (maintenance years, tax changes, depleting production).
  • Target/minimum schedules, deferrals, cash sweeps and balloons add flexibility or speed repayment. A 100% sweep sends all excess cash to prepayment.

Yescombe shows that the profile matters more to investors than the interest rate. Moving from level principal to annuity raised equity IRR in his example far more than a 0.25% margin cut. Yescombe ch.12 (p.336) Lenders cap the weighted average life, so sculpting and longer tenors trade higher debt against a longer average life. Yescombe ch.12 (pp.331–332)

Worked example 2 · three profiles on one cash flowChecked by code

CFADS starts at $100m and grows 2% a year for 25 years, with a 15% dip in years 11–12 for a major overhaul. Capex is $1,050m, with 18-year debt at 6.5% and a minimum DSCR of 1.35.

level principal: debt $614m, average life 9.5 yrs, equity IRR 12.3% annuity: debt $773m, LLCR 1.53, average life 11.2 yrs, equity IRR 14.7% sculpted: debt $876m (83% gearing), LLCR 1.35, PLCR 1.65, average life 12.0 yrs, equity IRR 18.3%

With the annuity, the overhaul years bind, so debt is sized off the dip and every other year carries excess cover. Sculpting puts the DSCR at 1.35 in every year, which raises debt by $100m and equity IRR by over 3 points, at the cost of a longer average life and an LLCR equal to the minimum DSCR. The alternative is to smooth the overhaul with a maintenance reserve account. Yescombe ch.12 (p.336) Clews ch.17 (p.319) Synthesis

Oil and gas variations

Upstream cash flows deplete and refinery margins swing, so pure annuities rarely fit. Clews notes that deferral rights (often 10–15% of an instalment, not in the final two periods), cash sweeps, and cover-ratio-driven repayment are common. The last of these, based on the LLCR or PLCR, is the basis of the upstream borrowing base (Module 5). Clews ch.17 (pp.320–321) A balloon leaves part of the debt to be refinanced at maturity. That is common in corporate finance but carries refinancing risk in a finite-life project. Clews ch.17 (p.320)

ALM analogy

Sculpting is cash-flow matching: the liability schedule (debt service) is shaped to the asset cash flows (CFADS) with a constant coverage margin, as you would match a bond portfolio to a liability run-off with a target surplus ratio. The LLCR is a funding ratio, PV of assets ÷ liabilities, and the debt tail is the run-off margin beyond the last liability cash flow. Synthesis

Dated: ratio norms

Yescombe's ratio and gearing guides date from 2014. Current norms vary by sector and lender type, often lower for contracted renewables with strong offtakers (DSCR 1.15–1.25 on P50 or 1.0 on P99 energy yield) and higher for merchant tails. Rating-agency criteria (S&P, Moody's, Fitch) now anchor many bond deals. Outside the texts

Going deeper: why sculpting makes LLCR equal DSCR

If debt service in each period is CFADS ÷ k, the debt equals the PV of those payments at the loan rate: D = PV(CFADS)/k. So LLCR = PV(CFADS)/D = k exactly. Under sculpting the loan-life ratio adds no information beyond the period test, and lenders lean on the PLCR (tail) and the average-life limit instead. Under an annuity sized to the worst year, LLCR > minimum DSCR, and the gap measures the "wasted" cover in good years that sculpting converts into debt. Synthesis

Key takeaways

  • ADSCR, LLCR and PLCR measure safety. Required minima rise with revenue risk.
  • Debt is sized backward from CFADS and the minimum DSCR, and gearing follows.
  • Annuity beats level principal for equity. Sculpting fits irregular cash flows and maximises debt.
  • Average-life limits, tails, sweeps and balloons bound the trade-off between lenders and sponsors.
